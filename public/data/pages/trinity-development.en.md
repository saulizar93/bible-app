## The question

Most churches today teach that God is **one being in three co-equal, co-eternal persons**: the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit. They teach that this is what the apostles believed and what Christians have always believed, and the Athanasian Creed says that whoever does not hold it "without doubt he shall perish everlastingly."

If the apostles taught that doctrine, the men who learned from them and from their disciples should have taught it too. Instead, when we read the Christian writers of the first three centuries, we find something else: a Son who is "**another God**" in "the **second place**," a Son who was not always Son, a Father who is "**greater**," a Spirit whose nature was still undecided in A.D. 380, and arguments that changed from one generation to the next. The doctrine as it is defined today was built step by step, by philosophers and councils, over several hundred years, and the Eastern and Western churches still disagree about part of it.

This page shows that development from the sources. In short:

- **Greek philosophy** had already moved from the immoral gods of Homer to a distant, **nameless** supreme God, with a "**second god**" below him. Hellenized Jews in Alexandria absorbed this, and so did the Christian **apologists**, who presented Christianity as the true philosophy.
- **Every major Christian writer before the fourth century** differs from the modern doctrine on one point or more. Justin calls the Son "another God" in the second place; Tertullian says there was a time when the Son was not; Irenaeus says the Father is above the Son; Origen says the Son is a "second God" and that prayer should be offered to the Father alone.
- The doctrine was defined by **councils called by emperors**: Nicaea (325) for the Son, Constantinople (381) for the Spirit, and in the West, from the Council of Toledo (589), the **filioque** ("and the Son"), which Rome itself did not add to the Creed until 1014 and which the Orthodox still reject.
- Meanwhile the Bible keeps revealing **the Name**: the Son "inherited" and was "given" the Father's name, came in the Father's name and manifested it, and the apostles did everything — preaching, healing, baptizing — "in the name of Jesus." Jesus means "**YHWH is salvation**." The Trinity ended with a Father who has no name; the apostles preached the Name.

Bible quotations are from the King James Version. The Fathers are quoted from the _Ante-Nicene Fathers_ and _Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers_ translations.

### This is not only a Oneness claim

Historians of every tradition recognize that the doctrine developed:

- The _Oxford Dictionary of the Christian Church_ calls subordinationism (making the Son lower than the Father) "a characteristic tendency in much Christian teaching of the first three centuries," and a "marked feature" of Justin Martyr and Irenaeus.
- Manlio Simonetti, in the _Encyclopedia of the Early Church_ (1992), says it "is evident in theologians like Justin Martyr, Tertullian, Origen, Novatian, and Irenaeus."
- The _New Catholic Encyclopedia_ (1967, vol. 14, p. 299) admits that "the formulation 'one God in three Persons' was not solidly established, certainly not fully assimilated into Christian life and its profession of faith, prior to the end of the 4th century."
- John Henry Newman wrote his famous _Essay on the Development of Christian Doctrine_ (1845) precisely to explain why the doctrines of his church cannot be found fully formed in the early centuries.
- And Gregory of Nazianzus, one of the architects of the doctrine, said it himself in 380: "The Old Testament proclaimed the Father openly, and the Son more obscurely. The New manifested the Son, and suggested the Deity of the Spirit. **Now the Spirit Himself dwells among us**, and supplies us with a clearer demonstration of Himself" (_Oration_ 31.26). In other words, the full doctrine was not in the New Testament; it came later.

# 1. The modern definition

To compare the Fathers fairly, we need the definition itself. The Westminster Confession (1646), shared in substance by Catholics, Orthodox and most Protestants, says:

> "In the unity of the Godhead there be three persons, of one substance, power, and eternity: God the Father, God the Son, and God the Holy Ghost. The Father is of none, neither begotten, nor proceeding; the Son is eternally begotten of the Father; the Holy Ghost eternally proceeding from the Father and the Son." (2.3)

So a Father who held the modern doctrine would teach all of these:

| | Point of the modern doctrine | What it rules out |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | One God in **three persons** | One God who is the Father, revealed as Son and Spirit |
| 2 | The three are of **one substance** (_homoousios_) | A Son of a different or lesser nature |
| 3 | The three are **co-equal** in power and glory | A Son "in the second place," "less" than the Father |
| 4 | The Son is **eternally** begotten | A Son who became Son at creation or at the incarnation |
| 5 | The Son is begotten by **nature**, not by the Father's will | A Son produced by a decision of the Father |
| 6 | The Holy Spirit is a **third divine person**, worshipped with the Father and the Son | A Spirit who is a power, a creature, or the Son |
| 7 | (West) The Spirit proceeds from the Father **and the Son** | The Eastern view, from the Father alone |

Keep this table in mind as we go through the centuries.

# 2. Where the "unnameable God" came from

### From Homer to the philosophers

Homer's _Iliad_ and _Odyssey_ (8th–7th centuries B.C.) were the Bible of the Greeks. But Homer's gods have the worst human vices, made worse by immortality: they are jealous, lustful, angry and dishonest. They demand worship while acting like criminals, and human beings are collateral damage in their quarrels.

Thinking Greeks could not accept such gods, but they would not reject Homer either. So from about **525 B.C.** (Theagenes of Rhegium is the first known example) they began to read Homer as **allegory**. Athena holding back Achilles became reason restraining anger; the adultery of Ares and Aphrodite became the cosmic forces of strife and love.

That led to a new idea of the divine. **Plato** (4th century B.C.) compared the highest reality, the Form of the Good, to the **sun**: the source of light, life and being, yet "beyond being" and too bright to look at (_Republic_ 508–509). In the _Timaeus_ he wrote: "To find the maker and father of this universe is a hard task, and having found him, **to declare him to all men is impossible**" (28c).

In the centuries around Christ, the "Middle Platonists" developed this into a **first God** who is remote and **ineffable** (beyond words, _arrhētos_, Alcinous, _Handbook of Platonism_ 10), and a **second god** who acts in the world. Numenius (2nd century A.D.) spoke openly of a "first god" and a "second god," the craftsman of the world. Later still, **Plotinus** (A.D. 204–270) described "the One," ineffable and beyond all names, from which flow Mind and Soul — a triad of descending divine levels that has often been compared to the Trinity.

### Hellenized Jews: the Name becomes unspeakable

The Jews of Alexandria in Egypt lived inside this Greek world. **Philo of Alexandria** (c. 20 B.C.–A.D. 50) taught that "**no proper name at all can be given**" to the truly Existent One (_On the Change of Names_ 11), and he called the Logos (the Word) "**the second God**" (_Questions on Genesis_ 2.62).

Jewish reverence for the Name hardened in the same period into a refusal to pronounce it at all. The Mishnah (c. A.D. 200) records Abba Saul's opinion that one who "pronounces the Name according to its letters" has no share in the world to come (_Sanhedrin_ 10:1). The Greek translation of the Old Testament, the **Septuagint** (LXX), made in Alexandria, shows the same tendency:

- **Leviticus 24:16.** The Hebrew condemns the one who **blasphemes** the name: "And he that blasphemeth the name of the LORD, he shall surely be put to death." The Septuagint reads: "he that **names** the name of the Lord, let him die the death… let him die for **naming** the name of the Lord." Blasphemy became mere pronunciation.
- **Exodus 3:14.** "I AM THAT I AM" became _egō eimi ho ōn_, "I am **the one who is**" — the language of Greek philosophy about pure Being.
- **The name itself.** Some of the oldest Septuagint fragments (such as Papyrus Fouad 266, 1st century B.C.) still wrote the name YHWH in Hebrew letters inside the Greek text; later copies replaced it with **Kyrios**, "Lord." That is why most Bibles today print "the LORD" in capitals where the Hebrew has YHWH.

The Orthodox churches still use the Septuagint as their Old Testament and regard it as part of Sacred Tradition.

Yet in the Old Testament itself, God's people used and honoured His name freely: they swore by it (Deuteronomy 6:13), praised it (Psalm 113:1-3), and the priests "put my name upon the children of Israel" in the blessing (Numbers 6:22-27). God said of His name: "**this is my name for ever**, and this is my memorial unto all generations" (Exodus 3:15).

# 3. Christians who wanted Christianity to be a philosophy

The New Testament warns:

> **Colossians 2:8-9:** "Beware lest any man spoil you through **philosophy and vain deceit, after the tradition of men**, after the rudiments of the world, and not after Christ. For in him dwelleth all the fulness of the Godhead bodily."

> **1 Corinthians 1:22-23:** "For the Jews require a sign, and **the Greeks seek after wisdom**: But we preach Christ crucified, unto the Jews a stumblingblock, and unto the Greeks foolishness."

In the second century, Christianity was despised by educated pagans as a superstition of slaves and women. A group of converted philosophers, the **apologists**, set out to defend it in the language of the schools.

- **Justin Martyr** (c. 150–160) kept wearing the philosopher's cloak after his conversion and called Christianity "this philosophy alone… safe and profitable" (_Dialogue with Trypho_ 8). He taught that the divine Logos had sown seeds of truth in the philosophers, so that "those who lived reasonably are Christians, even though they have been thought atheists; as, among the Greeks, **Socrates and Heraclitus**" (_First Apology_ 46). He claimed Plato had borrowed from Moses, and that in the _Timaeus_ Plato "**gave the second place to the Logos** which is with God… and **the third to the Spirit**" (_First Apology_ 60).
- **Clement of Alexandria** (c. 200) said that philosophy was given to the Greeks "as a schoolmaster to bring them to Christ," as the Law was to the Jews (_Stromata_ 1.5).
- **Origen** (c. 185–254) studied, according to the pagan philosopher Porphyry, under Ammonius Saccas, the teacher of Plotinus (Eusebius, _Church History_ 6.19).
- Even **Tertullian**, who protested "What indeed has Athens to do with Jerusalem?" (_Prescription against Heretics_ 7), reasoned with Stoic ideas about substance and taught that God is a body.

These men did not intend to corrupt the faith. But they **identified the Father with the supreme, nameless God of the philosophers**, and the Son with the philosophers' Logos, the "second" divine power who deals with the world. That is the soil the Trinity grew from.

### Justin: the Father has no name

> "For no one can utter **the name of the ineffable God**; and if any one dare to say that there is a name, **he raves with a hopeless madness**." (_First Apology_ 61)

> "But to the Father of all, who is unbegotten, **there is no name given**. For by whatever name He be called, He has as His elder the person who gives Him the name. But these words, Father, and God, and Creator, and Lord, and Master, are not names, but appellations." (_Second Apology_ 6)

Compare God's own words: "Therefore **my people shall know my name**: therefore they shall know in that day that I am he that doth speak: behold, it is I" (Isaiah 52:6).

# 4. The Fathers, one by one

### The apostolic fathers (c. 95–150): no Trinity, no "persons"

The earliest writings after the New Testament use the words Father, Son and Spirit, as the Bible does, but they have no doctrine of three persons, and some of their statements fit the modern doctrine badly.

- **Clement of Rome** (c. 96): "Have we not one God, and one Christ, and one Spirit of grace poured out upon us?" (_1 Clement_ 46.6). Three are named; nothing is said about persons, substance or equality.
- **Ignatius of Antioch** (c. 110) speaks of Jesus in the strongest terms: "**our God, Jesus the Christ**, was conceived in the womb by Mary" (_Ephesians_ 18.2); the believers are "kindled into life by **the blood of God**" (_Ephesians_ 1.1); there is "one Physician… **God existing in flesh**" (_Ephesians_ 7.2); and "there is **one God, who manifested Himself through Jesus Christ His Son, who is His Word that proceeded from silence**" (_Magnesians_ 8.2). Ignatius never speaks of three persons.
- **The Didache** (c. 100) gives the threefold formula of Matthew 28:19 for baptism (7.1), but in the same work speaks of "those who have been baptized **into the name of the Lord**" (9.5).
- **The Shepherd of Hermas** (c. 140), read as Scripture in many churches, says: "that Holy Spirit… **that Spirit is the Son of God**" (_Similitude_ 9.1.1), and that "the holy, pre-existent Spirit, which created the whole creation, God made to dwell in flesh that He desired" (_Similitude_ 5.6.5). For Hermas, the Son and the Holy Spirit are not two distinct persons.
- **2 Clement** (c. 150): "Christ the Lord who saved us, **being first spirit, became flesh**" (9.5).

### Justin Martyr (c. 150–160): "another God," in the second place

Justin is the first Christian writer to clearly make the Son **a second divine being below the Father**:

> "There is, and there is said to be, **another God and Lord subject to the Maker of all things**; who is also called an Angel." (_Dialogue_ 56)

> "…He is **another than the God who made all things — numerically, I mean, not in will**." (_Dialogue_ 56)

> "…we reasonably worship Him, having learned that He is the Son of the true God Himself, and holding Him **in the second place**, and the prophetic Spirit **in the third**." (_First Apology_ 13)

And the Son was produced by the Father's **will** before creation: "God begat before all creatures a Beginning, a certain rational power from Himself" (_Dialogue_ 61); He was "begotten from the Father **by His power and will**" (_Dialogue_ 128). In one place Justin even lists, between the Son and the Spirit, "the host of the other good angels who follow and are made like to Him" as objects of Christian worship and reverence (_First Apology_ 6).

**Against the modern table:** no three persons of one substance (1, 2); a Son "subject to" and "in the second place" (3); a Son begotten by the Father's will (5); a Father with no name.

### Theophilus of Antioch (c. 180): the first "triad"

Theophilus is the first writer known to use the word "triad" (_trias_) for God — but his triad is "**God, and His Word, and His Wisdom**," and he immediately adds a **fourth**: "And the fourth is the type of man" (_To Autolycus_ 2.15). The Word was first "innate in the heart of God," and then, when God wished to create, "God begat Him, **emitting** Him along with His Wisdom before all things" (2.10, 2.22): a Word that becomes a Son at creation.

**Against the table:** Wisdom, not a "Holy Spirit person"; a Son "emitted" for creation rather than eternally begotten (4).

### Athenagoras and Tatian (c. 170–180)

**Athenagoras** calls the Son "the **first product** of the Father," explaining that God, being eternal Mind, always had the Logos within Him, and that He "came forth" to be the form and energy of creation (_Plea for the Christians_ 10). **Tatian** says the Logos "springs forth" by the Father's will and is "the first-begotten work of the Father" (_Address to the Greeks_ 5).

### Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 180): the Father above all

Irenaeus is a great defender of the faith against the Gnostics, but he never uses "Trinity" or "three persons," and he plainly places the Father above the Son. Commenting on the fact that the Son did not know the day of judgment (Mark 13:32):

> "…that we may learn through Him that **the Father is above all things**. For 'the Father,' says He, '**is greater than I**.'" (_Against Heresies_ 2.28.8)

For Irenaeus the Son and the Spirit are **the "hands" of God**: "the Father… His hands, that is, the Son and the Holy Spirit, by whom and in whom, freely and spontaneously, He made all things" (_Against Heresies_ 4.20.1; also 5.6.1). The hands of a person are not two additional persons.

**Against the table:** a Father "above" and "greater" (3); no third person (6); no "three persons" (1).

### Hippolytus of Rome (c. 200–235): the Word becomes Son

Hippolytus fought the believers in Rome who taught that the Father Himself became incarnate. His own view: the Word existed with God, and was **called Son because He would become Son** at the incarnation:

> "What Son of His own, then, did God send through the flesh but the Word, whom He addressed as Son **because He was to become such in the future**?" (_Against Noetus_ 15)

And the Word was begotten "when [the Father] willed, as He willed" (_Refutation of All Heresies_ 10.33).

Hippolytus also tells us something remarkable. The bishops of Rome in his day, **Zephyrinus** (199–217) and **Callistus** (217–222), sided with his opponents. He reports that Zephyrinus publicly declared: "**I know that there is one God, Jesus Christ; nor except Him do I know any other** that is begotten and amenable to suffering" (_Refutation_ 9.6; 9.11 in the Greek numbering). Hippolytus calls this heresy; but it was the confession of the bishop of Rome.

**Against the table:** a Son who is Son only from the incarnation (4), begotten by will (5).

### Tertullian (c. 200–220): the first "Trinity" — and a Son who had a beginning

Tertullian of Carthage coined the Latin words that became the vocabulary of the doctrine: _trinitas_, _persona_, _substantia_ (_Against Praxeas_ 2–3, c. 213). Yet his Trinity is not the modern one.

- **The Son had a beginning as Son.** "There was a time when neither sin existed with Him, **nor the Son**" (_Against Hermogenes_ 3). God "could not have been the Father previous to the Son."
- **The Son was born at creation.** The Word was in God as His reason; "then, therefore, does the Word also Himself assume His own form… when God says, 'Let there be light.' **This is the perfect nativity of the Word**" (_Against Praxeas_ 7).
- **The Son is a portion of the Father.** "For **the Father is the entire substance, but the Son is a derivation and portion of the whole**, as He Himself acknowledges: 'My Father is greater than I'" (_Against Praxeas_ 9).
- **God is a body.** "For who will deny that **God is a body**, although 'God is a Spirit'? For Spirit has a bodily substance of its own kind" (_Against Praxeas_ 7).

Tertullian also admits that **most believers** of his day resisted his teaching:

> "The simple, indeed (I will not call them unwise and unlearned), **who always constitute the majority of believers**, are startled at the dispensation [of the three]… They are constantly throwing out against us that we are preachers of **two gods and three gods**… 'We,' say they, '**maintain the Monarchy**' [the rule of one God]." (_Against Praxeas_ 3)

**Against the table:** not co-eternal as Son (4), a "portion" and lesser (2, 3), and a corporeal God.

### Origen of Alexandria (c. 230–250): a "second God"

Origen was the first to teach that the Son is **eternally** begotten — the point closest to the later doctrine. But on almost every other point he differs:

- "We may call Him a **second God**" (_Against Celsus_ 5.39).
- Only the Father is "**the God**" (_ho theos_, with the article), "God of Himself" (_autotheos_); the Son is "God" (_theos_) without the article, God by **participation** in the Father's divinity (_Commentary on John_ 2.2).
- In a Greek fragment of _On First Principles_ (1.3.5): the Father's power is greater than the Son's, and the Son's greater than the Spirit's.
- The Holy Spirit came into being **through the Son**, as one of "all things" made by the Word (_Commentary on John_ 2.10).
- Prayer should be offered to the Father alone: "**we should not pray to any begotten being, not even to Christ Himself**, but only to the God and Father of all" (_On Prayer_ 15.1).

Origen also says that many Christians of his time, afraid "to proclaim **two Gods**," either denied that the Son was distinct from the Father or denied His deity (_Commentary on John_ 2.2).

**Against the table:** a "second God" (2, 3); a Spirit made through the Son (6); prayer to the Father only.

### Novatian, Dionysius, and the Council of Antioch (c. 250–268)

- **Novatian** of Rome (c. 250), in the first Latin book titled _On the Trinity_, teaches that the Father alone is the one God, that the Son proceeded from the Father "when the Father willed," and that the Son is subject to the Father and returns all His authority to Him (_On the Trinity_ 31).
- **Dionysius of Alexandria** (c. 260) wrote that "the Son of God is a work and made," related to the Father as a vine to the vinedresser, and that "being made, He was not before He came to be." Athanasius later tried to explain this away (_On the Opinion of Dionysius_ 4).
- **The Council of Antioch** (268), which deposed Paul of Samosata, **rejected the word _homoousios_** ("of one substance"). Fifty-seven years later, the Council of Nicaea made that same word the test of orthodoxy (Athanasius, _On the Councils_ 43; Hilary, _On the Councils_ 81, 86).

None of them had "the right Trinity." If the apostles had taught it, their followers would not have needed three hundred years, an emperor and a series of councils to discover it.

# 5. The fourth century: emperors and councils define the doctrine

### Nicaea (325)

When the presbyter **Arius** of Alexandria taught that the Son was a creature ("there was when He was not"), Emperor **Constantine** — not yet baptized — called a council at Nicaea, attended and presided over its sessions, and promoted the word **_homoousios_**, the very word the Council of Antioch had rejected in 268.

The original Creed of Nicaea:

- says the Son is "of the substance of the Father," "true God of true God," "of one substance (_homoousios_) with the Father";
- says about the Spirit only: "**And in the Holy Spirit**." Nothing about His divinity, His worship, or His being a person;
- ends with an anathema on those who say the Son is "**of another _hypostasis_ or _ousia_**" than the Father — treating _hypostasis_ and _ousia_ as the **same thing**. Fifty years later, orthodoxy would require exactly the opposite: **one _ousia_, three _hypostases_**.

### Sixty years of councils

Nicaea did not settle anything. For the next half century, councils contradicted each other according to which emperor was in power: Antioch (341), Sardica (343), Sirmium (351, 357 — the "Blasphemy of Sirmium," which said the Father is greater and forbade the word "substance"), Rimini and Seleucia (359), Constantinople (360). Athanasius, the champion of Nicaea, was exiled **five times**. After Rimini, Jerome wrote: "**The whole world groaned, and was astonished to find itself Arian**" (_Dialogue against the Luciferians_ 19).

### The Cappadocians redefine the words

In the 360s–380s, **Basil of Caesarea**, **Gregory of Nazianzus** and **Gregory of Nyssa** gave the doctrine its classic form by making a new distinction: _ousia_ (the one shared divine essence) and _hypostasis_ (each of the three) are **not** the same (Basil, _Letters_ 214, 236). The terms Nicaea had treated as one were now divided.

And the Spirit was still undecided. In 380, Gregory of Nazianzus described the confusion among the orthodox themselves:

> "Of the wise men amongst ourselves, **some have conceived of Him as an Activity, some as a creature, some as God; and some have been uncertain** which of these to call Him, from reverence for Scripture, they say, as though it did not make the matter clear either way." (_Oration_ 31.5)

Basil himself avoided calling the Holy Spirit "God" outright in public, and Gregory had to defend his caution.

### The emperor makes it law (380)

On February 27, 380, before the council met, Emperor **Theodosius I** issued the **Edict of Thessalonica**:

> "…let us believe the one deity of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, in equal majesty and in a holy Trinity. We authorize the followers of this law to assume the title of Catholic Christians; but as for the others, since, in our judgment they are **foolish madmen**, we decree that they shall be branded with the ignominious name of heretics… They will suffer in the first place the chastisement of the divine condemnation and **in the second the punishment of our authority**." (_Theodosian Code_ 16.1.2)

### Constantinople (381)

The next year Theodosius called the Council of Constantinople, which added to the Creed the article on the Spirit: "the Lord, the giver of life, who proceeds from the Father, who with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified." (The text of this creed is known to us only from the Council of Chalcedon in 451.)

### The first execution (385)

In **385**, Bishop **Priscillian** of Ávila and several followers were beheaded at Trier by the usurper emperor Magnus Maximus, after being accused by other bishops. The formal charge was sorcery, but his teaching had been condemned as heretical, and among the accusations was that he confused the Father, Son and Spirit as one. He was the first Christian put to death by a Christian government over a charge of heresy. Martin of Tours and Ambrose of Milan protested.

### Augustine: "not to say it, but not to be silent"

Augustine's _On the Trinity_ (c. 400–420) gave the West its doctrine, including the idea that the Spirit proceeds from the Father **and the Son** (15.26.47). And he admitted that even the word "persons" does not really explain anything:

> "…when the question is asked, What three? human utterance is weighed down by deep poverty of speech. All the same, **we say three 'persons,' not that we wish to say it, but that we may not be silent**." (_On the Trinity_ 5.9.10)

# 6. After the councils: the doctrine keeps growing

- **The Athanasian Creed** (_Quicunque vult_), written in Latin in the late fifth or sixth century — long after Athanasius — states the doctrine in its fullest Western form and declares that whoever does not "keep whole and undefiled" this faith "**without doubt he shall perish everlastingly**."
- **Toledo III (589).** When the Visigothic king Reccared of Spain renounced Arianism, the council inserted into the Creed that the Spirit proceeds from the Father "**and the Son**" (_filioque_). This is the first council known to have added it to the Creed.
- **Aachen (809).** Charlemagne's theologians pressed for the filioque. **Pope Leo III** refused to add it, and had the Creed **without** the filioque engraved in Greek and Latin on two silver shields in St. Peter's.
- **1014.** At the coronation of Emperor Henry II, Rome finally sang the Creed with the filioque.
- **1054.** The filioque was one of the issues in the great schism between Rome and Constantinople.
- **Lateran IV (1215)** and **Lyons II (1274)** defined further points; **Florence (1439–1442)** defined that in God "everything is one, where there is no opposition of relation."
- The **Orthodox churches still reject** the filioque as an unauthorized addition to the Creed of 381.

So, more than a thousand years after the apostles, the two largest churches that claim to have preserved apostolic tradition **still do not agree on the Trinity**.

# 7. The Name: the Son inherited the Father's name

While the philosophers' Father became a God without a name, the Bible moves in the opposite direction: from the promise that God's people would **know His name**, to the revelation of that name in the Son.

### One God, one name

> **Deuteronomy 6:4:** "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God is one LORD." (Jesus called this "the first of all the commandments," Mark 12:29.)

> **Zechariah 14:9:** "And the LORD shall be king over all the earth: in that day shall there be **one LORD, and his name one**."

> **Isaiah 43:10-11:** "…before me there was no God formed, neither shall there be after me. I, even I, am the LORD; and **beside me there is no saviour**."

> **Isaiah 52:6:** "Therefore **my people shall know my name**: therefore they shall know in that day that I am he that doth speak: behold, it is I."

> **Proverbs 30:4:** "…**what is his name, and what is his son's name**, if thou canst tell?"

> **Exodus 23:21:** "Beware of him, and obey his voice… for **my name is in him**."

### The Son is called the Father

> **Isaiah 9:6:** "For unto us a child is born, unto us **a son** is given: and the government shall be upon his shoulder: and his name shall be called Wonderful, Counsellor, **The mighty God, The everlasting Father**, The Prince of Peace."

### The name given to the Son: JESUS, "YHWH is salvation"

> **Matthew 1:21, 23:** "And she shall bring forth a son, and thou shalt call his name **JESUS**: for **he shall save his people from their sins**… and they shall call his name Emmanuel, which being interpreted is, **God with us**."

The name was not chosen by Joseph and Mary; it was delivered by the angel. In Hebrew, **Yeshua** (the short form of **Yehoshua**, Joshua; compare Numbers 13:16) means "**YHWH is salvation**" or "YHWH saves." The angel explains the name: He shall save His people. And God had said, "I, even I, am the LORD; and beside me there is no saviour" (Isaiah 43:11). The name of the Saviour is the name of YHWH revealed as Saviour.

### Inherited, given, and above every name

> **Hebrews 1:4:** "Being made so much better than the angels, as **he hath by inheritance obtained** a more excellent name than they."

A son inherits his **father's** name.

> **Philippians 2:9-11:** "Wherefore God also hath highly exalted him, and **given him a name which is above every name**: That **at the name of Jesus every knee should bow**, of things in heaven, and things in earth, and things under the earth; And that every tongue should confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father."

Paul is quoting what YHWH said of **Himself**: "I am God, and there is none else… **unto me every knee shall bow**, every tongue shall swear" (Isaiah 45:22-23). What belongs to YHWH alone now belongs to the name of Jesus.

### He came in the Father's name and manifested it

> **John 5:43:** "**I am come in my Father's name**, and ye receive me not…"

> **John 10:25:** "…the works that I do **in my Father's name**, they bear witness of me."

> **John 10:30:** "I and my Father are one."

> **John 14:9:** "…**he that hath seen me hath seen the Father**; and how sayest thou then, Shew us the Father?"

> **John 17:6:** "**I have manifested thy name** unto the men which thou gavest me out of the world…"

> **John 17:11:** "Holy Father, keep through thine own name those whom thou hast given me…" — in the great majority of Greek manuscripts, and in most modern translations: "keep them in **your name, the name which you have given me**."

> **John 17:26:** "And **I have declared unto them thy name**, and will declare it…"

> **Hebrews 2:12** (quoting Psalm 22:22): "Saying, **I will declare thy name** unto my brethren, in the midst of the church will I sing praise unto thee."

Justin said that whoever claims the Father has a name "raves with a hopeless madness." Jesus said He had **manifested and declared** the Father's name.

### The one name in eternity

> **Revelation 14:1:** "…an hundred forty and four thousand, having **his Father's name** written in their foreheads." (Most Greek manuscripts: "**his name and his Father's name**.")

> **Revelation 22:3-4:** "…but **the throne of God and of the Lamb** shall be in it; and his servants shall serve **him**: And they shall see **his** face; and **his name** shall be in their foreheads."

One throne, one face, one name.

### Everything in the name of Jesus

> **Acts 4:12:** "Neither is there salvation in any other: for **there is none other name under heaven** given among men, whereby we must be saved."

> **Joel 2:32 / Romans 10:13:** "whosoever shall call on the name of the LORD shall be delivered… whosoever shall call upon the name of the Lord shall be saved."

The apostles did **everything** in that one name:

- **All our words and deeds:** "whatsoever ye do in word or deed, do all in the name of the Lord Jesus" (Colossians 3:17).
- **Thanksgiving:** "giving thanks always… in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ" (Ephesians 5:20).
- **Prayer:** "Whatsoever ye shall ask the Father in my name, he will give it you" (John 16:23).
- **Preaching:** Paul "had preached boldly at Damascus in the name of Jesus" (Acts 9:27); "repentance and remission of sins should be preached **in his name** among all nations" (Luke 24:47).
- **Healing:** "In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth rise up and walk" (Acts 3:6).
- **Casting out demons:** "In my name shall they cast out devils" (Mark 16:17).
- **Gathering together:** "where two or three are gathered together in my name, there am I" (Matthew 18:20).
- **Suffering:** "rejoicing that they were counted worthy to suffer shame for his name" (Acts 5:41).
- **Baptism:** see below.

Not a single prayer, healing, sermon or baptism in the New Testament is performed "in the name of the Trinity."

### Baptism in the name of Jesus

> **Acts 2:38:** "Repent, and be baptized every one of you **in the name of Jesus Christ** for the remission of sins, and ye shall receive the gift of the Holy Ghost."

> **Acts 8:16:** "…only they were baptized **in the name of the Lord Jesus**."

> **Acts 10:48:** "And he commanded them to be baptized **in the name of the Lord**."

> **Acts 19:3-5:** "Unto what then were ye baptized? And they said, Unto John's baptism… When they heard this, they were baptized **in the name of the Lord Jesus**."

> **Acts 22:16:** "…arise, and be baptized, and wash away thy sins, **calling on the name of the Lord**."

> **1 Corinthians 1:13:** "Is Christ divided? **was Paul crucified for you? or were ye baptized in the name of Paul?**" — implying they were baptized in the name of the One who was crucified for them.

> **Romans 6:3-4:** "…so many of us as were **baptized into Jesus Christ** were baptized into his death… we are buried with him by baptism into death."

> **Galatians 3:27:** "For as many of you as have been baptized into Christ have put on Christ."

> **Colossians 2:12:** "Buried with him in baptism…"

### Matthew 28:19: the name, singular

> **Matthew 28:18-19:** "All power is given unto me in heaven and in earth. Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them in **the name** (not "names") of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost."

Jesus commanded baptism in one **name**. The apostles, who heard the command, carried it out by baptizing in the name of Jesus Christ (Acts 2:38). The parallel accounts of the same commission point to the same name: "**in my name**" (Mark 16:15-17), "**in his name** among all nations, beginning at Jerusalem" (Luke 24:47), and John 20:21-23. (Eusebius of Caesarea, before Nicaea, often quoted the commission as "make disciples of all nations **in my name**"; every surviving manuscript of Matthew has the threefold wording, which, as the apostles understood it, describes the one name.)

Baptism in the name of Jesus did not disappear at once. Around A.D. 256, Cyprian of Carthage argued against accepting those baptized "in the name of Jesus Christ" (_Letters_ 73), while an anonymous Roman treatise of the same years, _On Rebaptism_, defended such baptism as valid.

### The Spirit is the Spirit of Jesus

The New Testament does not present the Holy Spirit as a third person with a separate name. It is "the Spirit of Christ," "the Spirit of His Son," "the Spirit of Jesus Christ":

- "…forbidden of the Holy Ghost… but **the Spirit** suffered them not" (Acts 16:6-7; most Greek manuscripts: "**the Spirit of Jesus**").
- "…if so be that **the Spirit of God** dwell in you. Now if any man have not **the Spirit of Christ**, he is none of his" (Romans 8:9).
- "…**the Spirit of Christ** which was in them [the prophets]…" (1 Peter 1:11).
- "God hath sent forth **the Spirit of his Son** into your hearts" (Galatians 4:6).
- "…the supply of **the Spirit of Jesus Christ**" (Philippians 1:19).
- "Now **the Lord is that Spirit**" (2 Corinthians 3:17).
- "…the Comforter… the Spirit of truth… he dwelleth with you, and shall be in you. **I will not leave you comfortless: I will come to you**" (John 14:16-18).
- "…the Holy Ghost, whom the Father will send **in my name**" (John 14:26).
- "…**lo, I am with you alway**, even unto the end of the world" (Matthew 28:20).

So the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit share **one name**, and that name is JESUS. "For in him dwelleth all the fulness of the Godhead bodily" (Colossians 2:9). "God was manifest in the flesh" (1 Timothy 3:16).

# 8. Common objections

**"The Fathers were groping for words; the councils only clarified what the apostles believed."** Clarifying would mean saying the same thing more precisely. But the councils reversed what the Fathers said: Justin's "another God… subject," Tertullian's "there was a time when… the Son" was not, Origen's "second God," the Council of Antioch's rejection of _homoousios_ — all of these became heresy by later standards. A doctrine that condemns everyone who taught before it was defined is not a clarification; it is a development.

**"Tertullian and Hippolytus condemned those who said the Father became incarnate."** They did. But their own alternative — a Son lower than the Father, who became Son at creation or at the incarnation — was condemned later too. And they themselves tell us that the bishops of Rome (Zephyrinus, Callistus) and "the majority of believers" held to the Monarchy of the one God.

**"Matthew 28:19 proves three persons."** It mentions one **name**, and the apostles fulfilled it with the name of Jesus Christ. Father, Son and Holy Spirit are three ways God has revealed Himself — as Father in creation, as Son in redemption, as Spirit in the church — not three persons with three names.

**"Ignatius and Clement mention Father, Son and Spirit."** So does the Bible, and so does every Oneness believer. Naming Father, Son and Spirit is biblical; defining them as three co-equal persons of one substance is the later development.

**"John 1:1 says the Word was with God."** And the same Gospel says "the Word **was** God" and "was made flesh" (John 1:1, 14). Hebrews calls the Son "the express image of his person" (Hebrews 1:3, singular). The Word is God's own self-expression, not a second divine person.

# Conclusion

If the apostles had taught "one God in three co-equal, co-eternal persons," the men who learned from them would have taught it. They did not. The earliest writers spoke of Jesus as God in the flesh without any doctrine of persons; the philosopher-apologists of the second century made the Father the nameless supreme God of Plato and the Son a "second God" in "the second place"; Tertullian invented the vocabulary but taught a Son who had a beginning; Origen taught a "second God" to whom one should not pray. The doctrine as it is defined today was assembled in the fourth century by councils called and enforced by emperors — Nicaea in 325, Constantinople in 381, the Edict of Thessalonica in 380 — and completed in the West by the filioque of Toledo in 589, which Rome itself adopted only in 1014 and the East rejects to this day.

That is a human development. The Bible's development runs the other way: from "my people shall know my name" (Isaiah 52:6) to the child who is "the everlasting Father" (Isaiah 9:6), to the name JESUS — YHWH is salvation — inherited by the Son, manifested to the disciples, and invoked in every prayer, healing and baptism of the apostolic church.

"And the LORD shall be king over all the earth: in that day shall there be one LORD, and his name one." (Zechariah 14:9)

"Neither is there salvation in any other: for there is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved." (Acts 4:12)
