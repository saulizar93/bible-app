## The claim

A common Catholic argument goes like this: "There was no Bible until the Catholic Church gave it to you. Pope Damasus and the Council of Rome (382) decided which books belong in the Bible, and the councils of Hippo (393) and Carthage (397) confirmed it. So if you accept the Bible, you are trusting the authority of the Catholic Church, and you should accept her traditions too."

This page answers that claim with Scripture and history. In short:

- **God** gave us the Bible, through the **prophets**, the **apostles** and their companions, at the moment they wrote it.
- **Israel** kept the Old Testament, and the **first-century churches** received, read and copied the New Testament as Scripture from the beginning, centuries before any council at Rome.
- Councils did not make books inspired. At most they **recognized** books the churches had already received for generations, and Rome added to them books that its own greatest scholars had said were not canonical.
- When Latin stopped being the language of the people, the medieval Roman Church **kept the Bible locked in Latin**, forbade or restricted translations, and burned translators. It took the **blood of Protestant martyrs** to put the Scriptures back in the hands of ordinary people.
- And once people had the Bible, they **learned to read**, and missionaries went as far as **inventing alphabets** so that people without writing could read God's Word in their own tongue.

The Scripture quotations on this page are from the King James Version.

# 1. Jesus and the apostles: "It is written" means "God says"

For Jesus, the Old Testament was not the opinion of a religious institution. It was **God speaking**, and it was the final authority over every tradition.

> **Matthew 4:4:** "But he answered and said, It is written, Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of God."

Jesus answered each of Satan's temptations with "It is written" (Matthew 4:4, 7, 10). He did not appeal to the priests, the Sanhedrin or the traditions of the elders; the written Word settled the matter.

> **Matthew 22:31-32:** "But as touching the resurrection of the dead, have ye not read that which was **spoken unto you by God**, saying, I am the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob?"

Moses wrote Exodus 3:6 about 1,400 years earlier, but Jesus told the Sadducees that **God was speaking to them** when they read it.

> **Matthew 19:4-5:** "Have ye not read, that he which made them at the beginning made them male and female, **And said**, For this cause shall a man leave father and mother, and shall cleave to his wife: and they twain shall be one flesh?"

In Genesis 2:24 these words are the narrator's comment, yet Jesus says the **Creator** said them. Whatever Scripture says, God says.

> **John 10:35:** "…and **the scripture cannot be broken**."

> **Matthew 5:18:** "For verily I say unto you, Till heaven and earth pass, one jot or one tittle shall in no wise pass from the law, till all be fulfilled."

Jesus also judged religious tradition **by** Scripture, never the other way around:

> **Mark 7:13:** "Making the word of God of none effect through your tradition, which ye have delivered: and many such like things do ye."

The apostles spoke the same way. "The Holy Ghost **by the mouth of David** spake" (Acts 1:16; see also Acts 4:25). The writer of Hebrews quotes Psalm 95 with "as the Holy Ghost saith" (Hebrews 3:7). Paul says that "**all scripture** is given by inspiration of God" (2 Timothy 3:16) — literally "God-breathed" (_theopneustos_). Peter says the prophets "spake as they were moved by the Holy Ghost" (2 Peter 1:21).

Notice who held the Old Testament: "unto them [the Jews] were committed the oracles of God" (Romans 3:2). Israel **kept** the Scriptures; Israel did not **create** their authority. And when the leaders of Israel set their traditions above Scripture, Jesus rebuked them with Scripture. If being the custodian of the Scriptures made an institution their master, then the Sanhedrin would have had authority over Jesus. The Catholic argument proves too much.

# 2. The apostles' writings were Scripture from the start

The New Testament writers knew they were writing with the same authority as the prophets.

**Paul quotes Luke as Scripture.**

> **1 Timothy 5:18:** "For **the scripture saith**, Thou shalt not muzzle the ox that treadeth out the corn. And, **The labourer is worthy of his reward.**"

The first quotation is Deuteronomy 25:4. The second is the words of Jesus as written in **Luke 10:7** ("for the labourer is worthy of his hire"). Paul puts a Gospel on the same level as the Law of Moses and calls both "the scripture" — around A.D. 63, while the apostles were still alive.

**Peter calls Paul's letters Scripture.**

> **2 Peter 3:15-16:** "…even as our beloved brother Paul also according to the wisdom given unto him hath written unto you; As also in all his epistles… in which are some things hard to be understood, which they that are unlearned and unstable wrest, as they do also **the other scriptures**, unto their own destruction."

"The other scriptures" means Paul's letters were already counted among the Scriptures, and already collected ("all his epistles") in Peter's lifetime.

**The apostles' commands stand beside the prophets.**

> **2 Peter 3:2:** "That ye may be mindful of the words which were spoken before by **the holy prophets**, and of **the commandment of us the apostles** of the Lord and Saviour."

> **Ephesians 2:20:** "And are built upon the foundation of **the apostles and prophets**, Jesus Christ himself being the chief corner stone."

**Paul says his letters are commandments of the Lord.**

> **1 Corinthians 14:37:** "If any man think himself to be a prophet, or spiritual, let him acknowledge that **the things that I write unto you are the commandments of the Lord**."

> **1 Thessalonians 2:13:** "…ye received it not as the word of men, but as it is in truth, **the word of God**…"

> **Galatians 1:11-12:** "…the gospel which was preached of me is not after man. For I neither received it of man, neither was I taught it, but by the revelation of Jesus Christ."

**The letters were to be read aloud in the churches and passed on.** This is how the New Testament was "published": not by a council, but by the apostles themselves ordering their writings to be read in the congregations, exactly as the Law was read in the synagogues.

> **1 Thessalonians 5:27:** "**I charge you by the Lord** that this epistle be read unto all the holy brethren."

> **Colossians 4:16:** "And when this epistle is read among you, cause that it be read also in the church of the Laodiceans; and that ye likewise read the epistle from Laodicea."

> **Revelation 1:3:** "Blessed is he that **readeth**, and they that hear the words of this prophecy, and keep those things which are written therein."

Even the verse Catholics quote for oral tradition points to the written letters: "hold the traditions which ye have been taught, whether by word, **or our epistle**" (2 Thessalonians 2:15). What the apostles taught by word, they also wrote; and their writings are the only form of their teaching we can verify today.

**Jude quotes Peter as the words of the apostles.** "Remember ye the words which were spoken before of the apostles of our Lord Jesus Christ; How that they told you there should be mockers in the last time" (Jude 17-18) — a quotation of 2 Peter 3:3.

**Jesus promised the apostles exactly this.** "The Holy Ghost… shall teach you all things, and bring all things to your remembrance, whatsoever I have said unto you" (John 14:26); "he will guide you into all truth" (John 16:13). The Gospels were written so "that ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ" (John 20:31) and "know the certainty of those things" (Luke 1:4).

# 3. The early church used them as Scripture, long before Rome

The writers of the generations after the apostles quote the New Testament constantly and treat it as Scripture. They did not need a council to tell them which books carried the apostles' authority.

### Clement of Rome (c. A.D. 96)

The bishop of Rome himself, writing to Corinth, sends them back to Paul's letter and says Paul wrote it under the Spirit's inspiration:

> "Take up the epistle of the blessed Apostle Paul. What did he write to you at the time when the Gospel first began to be preached? Truly, **under the inspiration of the Spirit**, he wrote to you concerning himself, and Cephas, and Apollos." (_1 Clement_ 47:1-3)

The letter also quotes or echoes Hebrews, Romans, 1 Corinthians and the words of Jesus as found in the Gospels. Note that the church of Rome in A.D. 96 was writing in **Greek**, the common language of the empire.

### Ignatius of Antioch (c. 107–110)

On his way to martyrdom in Rome, Ignatius distinguished his own letters from the apostles' authority:

> "I do not enjoin you, as Peter and Paul did. **They were Apostles**, I am a convict." (_To the Romans_ 4)

He reminds the Ephesians of Paul, "who in all his Epistles makes mention of you" (_To the Ephesians_ 12), and his letters echo Matthew, John and many of Paul's letters.

### Polycarp of Smyrna (c. 110–140)

A disciple of the apostle John, Polycarp quotes Ephesians 4:26 and calls it **Scripture**:

> "For I trust that ye are well versed in **the Sacred Scriptures**… It is declared then **in these Scriptures**, 'Be ye angry, and sin not,' and, 'Let not the sun go down upon your wrath.'" (_To the Philippians_ 12:1)

He also tells the Philippians that Paul "wrote you a letter, which, if you carefully study, you will find to be the means of building you up in that faith which has been given you" (3:2). His short letter quotes or alludes to Matthew, Luke, Acts, Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Ephesians, Philippians, 1 and 2 Thessalonians, 1 and 2 Timothy, 1 Peter and 1 John.

### The Epistle of Barnabas (c. 100–130) and 2 Clement (c. 140–160)

Both quote the words of Jesus from Matthew with the formula used for Scripture:

> "Let us beware lest we be found, **as it is written**, 'Many are called, but few are chosen.'" (_Barnabas_ 4:14, quoting Matthew 22:14)

> "And **another Scripture** also saith, 'I came not to call the righteous, but sinners.'" (_2 Clement_ 2:4, quoting Matthew 9:13)

### Justin Martyr (c. 155)

Describing Sunday worship in Rome, Justin puts the Gospels on the same level as the Old Testament prophets:

> "On the day called Sunday… **the memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets are read**, as long as time permits." (_First Apology_ 67)

He explains that these memoirs "are called Gospels" (_First Apology_ 66).

### Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 180)

Irenaeus, who as a boy had heard Polycarp, quotes nearly every book of the New Testament and declares that there are exactly four Gospels (_Against Heresies_ 3.11.8). And he applies to the **Scriptures** the very words Catholics use for the Church:

> "We have learned from none others the plan of our salvation, than from those through whom the Gospel has come down to us, which they did at one time proclaim in public, and, at a later period, by the will of God, **handed down to us in the Scriptures, to be the ground and pillar of our faith**." (_Against Heresies_ 3.1.1)

> "The Scriptures are indeed perfect, since they were spoken by the Word of God and His Spirit." (_Against Heresies_ 2.28.2)

### And many more, all before 382

| Date | Witness | What it shows |

| c. 170–200 | Muratorian Fragment | A list of New Testament books read in the churches (date debated) |
| c. 200 | Papyri P46, P66, P75 | Paul's letters already copied as one collection; Luke and John in one book |

Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, wrote of those 27 books in 367:

> "These are fountains of salvation, that they who thirst may be satisfied with the living words they contain. In these alone is proclaimed the doctrine of godliness. **Let no man add to these, neither let him take ought from these.**"

And in the same letter he said that Wisdom, Sirach, Esther, Judith and Tobit are "**not indeed included in the canon**, but appointed by the Fathers to be read by those who newly join us."

None of these men was waiting for a council at Rome to tell them which books were Scripture. They already knew, because the churches had received them from the apostles.

# 4. Recognized, not created: Scripture is like Jesus

Many Jews took years to recognize who Jesus was. "He came unto his own, and his own received him not" (John 1:11). "For neither did his brethren believe in him" (John 7:5). Thomas doubted until he saw the wounds, and then confessed, "My Lord and my God" (John 20:28).

Did Jesus **become** God when Thomas finally believed? Of course not. Elizabeth called Mary "the mother of **my Lord**" (Luke 1:43) while He was still in the womb. He was Lord from the beginning; people simply took time to recognize it. When Peter confessed, "Thou art the Christ, the Son of the living God," Jesus answered that "flesh and blood hath not revealed it unto thee, but my Father which is in heaven" (Matthew 16:16-17). Peter did not make Jesus the Christ; God revealed it to him.

Scripture is the same. A book is God-breathed the moment the prophet or apostle writes it (2 Timothy 3:16; 2 Peter 1:21). When Paul wrote to the Thessalonians, it was "the word of God" that day (1 Thessalonians 2:13), not three hundred years later when a synod drew up a list. If the churches took time to settle a few disputed books, that says something about the churches, not about the books. "My sheep hear my voice" (John 10:27): the church does not give the Shepherd His voice; it recognizes it.

The church's role is like that of John the Baptist. John pointed at Jesus — "Behold the Lamb of God" (John 1:29) — but nobody would say John "gave us" Jesus, or that Jesus' authority depended on John. "He must increase, but I must decrease" (John 3:30). A church that points to the Scriptures is doing its job; a church that claims to stand above them because it pointed to them has forgotten that job.

Even the verse Catholics quote — the church is "the pillar and ground of the truth" (1 Timothy 3:15) — describes a pillar. A pillar holds something up and displays it; it does not create it. And Irenaeus, as we saw, used the same words for the Scriptures.

# 5. Rome's list is not today's Catholic Bible

If the Council of Rome in 382 "gave us the Bible," the Catholic Bible of today should match it. It does not.

### What the Roman list says

The list attributed to the Council of Rome under Damasus survives only inside the _Decretum Gelasianum_, a document that many scholars date to the sixth century rather than to 382. Taking it as Catholic apologists do:

- It has **no Baruch**. Jeremiah appears "with Cinoth, that is, his Lamentations," and nothing more. Today's Catholic Bibles include Baruch (with the Letter of Jeremiah as chapter 6), as Trent required.
- It attributes only one letter to the apostle John, and the other two to "**another John, the elder**" (_alterius Iohannis presbyteri epistulae duae_).
- It lists "**two books of Esdras**." In the Greek Old Testament that the Western Church used at the time, "Esdras A" is the book now called 1 Esdras and "Esdras B" is Ezra–Nehemiah, and the North African councils and Augustine likewise list "two books of Esdras" (_On Christian Doctrine_ 2.8.13). Many scholars believe this included 1 Esdras. Trent, by contrast, defined "the first of Esdras, and the second, **which is called Nehemias**," and 1 Esdras (called "3 Esdras" in the Latin Bible) was left out. The official Clementine Vulgate of 1592 printed 3 and 4 Esdras and the Prayer of Manasseh in an appendix after the New Testament, outside the canon, "so that they would not perish entirely." The Orthodox churches still have 1 Esdras in their Bibles today.

The East, meanwhile, never followed Rome's list. Athanasius (367), Cyril of Jerusalem and Gregory of Nazianzus gave the Hebrew Old Testament; the Syriac church long used a New Testament without 2 Peter, 2–3 John, Jude and Revelation; the Ethiopian church's Bible has 81 books, including 1 Enoch. There was no single "Catholic Bible" handed down from Rome to the world.

### The deuterocanonical books were not canonical for over a thousand years

The books Catholics call "deuterocanonical" were never in the Hebrew Scriptures, and the Fathers who knew Hebrew rejected them. **Jerome**, the translator of the Latin Vulgate, wrote in its own prefaces that Wisdom, Sirach, Judith and Tobit "are not in the canon" and that the Church reads them "for the edification of the people, not to give authority to doctrines of the Church." **Pope Gregory the Great** called 1 Maccabees "not canonical." The standard medieval commentary, the _Glossa Ordinaria_, said their authority "is not considered adequate for proving those things which come into doubt." And **Cardinal Cajetan**, the papal legate who confronted Luther, wrote in 1532, in a commentary dedicated to Pope Clement VII, that these books "are not canonical." They became canonical by decree only at the **Council of Trent in 1546**, with an anathema. (The full story, with the quotations, is on the page **Deuterocanonical Books**.)

### Trent tied the canon to the Latin text — modern Catholic Bibles left it

Trent declared the books canonical "**as they are contained in the old Latin Vulgate edition**," and declared that Vulgate the "authentic" text for preaching and debate. But the Latin Bible itself kept changing:

- **1590:** Pope Sixtus V published an official Vulgate with a bull forbidding any change to it. It was so full of errors that, after his death that same year, it was withdrawn and replaced.
- **1592:** The Clementine Vulgate replaced it and remained the official Latin Bible for almost four centuries.
- **1943:** Pope Pius XII (_Divino Afflante Spiritu_) encouraged translating from the **Hebrew and Greek**. Since then, Catholic Bibles like the New American Bible, the Jerusalem Bible and the Biblia de América translate from the original languages, not from the Vulgate.
- **1979:** The **Nova Vulgata** replaced the Clementine text as the official Latin Bible, correcting the Latin to agree with the Hebrew and Greek.

So the text Trent called "authentic" is not the text read in Catholic churches today. Three examples, two of them from Bibles in this app:

**Genesis 3:15.** The Vulgate says _ipsa conteret caput tuum_, "**she** shall crush thy head," which was read for centuries as a prophecy of Mary.

> **Douay-Rheims:** "…**she** shall crush thy head, and thou shalt lie in wait for her heel."
> **Torres Amat:** "…**ella** quebrantará tu cabeza, y tú andarás acechando a su calcañar."
> **Straubinger** (translated from the original languages): "…**este** te aplastará la cabeza, y tú le aplastarás el calcañar."
> **KJV:** "…**it** shall bruise thy head, and thou shalt bruise his heel."

The Hebrew says "he/it." The Nova Vulgata now reads _ipsum_ ("it"), and the New American Bible says "**They** will strike at your head." Two official Catholic Latin Bibles disagree, and two Catholic Spanish Bibles in this app disagree.

**1 John 5:7.** The Clementine Vulgate, the Douay-Rheims and Torres Amat include the "three that bear record in heaven." In 1897 the Holy Office, with the approval of Leo XIII, ruled that its authenticity could not safely be denied. Modern Catholic Bibles, like the New American Bible, now leave it out of the text, and Straubinger prints it in brackets.

**Tobit, Judith, Sirach.** For these books, modern Catholic Bibles translate the Greek, which differs from the Vulgate in whole verses and chapters (the dog wagging its tail in Tobit 11:9, the three "Tobias nights" in Tobit 6 and 8, and Judith 13 ending at verse 20 instead of 31 — see **Deuterocanonical Books**, section 7).

The conclusion is simple: Rome did not hand down one fixed Bible. It recognized the books the churches already held, added books its own scholars called non-canonical, attached them to a Latin text it later abandoned, and today uses Bibles translated from the same Hebrew and Greek the Reformers went back to.

# 6. How Europe lost the Bible: Latin

When the Bible was written, it was in the languages people spoke: Hebrew and Aramaic for Israel, and **Koine Greek**, the common language of the Roman Empire, for the New Testament. When God poured out His Spirit at Pentecost, the crowds said, "how hear we every man **in our own tongue**, wherein we were born?… we do hear them speak in our tongues the wonderful works of God" (Acts 2:8, 11). After the exile, Ezra's Levites "read in the book in the law of God distinctly, and gave the sense, and **caused them to understand** the reading" (Nehemiah 8:8). And Paul insisted: "except ye utter by the tongue words easy to be understood, how shall it be known what is spoken?… in the church I had rather speak five words with my understanding… than ten thousand words in an unknown tongue" (1 Corinthians 14:9, 19).

The early church followed that pattern. As soon as believers in the West spoke Latin rather than Greek, the Bible was translated into Latin (the Old Latin versions, 2nd–3rd centuries). Even the church of Rome, which wrote in Greek in Clement's day, switched to Latin when its people did. And Jerome's translation (c. 382–405) was called the **Vulgate** — from _vulgata_, "common, popular" — because it was a Bible in the **common language** of the people.

Then the Western Empire fell. Visigoths sacked Rome in 410 and Vandals in 455; Germanic kingdoms replaced Roman rule across the West, and the last Western emperor was deposed in 476. Over the following centuries the spoken Latin of the provinces turned into Spanish, Portuguese, French and Italian, while the Germanic, Celtic and Slavic peoples had never spoken Latin at all. By the ninth century ordinary people no longer understood it: the Council of Tours (813) ordered bishops to preach their homilies "in the rustic Roman language or in German, so that all may more easily understand what is said."

At that point the medieval Roman Church had a choice: do for the new nations what Jerome had done for the Romans, or keep the Bible in a language only the clergy could read. With few exceptions, it chose Latin — and when others translated the Bible, it fought them.

# 7. How Rome kept the Bible from the people

These are not Protestant legends; they are papal letters, council canons and official indexes.

- **1080 — Pope Gregory VII**, refusing the Slavic liturgy to Duke Vratislav of Bohemia: "it has pleased Almighty God that holy scripture should be **a secret in certain places**, lest, if it were plainly apparent to all men, perchance it would be little esteemed and be subject to disrespect; or it might be falsely understood by those of mediocre learning, and lead to error."
- **1199 — Pope Innocent III**, writing to Metz, where lay believers were reading the Gospels and Paul in French: he condemned their meetings and warned that the depths of Scripture are not for simple people, applying Hebrews 12:20: "if so much as a beast touch the mountain, it shall be stoned."
- **1229 — Council of Toulouse**, canon 14: "We prohibit also that **the laity should be permitted to have the books of the Old or New Testament**; unless anyone from motives of devotion should wish to have the Psalter or the Breviary for divine offices or the hours of the blessed Virgin; but **we most strictly forbid their having any translation of these books**."
- **1234 — Council of Tarragona** (Aragon): no one may possess the books of the Old or New Testament in the Romance language; anyone who has them must turn them over to the bishop within eight days "**so that they may be burned**."
- **1408 — Constitutions of Oxford** (Archbishop Arundel), against the followers of Wycliffe: "no one shall in future translate on his own authority any text of Holy Scripture into the English tongue… and that no book… of this kind be read… under pain of the greater excommunication."
- **1478 — The Valencian Bible**, one of the first printed Bibles in a Romance language, was hunted down by the Spanish Inquisition; only fragments survive.
- **1559 — Index of Pope Paul IV**: vernacular Bibles could not be printed or kept without the permission of the Holy Office.
- **1559 — Spanish Index of Inquisitor General Valdés**: banned the Bible "in our vulgar tongue or translated into any other vulgar tongue." Spanish Catholics had no legal Bible in Spanish until a decree of **1782**.
- **1564 — Index of the Council of Trent, Rule IV**: "Since it is manifest from experience that if the Holy Bible, translated into the vulgar tongue, be indiscriminately allowed to every one, the temerity of men will cause more evil than good to arise from it," reading a vernacular Bible required **written permission** from the bishop or inquisitor, and "if any one shall have the presumption to read or possess it without such written permission, **he shall not receive absolution until he have first delivered up such Bible**." In 1596 Pope Clement VIII took even that power away from bishops.
- **1713 — Pope Clement XI, _Unigenitus_**, condemned as errors these propositions of the Catholic priest Pasquier Quesnel: "**The reading of Sacred Scripture is for all**" (prop. 80); "To snatch away from the hands of Christians the New Testament… is to close for them the mouth of Christ" (prop. 84); "To forbid Christians to read Sacred Scripture, especially the Gospels, is to forbid the use of light to the sons of light" (prop. 85).
- **1816–1864 — Popes against the Bible societies.** Pius VII (1816) called the Bible societies "a most crafty device, by which the very foundations of religion are undermined"; Leo XII (_Ubi Primum_, 1824), Gregory XVI (_Inter Praecipuas_, 1844) and Pius IX (_Qui Pluribus_, 1846) condemned them; and the **Syllabus of Errors** (1864) listed "Bible Societies" alongside socialism, communism and secret societies as "pests" often condemned "in the severest terms."

Prophecy describes the result: "My people are destroyed for lack of knowledge" (Hosea 4:6); "I will send a famine in the land, not a famine of bread, nor a thirst for water, but of hearing the words of the LORD" (Amos 8:11).

# 8. The blood of the translators

The Bible came back to the people of Europe through men and women who paid for it with their freedom and their lives.

### England

- **John Wycliffe** (d. 1384) and his followers produced the first complete Bible in English. Archbishop Arundel wrote to the pope in 1411 that "that pestilent wretch John Wycliffe… to fill up the measure of his malice, devised the expedient of **a new translation of the Scriptures into the mother tongue**." The Council of Constance condemned him in 1415, and in 1428, on papal orders, his bones were dug up, burned, and the ashes thrown into the river Swift.
- **Lollards** — Wycliffe's followers — were burned under the statute _De haeretico comburendo_ (1401). John Foxe records seven people burned at Coventry in 1519 for teaching their children the Lord's Prayer and the Ten Commandments in English.
- **William Tyndale** translated the New Testament from Greek (1526). The Bishop of London bought up copies and burned them at St Paul's Cross. Tyndale had told a cleric, "If God spare my life, ere many years I will cause a boy that driveth the plough shall know more of the Scripture than thou dost." He was betrayed, strangled and burned at Vilvoorde in 1536. His last words were, "**Lord, open the King of England's eyes.**" Within three years the king ordered an English Bible — largely Tyndale's translation — placed in every parish church.
- **John Rogers**, who completed and published Tyndale's work as the Matthew Bible, was the first Protestant burned under Queen Mary, in 1555.

### Bohemia

- **Jan Hus**, who preached in Czech and followed Wycliffe's teaching on Scripture, came to the Council of Constance under a safe-conduct and was burned on July 6, 1415.

### Spain — the story of our Spanish Bible

- **Francisco de Enzinas** translated the New Testament into Spanish (1543) and presented it to Emperor Charles V; he was imprisoned and escaped. His brother **Jaime de Enzinas** was burned in Rome in 1547.
- **Juan Pérez de Pineda** published a Spanish New Testament in Geneva (1556) and was burned in effigy.
- **Julián Hernández** ("Julianillo"), a small man who smuggled Spanish New Testaments into Seville hidden in wine barrels, was caught, imprisoned and tortured for three years, and burned at Seville on December 22, 1560.
- **Constantino Ponce de la Fuente**, preacher of Seville's cathedral, died in the Inquisition's prison; his bones and effigy were burned. **Dr. Juan Gil (Egidio)** was exhumed and burned the same year.
- **Casiodoro de Reina**, a monk of San Isidoro del Campo in Seville, fled with other monks in 1557. The Inquisition burned him in effigy in Seville in 1562. Hunted across Europe, he finished the first complete Spanish Bible translated from Hebrew and Greek, the **Biblia del Oso**, printed in Basel in **1569**.
- **Cipriano de Valera**, another of those monks, revised it in Amsterdam in **1602**. Every Reina-Valera Bible — the 1909, the 1960, the RVG, and the RVO in this app — descends from the work of these fugitives.

These are only a few names. The Waldensians, who read the Scriptures in their own language from the twelfth century, were hunted for centuries; thousands were massacred in Provence in 1545 and in Piedmont in 1655.

# 9. The Bible taught the world to read

When the Reformation put the Bible back into the common language, it created a reason for every man, woman and child to learn to read.

- **Germany:** Luther's New Testament appeared in 1522 and his complete Bible in 1534. In 1524 he urged every city to found Christian schools so children could read the Scriptures. Economists Sascha Becker and Ludger Woessmann ("Was Weber Wrong?", 2009) showed that Protestant counties in nineteenth-century Prussia were far more literate than Catholic ones, and that Bible reading explains it.
- **Sweden:** the Church Law of 1686 required every person to learn to read, and pastors examined each household every year. By the late 1700s Sweden and Finland had near-universal reading ability.
- **Scotland:** the _First Book of Discipline_ (1560) called for a school in every parish, and the Act for Settling of Schools (1696) made it law.
- **New England:** the Massachusetts school law of 1647 began: "It being one chief project of that old deluder, Satan, to **keep men from the knowledge of the Scriptures, as in former times by keeping them in an unknown tongue**…" — and therefore required every town of fifty households to appoint a teacher.
- **Sunday schools:** Robert Raikes (1780) began teaching poor working children in England to read, using the Bible.
- **The mission field:** political scientist Robert Woodberry ("The Missionary Roots of Liberal Democracy," 2012) found that the historical presence of Protestant missionaries is one of the strongest predictors of mass education, printing and literacy across Africa, Asia and Latin America. Missionary Frank Laubach's "Each one teach one" method (from 1930) has been used to teach millions of adults to read.

# 10. Alphabets for the Gospel

The pattern began early: **Ulfilas** created the Gothic alphabet (4th century) to translate the Bible for the Goths; **Mesrop Mashtots** created the Armenian alphabet (c. 405) for the same reason; **Cyril and Methodius** created the Glagolitic alphabet for the Slavs (860s). That is the apostolic instinct of Pentecost: every nation hearing "in our tongues the wonderful works of God."

Evangelical missionaries carried it to the ends of the earth, reducing hundreds of languages to writing for the first time so their speakers could read the Scriptures:

- **Cree (Canada):** Methodist missionary **James Evans** invented the Cree syllabics (1840), and whole communities learned to read in a short time.
- **Hawaiian:** American missionaries arriving in 1820 developed the Hawaiian alphabet; the complete Hawaiian Bible was printed in 1839, and within a generation reading had spread across the islands.
- **Setswana (southern Africa):** **Robert Moffat** of the London Missionary Society wrote down the language and translated and printed the whole Bible at Kuruman (1857), one of the first complete Bibles printed in Africa.
- **A-Hmao (China):** **Samuel Pollard**, a Methodist, created the Pollard script (c. 1905), still used today.
- **Lisu (Burma and China):** **James O. Fraser** of the China Inland Mission, with Karen evangelist Sara Ba Thaw, created the Fraser alphabet (c. 1915); the Lisu New Testament followed in 1938.
- **Wycliffe and SIL:** **Cameron Townsend**, an American missionary in Guatemala, translated the New Testament into Kaqchikel when a man asked him, "If your God is so great, why doesn't He speak my language?" In 1934 he started the training camp that became **Wycliffe Bible Translators** and **SIL** (1942) — named after John Wycliffe — and their first field was the indigenous languages of Mexico. As of 2024, the Wycliffe Global Alliance reports a complete Bible in **756** languages, a New Testament in **1,726** more, and portions of Scripture in another **1,274**.

"After this I beheld, and, lo, a great multitude, which no man could number, **of all nations, and kindreds, and people, and tongues**, stood before the throne, and before the Lamb" (Revelation 7:9).

# 11. Common objections

**"There were Bibles in the vernacular before Luther."** Some: the German Mentelin Bible (1466) and about eighteen German editions before 1522, the Valencian Bible (1478), and manuscript translations like the Alba Bible (1422–1433), made for a nobleman. They were translated from the Latin, expensive, and few. And when printing made the Bible cheap and the Reformation put it in ordinary hands, Rome answered with Trent's Rule IV, Spain banned it outright for over 200 years (1559–1782), and the Valencian Bible was destroyed by the Inquisition.

**"The Church only banned heretical translations."** Toulouse (1229) forbade the laity to have the Old or New Testament at all, Tarragona (1234) ordered all Romance Bibles burned, Trent required written permission for any vernacular Bible, and _Unigenitus_ (1713) condemned the statement that "the reading of Sacred Scripture is for all."

**"The Catholic Church encourages Bible reading today."** Thankfully, it does: Vatican II (_Dei Verbum_ 22, 1965) said "easy access to Sacred Scripture should be provided for all the Christian faithful," and quoted Jerome: "ignorance of the Scriptures is ignorance of Christ" (_Dei Verbum_ 25). But that came more than four centuries after the Protestant martyrs died for saying the same thing — and it is an admission that they were right.

# Conclusion

The Catholic Church did not give us the Bible. God gave it, "at sundry times and in divers manners… by the prophets," and "in these last days… by his Son" (Hebrews 1:1-2), through the apostles He chose and the companions who wrote with them. Israel preserved the Old Testament; the apostolic churches received, read and copied the New Testament as Scripture from the first century, and the Fathers quoted it as Scripture long before any council at Rome.

At most, the councils **recognized** what the churches already received — and Rome added to it books that Jerome, Gregory the Great and Cardinal Cajetan said were not canonical, tied them to a Latin text that Rome itself later abandoned, and for a thousand years kept the Bible in a language the people could not read, forbidding, burning and punishing those who translated it. The Bible we hold in our own language today was paid for by the blood of men like Tyndale, Hus and Julián Hernández, and carried to the nations by missionaries who taught the world to read.

"To the law and to the testimony: if they speak not according to this word, it is because there is no light in them" (Isaiah 8:20). "Search the scriptures… they are they which testify of me" (John 5:39).
