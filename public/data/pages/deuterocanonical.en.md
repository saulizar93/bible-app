## How this study began

Years ago someone told me, "Protestant Bibles took books out of the Bible." The Catholic Bible has seven more Old Testament books — Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach (Ecclesiasticus), Baruch, 1 and 2 Maccabees — plus additions to Esther and Daniel. I had no answer. I did not even know those books existed. So I began to study, and that question led me down a rabbit hole of church history, Jerome's prefaces, medieval commentaries and the Council of Trent.

What I found is the opposite of the accusation. Protestants did not take books *out*. These books were never part of the Hebrew Scriptures, the Church Fathers who knew Hebrew rejected them, popes and cardinals called them non-canonical for over a thousand years, and they were only declared canonical — with an anathema on anyone who disagreed — at the Council of Trent in **1546**, in the middle of the fight with the Reformation over purgatory and indulgences.

The Catholic Church calls these books "deuterocanonical" ("second canon"); Protestants call them the "Apocrypha." This page uses both terms.

# 1. The Bible Jesus and the apostles used

The Old Testament was entrusted to Israel: "unto them were committed the oracles of God" (Romans 3:2). The Jewish canon was the 22 (or 24) books that Protestants have today, counted differently. The first-century Jewish historian **Josephus** wrote that the Jews had only 22 books "justly believed to be divine," and that books written after the time of Artaxerxes (c. 400 B.C.) were "not esteemed of equal authority with the former, because there has not been an exact succession of prophets since that time" (*Against Apion* 1.8).

Jesus framed the whole Old Testament as running "from the blood of Abel unto the blood of Zacharias" (Luke 11:51) — from Genesis to Chronicles, the first and last books of the Hebrew Bible. The New Testament quotes or alludes to almost every Old Testament book, often with "it is written" or "the Scripture saith," but it **never** introduces a deuterocanonical book that way. (Hebrews 11:35 may allude to the martyrs of 2 Maccabees 7, but alluding to a history is not citing Scripture — Jude 14 quotes 1 Enoch, and no one, Catholic or Protestant, has Enoch in the Bible.)

# 2. What the early Church said

The early lists of Old Testament books, especially from those who checked with the Jews, match the Hebrew canon:

- **Melito of Sardis** (c. 170) travelled to the East "to learn accurately the books of the Old Testament" and listed them; none of the deuterocanonical books appears (Eusebius, *Church History* 4.26).
- **Origen** (c. 240), the greatest biblical scholar of the early Church and one of the very few who learned Hebrew, gave the 22 books "as the Hebrews hand them down," and added: "and outside these are the Maccabees" (Eusebius, *Church History* 6.25).
- **Athanasius** of Alexandria (Festal Letter 39, A.D. 367) listed the canon and then wrote that Wisdom, Sirach, Esther, Judith and Tobit are "**not indeed included in the canon**, but appointed by the Fathers to be read by those who newly join us."
- **Cyril of Jerusalem** (c. 350) told new believers to read "the divine Scriptures, these twenty-two books of the Old Testament… but have nothing to do with the apocryphal writings" (*Catechetical Lectures* 4.33-36).
- **Rufinus** (c. 400) explained that Wisdom, Sirach, Tobit, Judith and Maccabees are "not 'Canonical' but 'Ecclesiastical,'" read in church "but not appealed to for the confirmation of doctrine" (*Commentary on the Apostles' Creed* 38).
- **Gregory of Nazianzus**, **Epiphanius**, **Hilary of Poitiers** and **John of Damascus** (8th century) also followed the Hebrew canon of 22 books.

To be fair, a few lists include Baruch with Jeremiah, and the North African councils of **Hippo (393)** and **Carthage (397)**, led by **Augustine**, did list the deuterocanonical books. But Augustine knew no Hebrew and depended on the Greek Septuagint and the Old Latin version. Even he admitted the difference: the books of the Maccabees "are held as canonical, not by the Jews, but by the Church" (*City of God* 18.36). The men who could actually read the Hebrew Bible — Origen and Jerome — knew these books were not in it.

# 3. Damasus, Jerome and the Vulgate

Pope **Damasus I** (366–384) commissioned his secretary **Jerome** around 382 to revise the Latin Gospels. A list of biblical books that includes the deuterocanonicals is attributed to a council at Rome in 382 under Damasus, but it survives only inside the later *Decretum Gelasianum*, and scholars still debate whether that part is really from 382 or from the sixth century.

After Damasus died, Jerome moved to Bethlehem and, having studied Hebrew with Jewish teachers, spent fifteen years (c. 390–405) translating the Old Testament directly from the Hebrew — the work that became the core of the **Latin Vulgate**. And in the very prefaces of that Bible, Jerome said plainly that the deuterocanonical books are **not canonical**:

> **Preface to Samuel and Kings (the "Helmeted Prologue," 391):** "This prologue to the Scriptures may be appropriate as a helmeted introduction to all the books which we turn from Hebrew into Latin… Wisdom, therefore, which generally bears the name of Solomon, and the book of Jesus, the Son of Sirach, and Judith, and Tobias, and the Shepherd **are not in the canon**."

> **Preface to the books of Solomon:** "As, then, the Church reads Judith, Tobit, and the books of Maccabees, but **does not admit them among the canonical Scriptures**, so let it read these two volumes [Wisdom and Sirach] for the edification of the people, **not to give authority to doctrines of the Church**."

> **Preface to Daniel:** the Hebrew "contains neither the history of Susanna, nor the hymn of the three youths, nor the **fables** of Bel and the Dragon."

> **Letter 107, to Laeta:** "Let her avoid all apocryphal writings, and if she is led to read such not by the truth of the doctrines which they contain but out of respect for the miracles contained in them, let her understand that they are not really written by those to whose names they are ascribed, that many faulty elements have been introduced into them, and that it requires infinite discretion to look for gold in the midst of dirt."

Jerome translated Tobit and Judith only because bishops pressed him to, and he did it quickly and reluctantly — Judith in "one short night's work." In his preface to Judith he notes that among the Hebrews it is placed with the books whose "authority… toward confirming those which have come into contention is judged less appropriate," and that he complied with the request "because this book is found by the Nicene Council to have been counted among the number of the Sacred Scriptures." (No canon from the Council of Nicaea survives, and no other ancient source mentions this.) His judgment never changed: the Vulgate carried the deuterocanonical books, but its own translator labelled them non-canonical.

# 4. Popes and theologians for the next thousand years

Jerome's view became the standard view of the learned Western Church through the Middle Ages.

- **Pope Gregory the Great** (590–604), quoting 1 Maccabees in his *Moralia on Job* (book 19), wrote: "With reference to which particular we are not acting irregularly, if from the books, **though not Canonical**, yet brought out for the edification of the Church, we bring forward testimony." A pope, and a Doctor of the Church, called Maccabees non-canonical.
- **The Glossa Ordinaria** (12th century), the standard commentary printed around the text of the Latin Bible and used by every medieval theologian, opened with a prologue drawn from Jerome: "The canonical books have been brought about through the dictation of the Holy Spirit… [The non-canonical books] the church reads and permits to be read by the faithful for devotion and edification. **Their authority, however, is not considered adequate for proving those things which come into doubt or contention.**" This is "the Gloss" that **Peter Lombard**, **Thomas Aquinas** and the other scholastics cite on almost every page.
- **Hugh of St. Victor** (12th century): "There are also in the Old Testament certain other books which are indeed read [in the church] but are not inscribed in the body of the text or in the canon of authority: such are the books of Tobit, Judith and the Maccabees."
- **Nicholas of Lyra** (14th century), the most widely read Bible commentator of the late Middle Ages, opened his commentary on Tobit: "Here begins the book of Tobit, **which is not in the canon**."
- **Cardinal Ximenes**, editor of the great Complutensian Polyglot Bible (1514–1517) dedicated to Pope Leo X, separated these books from the canonical ones in his preface: they "are not canonical Scripture" and are received by the Church for the edification of the people rather than for establishing doctrine.

# 5. During the Reformation, even Luther's opponents agreed

The most striking witness is **Cardinal Cajetan** (Thomas de Vio), the papal legate who confronted **Martin Luther** at Augsburg in 1518 and the leading Thomist theologian of his day. In 1532 — fourteen years before Trent — he finished a commentary on the historical books of the Old Testament, **dedicated to Pope Clement VII**, and closed it with these words:

> "Here we close our commentaries on the historical books of the Old Testament. For the rest (that is, Judith, Tobit, and the books of Maccabees) are counted by St. Jerome out of the canonical books, and are placed amongst the Apocrypha, along with Wisdom and Ecclesiasticus… Nor be disturbed if you find these books among the canonical ones in councils… These books… **are not canonical, that is, not in the nature of a rule for confirming matters of faith.**"

Luther made the same argument from the same source. In the **Leipzig Debate (1519)**, Johann Eck defended purgatory from 2 Maccabees 12; Luther answered that the book is not part of the canon and appealed to Jerome. In his German Bible (1534), Luther kept the Apocrypha but placed them between the Testaments under the heading: "Apocrypha: these are books which are not held equal to the Holy Scriptures, and yet are profitable and good to read." The first great Protestant Bibles kept them too: Reina's Spanish *Biblia del Oso* (1569) included them with a note in its preface that they are not canonical, Valera's revision (1602) grouped them between the Testaments, and so did the English King James Version (1611). The books were not removed; they were labelled honestly, exactly as Jerome had done.

# 6. Why Trent needed them: purgatory

The Reformation began in 1517 with Luther's 95 Theses against the **sale of indulgences** — payments that were said to shorten the time of souls in purgatory. Purgatory has no clear basis in the Hebrew Scriptures or the New Testament. Its main biblical support is a passage in 2 Maccabees, which the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* still cites today (CCC 1032):

> **2 Maccabees 12:43-46 (KJV):** "And when he had made a gathering throughout the company to the sum of two thousand drachms of silver, he sent it to Jerusalem to offer a sin offering… For if he had not hoped that they that were slain should have risen again, it had been superfluous and vain to pray for the dead… Whereupon he made a reconciliation for the dead, that they might be delivered from sin."

Even this text does not fit Catholic doctrine. Verse 40 says the dead soldiers were found wearing "things consecrated to the idols of the Jamnites, which is forbidden the Jews by the law" — they died in the act of idolatry, which Catholic theology classifies as a mortal sin that sends a soul to hell, not to purgatory, where there is no more forgiveness.

On **April 8, 1546**, in its fourth session, the **Council of Trent** declared all these books "sacred and canonical" and added: "If anyone receive not, as sacred and canonical, the said books entire with all their parts, as they have been used to be read in the Catholic Church, and as they are contained in the old Latin Vulgate edition… **let him be anathema**." The Catholic Encyclopedia (1908) admits that "all through the Middle Ages we find evidence of hesitation about the character of the deuterocanonicals." (Catholic apologists point to the Council of Florence in 1442, which listed these books in a decree for the Jacobite Christians; but that decree avoided the words "canon" and "canonical," and Cardinal Cajetan, writing ninety years later with papal approval, still followed Jerome.) What had been an open question for 1,500 years was closed by anathema 29 years after the indulgence controversy began.

# 7. A faulty, unstable text

Even if one wanted to accept these books, there is no single text to accept.

- **Judith.** Jerome himself wrote in his preface: "**I have removed the extremely faulty variety of the many books**; only those which I was able to find in the Chaldean words with understanding intact did I express in Latin." The Vulgate Judith is considerably shorter than the Greek; chapter 9, Judith's prayer, has 19 verses in the Vulgate and 14 in the Greek.
- **Tobit** survives in at least three different Greek forms (a short one, a long one found in Codex Sinaiticus, and a mixed one), in Aramaic and Hebrew fragments from Qumran, and in Jerome's Latin, which he made in a single day from an Aramaic copy with the help of a Jewish interpreter. They differ in names, details and whole verses.
- **Sirach** exists in a Hebrew original recovered only in part (from the Cairo Genizah, Masada and Qumran), a shorter and a longer Greek form, and a Latin text with many additions; in the Greek manuscripts a block of chapters (30–36) is even out of order.
- **Esther and Daniel**: the Vulgate puts the Greek additions to Esther at the end (Esther 10:4–16:24) and the additions to Daniel as chapters 13–14, because Jerome marked them as not found in the Hebrew. Modern Catholic Bibles insert them into the story with letters (A–F).

Trent canonized the books "as contained in the old Latin Vulgate." Yet modern Catholic Bibles — the New American Bible, the Jerusalem Bible, the Biblia de América — do **not** translate the Vulgate for these books. They translate the long Greek of Sinaiticus for Tobit, the Greek for Judith, the Hebrew and Greek for Sirach. So the text read in Catholic churches today is not the text Trent declared canonical. Examples:

- **Tobit 6:16-22 and 8:4 (Vulgate):** Raphael tells Tobias to remain continent with his bride for three nights of prayer ("for they who in such manner receive matrimony, as to shut out God from themselves… over them the devil hath power"). These verses — the origin of the medieval "Tobias nights" — are **not in the Greek** that modern Catholic Bibles follow.
- **Tobit 11:9 (Vulgate):** "Then the dog, which had been with him in the way, ran before, and coming as if he had brought the news, shewed his joy by his fawning and wagging his tail." The Greek only says the dog went along with them.
- **Sirach 24:24 (Vulgate):** "I am the mother of fair love, and of fear, and of knowledge, and of holy hope" — a line long applied to Mary, absent from the Hebrew and the oldest Greek manuscripts.

# 8. Errors in the books themselves

The Hebrew Scriptures are true in their history and their theology. These books contain historical, geographical and theological errors that their own defenders struggle to explain.

### Historical and geographical errors

- **Judith 1:1:** "In the twelfth year of the reign of Nabuchodonosor, **who reigned in Nineve**, the great city." Nebuchadnezzar was king of **Babylon**, not Assyria; his father had helped destroy Nineveh in 612 B.C., years before his reign began. "Arphaxad, which reigned over the Medes" is unknown to history.
- **Judith 4:3** says that during this war the Jews "were **newly returned from the captivity**" and had re-sanctified the temple — but the return from captivity happened after Nebuchadnezzar's empire had fallen (538 B.C.). The book mixes Assyrian, Babylonian and Persian eras; its general Holofernes and the eunuch Bagoas bear Persian names.
- **Tobit** says he was "young" when the northern tribes broke away from Jerusalem (Tobit 1:4-5; 931 B.C.) yet was carried captive by Shalmaneser (722 B.C.) and died at 158 (Tobit 14:11, KJV) — a lifespan the chronology cannot fit. At the end he hears of Nineveh's destruction by "Nabuchodonosor and Assuerus" (Tobit 14:15); Nineveh fell to Nabopolassar and Cyaxares.
- **Baruch 6:3** (the Letter of Jeremiah) says the exile in Babylon would last "**seven generations**"; the real Jeremiah prophesied **seventy years** (Jeremiah 25:11; 29:10). Baruch 1:1-2 also places Baruch in Babylon, while Jeremiah 43:6-7 says he was taken to Egypt.
- **2 Maccabees 1:18** credits Nehemiah with having "builded the temple and the altar" — the temple was rebuilt under Zerubbabel and Jeshua, about 70 years before Nehemiah (Ezra 3–6).
- **The death of Antiochus IV** is told three different ways: he dies in bed of grief in Babylon (1 Maccabees 6:8-16); he is stoned and hacked to pieces by the priests in the temple of Nanea in Persia (2 Maccabees 1:13-16); and he falls from his chariot and is eaten by worms in the mountains (2 Maccabees 9:7-9, 28). 1 Maccabees places his death after the rededication of the temple; 2 Maccabees, before.
- **Susanna** (Daniel 13) contains Greek puns — the tree names *schinos* and *prinos* answered with the verbs *schisei* ("he will cut") and *prisei* ("he will saw") — which only work in Greek. Jerome reported this objection from Porphyry: the story was composed in Greek, not in the Hebrew of Daniel.

### Theological errors

- **Salvation by almsgiving.** "For alms doth deliver from death, and **shall purge away all sin**" (Tobit 12:9; also Tobit 4:10; Sirach 3:30). Scripture says it is "the blood of Jesus Christ his Son" that "cleanseth us from all sin" (1 John 1:7; Hebrews 9:22).
- **An angel who lies.** Raphael, sent by God, introduces himself as "**Azarias, the son of Ananias the great**, and of thy brethren" (Tobit 5:12), and only later reveals, "I am Raphael, one of the seven holy angels" (12:15).
- **Magic.** Raphael teaches that smoke from a fish's heart and liver drives away demons: "if a devil or an evil spirit trouble any, we must make a smoke thereof… and the party shall be no more vexed" (Tobit 6:7; 8:2-3).
- **Sin from the woman.** "Of the woman came the beginning of sin, and through her we all die" (Sirach 25:24) — but Paul writes that "**by one man** sin entered into the world" (Romans 5:12).
- **Praise for suicide.** Razis, cornered, falls on his sword, throws himself from a wall, then "**plucked out his bowels**, and taking them in both his hands, he cast them upon the throng" — and the book presents this as a noble death (2 Maccabees 14:41-46).
- **The pre-existence of souls.** "Being good, I came into a body undefiled" (Wisdom 8:20) — a Platonic idea, not a biblical one.

### "There was no prophet" — the books admit it

The author of 1 Maccabees himself says that **prophecy had ceased** in his day:

> "…until there should come **a prophet** to shew what should be done with them" (1 Maccabees 4:46).
> "So was there a great affliction in Israel, the like whereof was not since the time that **a prophet was not seen among them**" (1 Maccabees 9:27).
> "…until there should arise **a faithful prophet**" (1 Maccabees 14:41).

The Old Testament is the writing of the prophets (Luke 24:27; 2 Peter 1:21). A book that says there were no prophets in its time is testifying that it does not belong to the prophetic Scriptures.

### The authors don't claim inspiration — they apologize

None of these books says "thus saith the LORD." Their authors write as editors and historians, and some apologize for their work:

- **2 Maccabees** is an abridgment of a five-volume history by "Jason of Cyrene" (2:23). Its writer says his goal was readability and enjoyment: "We have been careful, that they that will read **may have delight**, and that they that are desirous to commit to memory might have ease" (2:25). He calls the work "this painful labour of abridging… a matter of sweat and watching" (2:26), and ends: "**If I have done well**, and as is fitting the story, it is that which I desired: **but if slenderly and meanly, it is that which I could attain unto.** For as it is hurtful to drink wine or water alone… even so speech finely framed delighteth the ears of them that read the story" (15:38-39). That is a literary author decorating a story, not a prophet carried along by the Holy Spirit.
- **Sirach's** translator, in his prologue, asks readers "to pardon us, wherein we may seem to come short of some words, which we have laboured to interpret. For the same things uttered in Hebrew, and translated into another tongue, have not the same force in them."

### 2 Maccabees reads like a comic book

Where 1 Maccabees is sober history, 2 Maccabees turns every victory into a spectacle:

- A horse with a terrible rider in golden armour appears and strikes the temple robber Heliodorus with its forefeet, while two young men scourge him (3:24-26).
- Horsemen in golden armour are seen charging through the sky over Jerusalem for **forty days** (5:2-3).
- In battle, **five men on horses with golden bridles** appear from heaven, shield Judas, and shoot arrows and thunderbolts at the enemy (10:29-30).
- The prophet Jeremiah hides the tabernacle, the ark and the altar of incense in a cave on Mount Nebo (2:4-8).
- The sacred fire of the temple, hidden in a pit during the exile, is found as "thick water" and bursts into flame when the sun shines on it (1:19-22).
- Jeremiah appears in a vision and hands Judas a **golden sword**: "Take this holy sword, a gift from God" (15:12-16).
- Razis tears out his own entrails and throws them at the crowd (14:46).

# Conclusion

The deuterocanonical books are valuable for history: 1 Maccabees tells the story behind Hanukkah, and these books show what Jews believed between the Testaments. Protestants have always said they may be read. But they were never part of the Scriptures entrusted to Israel; Jesus and the apostles never cited them as Scripture; the Fathers who knew Hebrew rejected them; Jerome, the translator of the Vulgate, labelled them non-canonical in the Vulgate itself; a pope, the standard medieval commentary and a cardinal writing to a pope said the same; and they contain errors that no inspired book contains. They became "canonical" by decree in 1546, when Rome needed 2 Maccabees to defend purgatory against the Reformation. Protestants did not take books out of the Bible. Trent put them in.
