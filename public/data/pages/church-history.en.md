## Why this page

The Catholic Church teaches that its doctrines and sacraments were handed down unchanged from the apostles. History shows something else: a church that grew, step by step, into something the apostles never knew — and that, along the way, became deeply corrupted by power, money and violence. Paul warned that it would happen:

> **Acts 20:29-30:** "For I know this, that after my departing shall grievous wolves enter in among you, not sparing the flock. Also of your own selves shall men arise, speaking perverse things, to draw away disciples after them."

> **2 Timothy 4:3-4:** "For the time will come when they will not endure sound doctrine; but after their own lusts shall they heap to themselves teachers, having itching ears; And they shall turn away their ears from the truth, and shall be turned unto fables."

> **Mark 7:7-8:** "Howbeit in vain do they worship me, teaching for doctrines the commandments of men. For laying aside the commandment of God, ye hold the tradition of men, as the washing of pots and cups: and many other such like things ye do."

This page is a timeline. It follows above all **how the sacraments took shape**: when each one appeared, when it was made obligatory, and when it was defined as dogma. It also records the corruption of the papacy and the violence used to impose its doctrines.

Entries marked **[One God]** record believers in the oneness of God through the centuries, and the decrees issued against them and against baptism in the name of Jesus.

Every date here is documented. Where popular anti-Catholic lists repeat claims that cannot be proved (for example "Pope Joan"), we leave them out or say so — the true history is damning enough. For the details, see also the pages **Image veneration**, **Development of the Trinity**, **First Bishops of Rome**, **Matthew 16:18**, **Baptism**, **Who gave us the Bible?** and **Deuterocanonical Books**.

# The sacraments at a glance

| Sacrament | In the New Testament | How it changed | Defined |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Baptism** | Believers, by immersion, in the name of Jesus (Acts 2:38; 8:36-39) | Threefold formula (c. 150 on); triple immersion (c. 200); infants (c. 200 on, general by c. 500); pouring and sprinkling (general in the West from the 1200s) | Florence 1439; Trent 1547 |
| **Lord's Supper → Mass** | Bread and cup, "in remembrance" (Luke 22:19; 1 Cor 11:24-26) | Called a sacrifice (c. 200 on); literal body "born of Mary" (Paschasius, c. 831); "transubstantiation" (word c. 1140); cup taken from the laity | Lateran IV 1215; Constance 1415 (one kind); Trent 1551, 1562 |
| **Penance (confession)** | Confess to God and to one another (1 John 1:9; James 5:16) | Public penance for the lapsed (250); private, repeatable confession from Irish monks (500s–600s) | Annual confession to a priest made obligatory, Lateran IV 1215 |
| **Confirmation** | Part of receiving the Holy Ghost (Acts 8:17; 19:6) | Separated from baptism in the West when bishops reserved the anointing (Innocent I, 416); named "confirmation" (5th c.) | Florence 1439; Trent 1547 |
| **Anointing of the sick → "Extreme unction"** | Prayer and oil for healing (James 5:14-15) | Became the anointing of the dying ("extreme unction," 12th c.) | Florence 1439; Trent 1551 |
| **Holy orders** | Elders/overseers and deacons, married (Titus 1:5-7) | Separate priesthood; celibacy imposed (Siricius 385; enforced Lateran I 1123, Lateran II 1139) | Trent 1563 |
| **Marriage** | Honourable in all (Hebrews 13:4); Eph 5:32 "mystery" | Called a "sacrament" from the Latin Vulgate's _sacramentum_; must be before a priest (Trent 1563, _Tametsi_) | Verona 1184; Florence 1439; Trent 1563 |

The number **seven** was not fixed until the twelfth century: Hugh of St. Victor still counted many more "sacraments," and Peter Lombard (c. 1150) was the first to list the seven. The Council of Florence (1439) defined them, and Trent (1547) anathematized anyone who says there are more or fewer.

# Before Christ: the background

- **332 B.C.** — Alexander the Great conquers Judea. According to Josephus, the Jews showed him the prophecy of Daniel about the Greek king (Daniel 8:5-8, 21), and he treated them with respect (_Antiquities_ 11.8.5). Everywhere he conquered, Greek culture (Hellenism) followed.
- **312 B.C.** — After Alexander's death the empire is divided; the Seleucid dynasty rules Syria and later Judea.
- **175–164 B.C.** — Antiochus IV Epiphanes outlaws circumcision and the Sabbath and desecrates the temple.
- **167 B.C.** — The Maccabean revolt wins Judean independence until Rome arrives in **63 B.C.** The Jews divide into Sadducees (the priestly aristocracy, close to the rulers, who denied the resurrection) and Pharisees (popular with the people, who believed in the resurrection).
- **37 B.C.** — Rome makes Herod the Great king of the Jews. He appointed high priests from Babylon and Egypt (Josephus, _Antiquities_ 15.2.4; 15.9.3).
- **c. 6–4 B.C.** — Jesus is born before Herod's death in 4 B.C. The monk Dionysius Exiguus, who created the "Anno Domini" count in 525, miscalculated by a few years. (The Gregorian calendar we use today came later, in 1582.)

# The apostolic church (A.D. 30–100)

- **c. 30–33** — Jesus is crucified and rises again. The first Christians do everything in the name of Jesus, including baptism, and rebaptize those baptized otherwise (Acts 2:38; 10:48; 19:5).
- **c. 44** — James the son of Zebedee is the first apostle martyred, by Herod Agrippa I (Acts 12:2). No one in the Bible prays to him or to any departed saint.
- **62** — James the brother of the Lord is killed on the orders of the high priest Ananus (Josephus, _Antiquities_ 20.9.1).
- **66–70** — The Jewish revolt; Rome destroys the temple in 70. Christians in Jerusalem had fled to Pella beforehand (Eusebius, _Church History_ 3.5.3).
- **c. 95–100** — The last books of the New Testament are written. More than 5,800 Greek manuscripts of the New Testament survive today.

# From the apostles to Constantine (100–312)

- **c. 110** — Ignatius of Antioch is the first to call the church "catholic" (universal) (_Smyrnaeans_ 8).
- **132–135** — The Bar Kokhba revolt. Many Jews followed Simon bar Kokhba as the Messiah; Christians could not. Hadrian crushes the revolt, renames Judea "Syria Palaestina" and Jerusalem "Aelia Capitolina." Christians and Jews separate decisively, and anti-Jewish feeling grows among Gentile Christians.
- **c. 155** — Justin Martyr is the first to describe baptism with a threefold formula (_First Apology_ 61), and says no one can utter the name of God. He calls Christianity the true "philosophy." (See **Development of the Trinity**.)
- **c. 155** — The church of Smyrna gathers Polycarp's bones as "more precious than gems" and plans to keep his martyrdom day: the beginning of the **cult of relics and martyrs** (_Martyrdom of Polycarp_ 18).
- **c. 180** — Irenaeus says Peter and Paul founded the church of Rome (_Against Heresies_ 3.3.2). Paul did not: the church at Rome existed before he arrived (Romans 1:8-13).
- **c. 200** — Tertullian mentions offerings for the dead on anniversaries and the **sign of the cross** on the forehead (_The Crown_ 3); he also describes **triple immersion** and opposes the baptism of infants (_On Baptism_ 18).
- **c. 213** — Tertullian uses the word _trinitas_ and admits that "the majority of believers" hold to the Monarchy of one God (_Against Praxeas_ 3).
- **c. 190–220** — **[One God]** In Smyrna and Rome, Noetus, Praxeas, Epigonus, Cleomenes and Sabellius teach that the Father and the Son are one God, not two persons, and the bishops of Rome Zephyrinus and Callistus confess "one God, Jesus Christ" (see **First Bishops of Rome**). Tertullian admits that "the majority of believers" hold to the Monarchy (_Against Praxeas_ 3). None of their own writings was preserved; we know them only through their opponents.
- **c. 220** — Tertullian mocks a bishop who claims power to forgive the gravest sins as "**the Pontifex Maximus — that is, the bishop of bishops**" (_On Modesty_ 1). _Pontifex Maximus_ was the title of the pagan high priest of Rome; the popes later took it for themselves (in use from the fifteenth century).
- **250–251** — Emperor Decius orders everyone to sacrifice to the gods. Many Christians die; many others sacrifice or buy certificates. Afterwards the church must decide whether to readmit the "lapsed": a **system of public penance** is created (Cyprian, _On the Lapsed_). This is the root of the **sacrament of penance**.
- **256** — Bishop Stephen of Rome claims "the succession from Peter" to impose his view; Cyprian and Firmilian reject him (see **Matthew 16:18**).
- **c. 250** — **[One God]** The Latin Christian poet Commodian writes in such strongly Monarchian terms that the _Catholic Encyclopedia_ calls him "an extreme Monarchian" (_Carmen Apologeticum_).
- **c. 257–262** — **[One God]** Dionysius of Alexandria finds the churches of Libya and Pentapolis full of "Sabellianism" and writes against it (Eusebius, _Church History_ 7.6); Dionysius of Rome condemns both the Sabellians and those who divide God into three (Athanasius, _On the Decrees of Nicaea_ 26).
- **303–311** — The Great Persecution under Diocletian. In **311** Galerius ends it (Edict of Toleration).

# The church joins the empire (312–600)

- **312–313** — Constantine wins at the Milvian Bridge; the Edict of Milan (313) gives Christians freedom. At his request a synod meets in Rome (313) to judge the Donatist dispute — the first time an emperor summons bishops to settle a church quarrel.
- **321** — Constantine makes "the venerable day of the Sun" a civil day of rest. (Christians already met on the first day of the week, Acts 20:7.)
- **325** — Council of Nicaea, called and attended by Constantine, adopts _homoousios_. It says nothing more about the Holy Spirit than "and in the Holy Spirit."
- **326** — Constantine has his son Crispus and his wife Fausta put to death.
- **337** — Constantine is baptized on his deathbed by Eusebius of Nicomedia, a supporter of Arius.
- **c. 363** — The Council of Laodicea forbids Christians to "**invoke angels**" (canon 35) — evidence that the practice had begun.
- **4th century** — Lamps and candles in churches, pilgrimages, and the cult of the martyrs' tombs spread; Vigilantius protests, and Jerome defends them (406). The earliest known prayer to Mary, "Under thy protection" (_Sub tuum praesidium_), dates from about this time.
- **336–351** — **[One God]** Marcellus of Ancyra, a champion of Nicaea who taught that God is one person, is deposed (336); Pope Julius receives him as orthodox (340–341), and the Western bishops at Serdica (343) confess "one hypostasis." His disciple Photinus is condemned (344–351). (See **First Bishops of Rome**.)
- **c. 380** — **[One God]** The _Apostolic Canons_ (canon 50) depose any bishop or presbyter who baptizes with one immersion "into the death of the Lord" instead of the threefold formula (see **Baptism**).
- **380** — Edict of Thessalonica: Nicene Trinitarianism becomes the religion of the empire; others are "madmen" to be punished. **388**: public religious debate is forbidden (_Theodosian Code_ 16.4.2).
- **381** — Council of Constantinople adds the article on the Holy Spirit; "Sabellians" must be received "as heathen" (canon 7).
- **381** — **[One God]** The same council anathematizes "the Sabellians, the Marcellians and the Photinians" (canon 1).
- **385** — Bishop Siricius of Rome issues the first papal decretal, saying that "the blessed apostle Peter… bears them in us," and orders married priests to abstain from their wives. The same year Priscillian is executed — the first Christian put to death by Christians for heresy.
- **c. 405** — Jerome completes the Latin Vulgate. He says the deuterocanonical books are not canonical (see **Deuterocanonical Books**). The Vulgate becomes the official Bible of Rome only at Trent (1546).
- **416** — Pope Innocent I reserves the anointing of the baptized to bishops; in the West it separates from baptism and becomes **confirmation**.
- **431** — Council of Ephesus calls Mary "Mother of God" (_Theotokos_). The pope's legate declares that Peter "lives and judges in his successors."
- **440–461** — Leo I builds the theology of the papacy on Peter.
- **447** — **[One God]** Leo the Great writes against the Priscillianists of Spain for teaching that the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit are one and the same person (_Letter_ 15.1).
- **476** — The Western Roman Empire falls. The bishop of Rome becomes the greatest power in the city.
- **c. 501** — The "Symmachan forgeries" invent the rule that "**the first see is judged by no one**."
- **c. 560** — **[One God]** Pope Pelagius I rules on people baptized only "in the name of the Lord" and requires baptism in the threefold name (Denzinger 445).
- **561** — **[One God]** The First Council of Braga: "If anyone does not confess that the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit are three persons… but says that they are a single and solitary person, as Sabellius and Priscillian have said, let him be anathema" (Denzinger 451).
- **589** — Third Council of Toledo adds the **filioque** to the Creed.
- **590–604** — Gregory the Great. He administers the lands and charity of Italy, promotes relics, and in his _Dialogues_ (593–594) teaches a **purifying fire** after death for small sins and tells of souls freed by Masses offered for them (_Dialogues_, book 4). He calls anyone who claims the title "universal bishop" a "precursor of Antichrist."

# The early Middle Ages (600–1050)

- **607** — Emperor Phocas declares the church of Rome "head of all the churches" for Pope Boniface III (_Liber Pontificalis_).
- **609** — Boniface IV turns the Pantheon, the temple of all the gods, into the church of St. Mary and All Martyrs. (The feast of All Saints on November 1 comes later, in the 700s–800s.)
- **6th–7th centuries** — Irish monks spread **private, repeatable confession** to a priest with fixed penances (the "penitentials").
- **692** — **[One God]** The Council in Trullo repeats that "Sabellians, who teach the identity of Father and Son," must be received "as heathen" and baptized again (canon 95).
- **726–843** — The war over images; Nicaea II (787) commands their veneration (see **Image veneration**).
- **800** — Pope Leo III, accused of perjury and adultery and attacked in Rome (799), crowns Charlemagne emperor. Pope and emperor become allies.
- **866** — **[One God]** Pope Nicholas I rules that those baptized "in the name of Christ only, as we read in the Acts of the Apostles," must not be rebaptized (_Responses to the Bulgarians_ 104; see **Baptism**).

### The Eucharistic controversy of the 9th century

- **c. 831** — **Paschasius Radbertus**, abbot of Corbie, writes _On the Body and Blood of the Lord_, the first full treatise on the Eucharist in the West. He teaches that after consecration the bread and wine are "**no other flesh than that which was born of Mary and suffered on the cross**," and he reads John 6 as literal eating of Christ's flesh. He presents it to King Charles the Bald in 844.
- **c. 843** — At the king's request, **Ratramnus**, a monk of the same monastery, answers him in a book with **the same title**, teaching that the bread and wine are the body and blood of Christ **in a figure** and spiritually, not in the flesh born of Mary. Rabanus Maurus also disagreed with Paschasius.
- Earlier Fathers had read John 6 spiritually: "**Believe, and thou hast eaten**" (Augustine, _Tractates on John_ 25.12); "It is a figure" (Augustine, _On Christian Doctrine_ 3.16.24). And Jesus Himself said:

> **John 6:63:** "It is the spirit that quickeneth; the flesh profiteth nothing: the words that I speak unto you, they are spirit, and they are life."

- **1059** — **Berengar of Tours**, who taught as Ratramnus did, is forced by a Roman synod to swear that the body of Christ is "**sensually, not only in sacrament but in truth, handled and broken by the hands of the priests and crushed by the teeth of the faithful**." He recants again in 1079. Ratramnus' book was later condemned and placed on the Index.

### The corruption of the papacy

- **897** — The "**Cadaver Synod**": Pope Stephen VI has the corpse of Pope Formosus dug up, dressed in papal robes, put on trial, condemned, and thrown into the Tiber. Between 896 and 904 there are nine popes; several are murdered.
- **904–964** — The period historians call the "**pornocracy**": the papacy is controlled by the Roman noblewoman Theodora and her daughter **Marozia**. Pope Sergius III is reported to have fathered Marozia's son, who became **Pope John XI** (931). Marozia had John X imprisoned and killed. Her grandson **John XII** became pope at about eighteen (955); a synod under Emperor Otto I deposed him (963) for murder, perjury, sacrilege, adultery and incest.
- **993** — John XV holds the first formal **papal canonization of a saint** (Ulrich of Augsburg).
- **1022** — The first heretics burned in the West since antiquity, at Orléans, by order of King Robert II.
- **1045** — **Benedict IX sells the papacy** to his godfather, who becomes Gregory VI. For a time three men claim to be pope; the Synod of Sutri (1046) deposes them all.
- **1054** — The **Great Schism** between Rome and Constantinople, over papal authority and the filioque.

# The height of papal power (1050–1300)

- **1059** — Nicholas II gives the election of the pope to the **cardinals**.
- **1075–1077** — Gregory VII's _Dictatus Papae_: the pope alone may be called universal, may depose emperors, and "can be judged by no one." He excommunicates Emperor Henry IV, who stands three days in the snow at **Canossa** (1077).
- **1095** — Urban II calls the **First Crusade** and promises the remission of the penance for sins to those who go: the beginning of **crusade indulgences**.
- **1123, 1139** — The First and Second Lateran Councils forbid priests to marry and declare clerical marriages invalid.
- **c. 1140** — Gratian's _Decretum_ founds **canon law**; the word **"transubstantiation"** appears in theology.
- **c. 1150** — Peter Lombard lists **seven sacraments**.
- **1184** — Council of Verona (_Ad abolendam_): bishops must search out heretics and hand them to the secular power — the beginning of the **inquisition**. The same council counts **marriage** among the sacraments.
- **1198–1216** — **Innocent III**, "Vicar of Christ," places whole kingdoms under **interdict** (France 1200, England 1208–1214) and makes King John of England his vassal (1213).

### The Fourth Lateran Council (1215)

Innocent III gathered more than 400 bishops and 800 abbots in Rome. The council opened on **November 11** and closed on **November 30, 1215** — less than three weeks. Its 71 canons were **drafted by Innocent III himself and were not debated**; they were simply read and approved on the last day. Among them:

- **Canon 1** defines **transubstantiation** as dogma: Christ's "body and blood are truly contained in the sacrament of the altar under the forms of bread and wine, the bread and wine having been transubstantiated" into His body and blood.
- **Canon 3** commands secular rulers to **exterminate heretics** from their lands, confiscates their property, and grants those who fight them the **same indulgence as crusaders** to the Holy Land.
- **Canon 21** (_Omnis utriusque sexus_) makes **confession to one's own priest at least once a year**, and communion at Easter, obligatory for every Catholic, under pain of being barred from the church and denied Christian burial.
- **Canons 67–70** are directed against the **Jews**: they limit Jewish moneylending (67); require Jews and Muslims to wear **distinctive clothing** so they can be told apart from Christians, and forbid them to appear in public during the last three days before Easter (68); bar Jews from **public office** (69); and prevent converted Jews from keeping any of their old customs (70). The distinctive dress led to the yellow badge and the pointed "Jewish hat" worn for centuries.

### The Host and the Jews

Once the bread was declared to be literally Christ's body, a new accusation arose: that Jews stole consecrated hosts to stab and torture them, re-enacting the crucifixion. These **host desecration** accusations were false, but they led to burnings and massacres:

- **1243** — Beelitz, near Berlin: the first recorded accusation.
- **1290** — Paris (the "miracle of the Rue des Billettes").
- **1298** — Röttingen: the accusation set off the **Rintfleisch massacres**, in which thousands of Jews in Franconia were killed.
- **1337–1338** — Deggendorf and Pulkau: massacres spread across Bavaria and Austria.
- **1370** — Brussels: Jews burned and the community expelled.
- **1510** — Knoblauch (Brandenburg): **38 Jews executed** and the rest expelled.

The accusations faded only after the Reformation, as fewer Christians believed the host was Christ's literal flesh.

### Worship of the Host

- **c. 1200** — The priest begins to **elevate the host** after consecration for the people to see and adore.
- **1219** — Pope Honorius III orders the people to **bow** at the elevation and when the host is carried to the sick (_Sane cum olim_).
- **1264** — Urban IV establishes the feast of **Corpus Christi**, with processions of the host, after a reported miracle at Bolsena.
- **1229** — The Council of Toulouse forbids lay people to own the Bible (see **Who gave us the Bible?**).
- **1231** — Gregory IX creates the **papal inquisition**. **1252** — Innocent IV authorizes **torture** (_Ad extirpanda_).
- **c. 1270** — Thomas Aquinas teaches that "it is necessary for salvation to be subject to the Roman pontiff" (_Against the Errors of the Greeks_ 2.38), the "treasury of merit," and that the image of Christ should receive _latria_ (see **Image veneration**).
- **1274** — Second Council of Lyons defines **purgatory**, the **seven sacraments** and the filioque.
- **1300** — Boniface VIII proclaims the first **Jubilee** with a plenary indulgence for pilgrims to Rome.
- **1302** — _Unam Sanctam_: "it is altogether necessary to salvation for every human creature to be subject to the Roman Pontiff."

# Decline, schism and reform (1300–1563)

- **1309–1377** — The popes live in **Avignon** under French influence.
- **1343** — Clement VI defines the **treasury of merit** from which indulgences are drawn (_Unigenitus_).
- **1378–1417** — The **Great Western Schism**: two, then three rival popes, each excommunicating the others.
- **1409** — The Council of Pisa deposes two popes and elects a third; none resigns.
- **1414–1418** — The Council of Constance deposes **John XXIII** (now counted an antipope), accused of crimes from simony to murder; declares that a council's authority comes directly from Christ and that even a pope must obey it (_Haec Sancta_); forbids the **cup** to the laity (1415); and burns **Jan Hus** (1415).
- **1431–1449** — Council of Basel again claims authority over the pope.
- **1431** — Joan of Arc is burned as a heretic by a church court; she was declared innocent in 1456 and made a saint in 1920.
- **1439** — Council of Florence defines **purgatory** and the **seven sacraments** (for the Armenians).
- **c. 1455** — Gutenberg prints the Bible — in Latin.
- **1478** — The **Spanish Inquisition** is founded (abolished 1834); Portugal 1536–1821; Rome 1542; Mexico 1571.
- **1492–1503** — Pope **Alexander VI** (Rodrigo Borgia) fathers several children, including Cesare and Lucrezia Borgia, and buys his election.
- **1517** — Martin Luther posts his **95 Theses** against the sale of indulgences.
- **1525–1527** — Persecution of believers' baptism was not only Catholic: Protestant Zurich ordered infants baptized and decreed **death by drowning** for rebaptizers; Felix Manz was drowned in 1527. The same year Catholic authorities burned the Anabaptist Michael Sattler.
- **1526** — Tyndale's English New Testament, translated from Greek, is burned; he is executed in **1536**.
- **1531** — **[One God]** The Spanish physician Michael Servetus publishes _On the Errors of the Trinity_, arguing from Scripture that God is one and that Jesus is "the Son of the eternal God," not an "eternal Son."
- **1531** — The reported appearance of the Virgin of Guadalupe at Tepeyac — a hill where the Aztecs had honoured the mother goddess Tonantzin. The Franciscan Bernardino de Sahagún complained that the natives were still worshipping Tonantzin there under Mary's name.
- **1545–1563** — The **Council of Trent**: declares the deuterocanonical books inspired (1546), sets tradition beside Scripture, makes the Vulgate "authentic," defines the Mass as a **propitiatory sacrifice** (1562), the seven sacraments, purgatory, indulgences, the veneration of images and relics (1563), and requires marriage before a priest (1563).
- **1548** — Ignatius of Loyola, founder of the Jesuits: "**What I see white, I will believe black if the hierarchical Church so defines it**" (_Spiritual Exercises_, Rules for Thinking with the Church 13).
- **1550–1551** — At Valladolid, the theologian Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda argues from Aristotle that the Indians are "natural slaves" who may be subjected by force; the Dominican Bartolomé de las Casas defends them.
- **1553** — **[One God]** Servetus publishes _The Restoration of Christianity_. The Catholic Inquisition at Vienne condemns him and burns him in effigy; he escapes, is arrested in Protestant Geneva at Calvin's instigation, and is burned alive on October 27, 1553, for denying the Trinity and infant baptism.
- **1559** — The **Index of Forbidden Books** (in force until 1966) includes Protestant Bibles.
- **1561** — Pius IV orders the Spanish Inquisition to prosecute priests who **solicit** penitents for sex during confession (_Cum sicut nuper_).
- **1565–1577** — **The confessional booth.** Until then confession was heard face to face, often with the penitent kneeling at the priest's knees. To stop the scandals of sexual solicitation, Cardinal Charles Borromeo, archbishop of Milan, required a wooden **confessional** with a grille separating priest and penitent (_Instructions on Church Building_, 1577). The _Roman Ritual_ of 1614 made it the rule for the whole church.
- **1569 / 1602** — Casiodoro de Reina's _Biblia del Oso_ (its cover bore the printer's mark of a bear and honey, and no translator's name) and Cipriano de Valera's revision; both men lived as fugitives from the Inquisition.

# Modern dogmas (1563–today)

- **1590** — Sixtus V publishes an "authentic" Vulgate under pain of excommunication; it is so full of errors that it is withdrawn after his death and replaced (1592).
- **1612** — **[One God]** Edward Wightman, who denied the Trinity, is burned at Lichfield — the last person burned for heresy in England.
- **1622** — Gregory XV extends the penalties for **solicitation in confession** to the whole church (_Universi Dominici gregis_); Benedict XIV renews them in 1741 (_Sacramentum Poenitentiae_).
- **1616, 1633** — Galileo is condemned for teaching that the earth moves around the sun.
- **1771** — **[One God]** Emanuel Swedenborg, in _The True Christian Religion_, teaches that God is one in person as well as in essence, and that the Lord Jesus Christ is that one God, the Father being in Him as the soul is in the body.
- **1854** — Pius IX defines the **Immaculate Conception** of Mary.
- **1864** — The **Syllabus of Errors** condemns religious liberty, Bible societies and the separation of church and state.
- **1870** — The First Vatican Council defines **papal infallibility** and universal jurisdiction. The same year Italy takes Rome and the Papal States end; the Lateran Treaty with Mussolini creates Vatican City (1929).
- **1908** — The decree _Ne temere_ declares marriages of Catholics invalid unless performed before a Catholic priest.
- **1913–1916** — **[One God]** At the Arroyo Seco camp meeting near Los Angeles (1913), R. E. McAlister preaches that the apostles always baptized in the name of Jesus Christ. In 1914 Frank Ewart and Glenn Cook are rebaptized in Jesus' name, and thousands of Pentecostals follow. In 1916 the Assemblies of God adopts a Trinitarian statement of faith, and about 156 Oneness ministers leave.
- **1917** — The reported appearances of Mary at Fátima.
- **1943** — Pius XII encourages translating the Bible from Hebrew and Greek (_Divino Afflante Spiritu_).
- **1945** — **[One God]** Two Oneness organizations merge to form the United Pentecostal Church.
- **1950** — Pius XII defines the **Assumption** of Mary into heaven, body and soul. The apocryphal _Transitus Mariae_ ("The Passing of Mary") had been listed among rejected books in the _Gelasian Decree_.
- **1954** — Pius XII proclaims Mary "**Queen of Heaven**" (_Ad Caeli Reginam_).
- **1962–1965** — The Second Vatican Council allows the Mass in the people's own languages.
- **1966** — The Index of Forbidden Books is abolished.

# Popes contradicting popes

- **Liberius** (358) signed a creed that abandoned Nicaea, under pressure in exile.
- **Zosimus** (417) declared Pelagius orthodox, then condemned him.
- **Vigilius** (553) refused to condemn the "Three Chapters," then condemned them.
- **Honorius I** (d. 638) was condemned as a heretic by the Third Council of Constantinople (681), and the condemnation was confirmed by Pope Leo II.
- **Gregory the Great** (c. 600) called the title "universal bishop" the mark of Antichrist; **Gregory VII** (1075) claimed it for the pope alone.
- **Councils of Constance and Basel** declared councils above the pope; **Vatican I** declared the pope above them.
- **Sixtus V** declared his Vulgate final under pain of excommunication; **Clement VIII** replaced it two years later.
- **Paul V** and **Urban VIII** condemned Galileo; **John Paul II** acknowledged the error (1992).

# Relics and superstitions

- John Calvin observed that if all the pieces of the "true cross" in Europe's churches were gathered together, "**they would form a whole ship's cargo**" (_Treatise on Relics_, 1543).
- Indulgences were attached to prayers, relics and pilgrimages, counted in "days" and "years" of reduced purgatory, until Paul VI abolished the counting of days in 1967.
- The **scapular** promise (that whoever dies wearing the Carmelite scapular will not suffer eternal fire) rests on a legendary vision of Simon Stock (1251), recorded only a century and a half later; the related "Sabbatine privilege" rests on a bull attributed to John XXII (1322) that historians consider spurious.
- The **"Chair of Peter"** in St. Peter's Basilica was examined in 1968–1974 and dated to the ninth century; it is generally identified as a throne given by Charles the Bald (875).
- The Church distinguishes _latria_ (worship of God), _dulia_ (veneration of saints) and _hyperdulia_ (veneration of Mary). Scripture knows no such distinction; it forbids bowing to images altogether (Exodus 20:5).

# Conclusion

None of this was in the church of the apostles. The Mass as a sacrifice, transubstantiation, confession to a priest, purgatory, indulgences, seven sacraments, prayers to saints, relics, images, the papacy and its infallibility each entered at a known point in history, usually through a council or a pope, often by force, and sometimes with blood — including the blood of Jews accused of torturing a wafer. The apostles left us something else: the Word of God, written by eyewitnesses who did not use the name of Jesus to rule nations, enrich themselves, burn heretics or justify slavery, but who gave their lives to tell what they had seen.

> **Ephesians 2:19-20:** "Now therefore ye are no more strangers and foreigners, but fellowcitizens with the saints, and of the household of God; And are built upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Jesus Christ himself being the chief corner stone;"

> **Hebrews 10:10-12:** "By the which will we are sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all. And every priest standeth daily ministering and offering oftentimes the same sacrifices, which can never take away sins: But this man, after he had offered one sacrifice for sins for ever, sat down on the right hand of God;"

> **1 Timothy 2:5:** "For there is one God, and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus;"
