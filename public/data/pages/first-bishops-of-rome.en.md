## The claim

The Catholic Church publishes a list of popes that runs without a break from Peter to the present day, and it teaches that the bishops of Rome have always held "full, supreme, and universal power over the whole Church" (_Catechism of the Catholic Church_ 882) and have always taught the same faith, including the Trinity.

This page tests both claims against the oldest evidence we have, for the first sixteen names on the list — from Peter to Callistus (died A.D. 222). In short:

- **They were not popes.** For most of the second century the church at Rome was led by a group of elders, not by one bishop with authority over the other churches. When a bishop of Rome did try to impose his will on other churches, other bishops refused and rebuked him.
- **Nothing shows that any of them was a Trinitarian.** For most of them we have no writings at all. The writings that survive from the church of Rome in that period teach one God and His Son, Jesus Christ, without any doctrine of three persons, and baptize "in the name of the Lord."
- **When we finally learn what the bishops of Rome believed, they were monarchians** — believers in the "monarchy," the oneness of God. The sixteenth bishop on the list, **Callistus**, taught that the Father is the Spirit who dwelt in the Son, and that the Son is the man in whom He dwelt. The man who attacked him for it, **Hippolytus**, defended a plurality of divine persons, set himself up as a rival bishop of Rome, and is remembered by the Catholic Church as **the first antipope**.
- **Tertullian admits that the majority of believers held the same faith** and were "startled" by his teaching of three.
- **At Nicaea (325), the party that insisted on _homoousios_ was a minority**, and several of the leading defenders of Nicaea were later condemned as "Sabellians" — the label used for believers in one God who is Father, Son and Spirit.

A word about method. For many of these men no writings survive, and we will say so plainly. But silence works both ways: if they had taught three co-equal persons, it would be strange that not one word of it survived, while the writings that did survive from their church do not teach it.

Bible quotations are from the King James Version. The Fathers are quoted from the _Ante-Nicene Fathers_ and _Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers_ translations.

# 1. Were they popes?

### Peter in the New Testament

The New Testament never says Peter was bishop of Rome, or even that he went there. Paul wrote his letter to the Romans around A.D. 57 and greeted by name more than twenty-five people in Rome (Romans 16) — but not Peter. When Paul arrived in Rome as a prisoner (Acts 28), the believers came out to meet him; Peter is not mentioned. Paul says Peter's mission was to the Jews ("the circumcision"), and his own to the Gentiles:

> **Galatians 2:7-9:** "But contrariwise, when they saw that the gospel of the uncircumcision was committed unto me, as the gospel of the circumcision was unto Peter; (For he that wrought effectually in Peter to the apostleship of the circumcision, the same was mighty in me toward the Gentiles:) And when James, Cephas, and John, who seemed to be pillars, perceived the grace that was given unto me, they gave to me and Barnabas the right hands of fellowship; that we should go unto the heathen, and they unto the circumcision."

Peter called himself not the head of the church but "also an elder":

> **1 Peter 5:1-4:** "The elders which are among you I exhort, who am also an elder, and a witness of the sufferings of Christ, and also a partaker of the glory that shall be revealed: Feed the flock of God which is among you, taking the oversight thereof, not by constraint, but willingly; not for filthy lucre, but of a ready mind; Neither as being lords over God’s heritage, but being ensamples to the flock. And when the chief Shepherd shall appear, ye shall receive a crown of glory that fadeth not away."

Early tradition does say that Peter and Paul were martyred in Rome (_1 Clement_ 5; Ignatius, _Romans_ 4). But being martyred in a city is not the same as being its bishop, much less the bishop of the whole church.

### Rome was led by elders

The earliest documents from and about the church of Rome show **no single bishop** in charge:

- **1 Clement** (c. A.D. 96), the letter later attributed to Clement, does not carry his name. It is sent by "the church of God which sojourns at Rome," and it uses "bishops" and "presbyters" (elders) for the same office (chapters 42–44).
- **Ignatius of Antioch** (c. 110) wrote seven letters on his way to martyrdom. In every letter to a church in Asia he speaks of its bishop and urges obedience to him — but his letter **to the Romans** mentions no bishop at all.
- **The Shepherd of Hermas** (Rome, c. 140) tells Hermas to read his book "in this city with **the elders who preside over the church**" (_Vision_ 2.4.3) — elders, plural.

Many historians, including Catholic scholars such as Francis A. Sullivan, S.J. (_From Apostles to Bishops_, 2001), and Raymond Brown and John Meier (_Antioch and Rome_, 1983), conclude that Rome was led by a college of presbyters until about the middle of the second century, and that a single bishop over the Roman church appears only after that. The early names on the list were real leaders of the Roman church, but they were not "popes."

### The lists themselves disagree

The first list of Roman bishops comes from Irenaeus (c. 180): Linus, Anacletus, Clement, Evaristus, Alexander, Sixtus, Telesphorus, Hyginus, Pius, Anicetus, Soter and Eleutherus (_Against Heresies_ 3.3.3). But Tertullian says Clement was ordained by Peter himself (_Prescription against Heretics_ 32), and other ancient lists put Clement second or change the order. A succession whose first links cannot be agreed upon is not proof of an office.

### When Rome tried to rule, the churches refused

- **Anicetus and Polycarp (c. 155).** Polycarp of Smyrna came to Rome to discuss the date of Easter with Anicetus. Neither persuaded the other; each kept the custom of his own church, and they parted in peace (Irenaeus, quoted by Eusebius, _Church History_ 5.24.16-17). Polycarp did not submit to Rome.
- **Victor (c. 190).** When Victor tried to cut off the churches of Asia from communion over the same question, many bishops opposed him, and "some of them sharply rebuked Victor." Irenaeus himself wrote to him and urged him not to cut off whole churches of God (Eusebius, _Church History_ 5.24.9-11).

### The title "pope"

_Papa_ ("father") was an affectionate title of honor given to many bishops, not a claim to rule the Church. Some examples:

- **Heraclas of Alexandria** (bishop c. 232-248): his successor Dionysius calls him "our blessed pope (_papas_) Heraclas" (Eusebius, _Church History_ 7.7.4). This is the earliest known use of the title for a bishop, and it is for a bishop of Alexandria, not of Rome.
- **Cyprian of Carthage** (c. 250): the clergy **of Rome itself**, writing while their own see was vacant, addressed him as "Cyprian, pope" (_Cypriano papae_; Cyprian, _Letters_ 30, 31 and 36).
- **Augustine of Hippo** (c. 400): Jerome opened his letters to him "To my lord truly holy and most blessed pope Augustine" (_Domino vere sancto et beatissimo papae Augustino_, Jerome, _Letters_ 105 and 112). Hippo was a small town in North Africa.
- **The bishops of Gaul** (400s): Sidonius Apollinaris addressed his letters to fellow bishops such as Lupus of Troyes as "the lord pope" (_domino papae_).
- **Today**: the head of the Coptic Orthodox Church is still called "Pope of Alexandria," and so is the Greek Orthodox Patriarch of Alexandria.

Only in the eleventh century did Gregory VII declare that the name belongs to the bishop of Rome alone: "that this name is unique in the world" (_Dictatus Papae_ 11, 1075).

# 2. The first sixteen names, one by one

### 1. Peter

Peter baptized in the name of Jesus Christ, and preached that there is no other name under heaven by which we must be saved:

> **Acts 2:38:** "Then Peter said unto them, Repent, and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the remission of sins, and ye shall receive the gift of the Holy Ghost."

> **Acts 10:48:** "And he commanded them to be baptized in the name of the Lord. Then prayed they him to tarry certain days."

> **Acts 4:10-12:** "Be it known unto you all, and to all the people of Israel, that by the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, whom ye crucified, whom God raised from the dead, even by him doth this man stand here before you whole. This is the stone which was set at nought of you builders, which is become the head of the corner. Neither is there salvation in any other: for there is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved."

Peter heard Jesus command baptism in the one **name** of the Father, the Son and the Holy Ghost (Matthew 28:19), and he obeyed it by baptizing in the name of Jesus. As a Jew he confessed the Shema: "The LORD our God is one LORD" (Deuteronomy 6:4).

In his two letters there is no Trinity, no "God the Son," no "eternal Son," no "three persons." He speaks of **God** and of **the Lord Jesus Christ** the way Paul does — of the one God, and of the Son through whom He saves us — and he calls the Spirit in the prophets "the Spirit of Christ":

> **1 Peter 1:3:** "Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, which according to his abundant mercy hath begotten us again unto a lively hope by the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead,"

> **1 Peter 1:11:** "Searching what, or what manner of time the Spirit of Christ which was in them did signify, when it testified beforehand the sufferings of Christ, and the glory that should follow."

> **1 Peter 1:20:** "Who verily was foreordained before the foundation of the world, but was manifest in these last times for you,"

> **1 Peter 2:5:** "Ye also, as lively stones, are built up a spiritual house, an holy priesthood, to offer up spiritual sacrifices, acceptable to God by Jesus Christ."

> **1 Peter 3:18:** "For Christ also hath once suffered for sins, the just for the unjust, that he might bring us to God, being put to death in the flesh, but quickened by the Spirit:"

> **2 Peter 1:1-2:** "Simon Peter, a servant and an apostle of Jesus Christ, to them that have obtained like precious faith with us through the righteousness of God and our Saviour Jesus Christ: Grace and peace be multiplied unto you through the knowledge of God, and of Jesus our Lord,"

Notice 1 Peter 1:20: the Son was "foreordained before the foundation of the world," and "manifest in these last times."

### 2. Linus

No writing of Linus survives. Paul mentions him among the believers in Rome who send greetings to Timothy, and Irenaeus says he is the Linus of that verse:

> **2 Timothy 4:21:** "Do thy diligence to come before winter. Eubulus greeteth thee, and Pudens, and Linus, and Claudia, and all the brethren."

Linus was one of Paul's circle. Paul taught that all the fullness of the Godhead dwells bodily in Jesus, baptized in the name of the Lord Jesus — and rebaptized those who had not been (Acts 19:5) — and opened every letter with grace and peace "from God our Father, and the Lord Jesus Christ," without ever naming a third person:

> **Colossians 2:9-10:** "For in him dwelleth all the fulness of the Godhead bodily. And ye are complete in him, which is the head of all principality and power:"

> **Acts 19:5:** "When they heard this, they were baptized in the name of the Lord Jesus."

> **Romans 1:7:** "To all that be in Rome, beloved of God, called to be saints: Grace to you and peace from God our Father, and the Lord Jesus Christ."

> **1 Corinthians 1:3:** "Grace be unto you, and peace, from God our Father, and from the Lord Jesus Christ."

> **2 Corinthians 1:2:** "Grace be to you and peace from God our Father, and from the Lord Jesus Christ."

> **Galatians 1:3:** "Grace be to you and peace from God the Father, and from our Lord Jesus Christ,"

> **Ephesians 1:2:** "Grace be to you, and peace, from God our Father, and from the Lord Jesus Christ."

> **Philippians 1:2:** "Grace be unto you, and peace, from God our Father, and from the Lord Jesus Christ."

The same greeting appears in Colossians 1:2, 1 Thessalonians 1:1, 2 Thessalonians 1:2 and Philemon 3.

### 3. Anacletus (Anencletus)

Nothing he wrote or said survives. Nothing at all is recorded of his theology.

### 4. Clement

Eusebius identifies him with the Clement whom Paul calls his fellow worker (_Church History_ 3.4.9):

> **Philippians 4:3:** "And I intreat thee also, true yokefellow, help those women which laboured with me in the gospel, with Clement also, and with other my fellowlabourers, whose names are in the book of life."

The letter known as _1 Clement_ (c. 96) is the earliest Christian writing outside the New Testament, and some churches read it as Scripture. It speaks of God and His Son as Paul does:

> "Grace to you, and peace, **from Almighty God through Jesus Christ**, be multiplied." (_1 Clement_, greeting)

> "The Apostles received the Gospel for us from the Lord Jesus Christ; Jesus Christ was sent forth from God. So then Christ is from God, and the Apostles are from Christ. Both therefore came of the will of God **in the appointed order**." (42:1-2)

> "Let all the Gentiles know that **Thou art God alone**, and Jesus Christ is Thy Son, and we are Thy people and the sheep of Thy pasture." (59:4)

To be fair, Clement also names Father, Son and Spirit together: "Have we not one God, and one Christ, and one Spirit of grace that was poured out upon us?" (46:6), and he swears "as God liveth, and the Lord Jesus Christ liveth, and the Holy Spirit" (58:2). So does the Bible, and so does every Oneness believer. What Clement never says is that they are three persons, co-equal and co-eternal, in one being.

### 5–9. Evaristus, Alexander I, Sixtus I, Telesphorus, Hyginus (c. 100–140)

No writing of any of these five survives, and no ancient author records anything they taught about God. Irenaeus notes only that Telesphorus "was gloriously martyred" (_Against Heresies_ 3.3.3), and that the Gnostic teachers Valentinus and Cerdo came to Rome under Hyginus (3.4.3). They led the Roman church **before** Justin Martyr, the first writer to call the Son "another God" in "the second place," began teaching in Rome around 150 (see **Development of the Trinity**).

### 10. Pius I (c. 140–155) and his brother Hermas

Three ancient sources say that Hermas, the author of _The Shepherd of Hermas_, was the brother of Pius. The oldest is the Muratorian Fragment (late second century):

> "Hermas wrote the Shepherd very recently, in our times, in the city of Rome, while bishop Pius, **his brother**, was occupying the chair of the church of the city of Rome."

The Liberian Catalogue (A.D. 354) and the Pseudo-Tertullian poem _Against Marcion_ say the same. _The Shepherd_ was one of the most widely read books in the early church and was copied in Codex Sinaiticus right after the New Testament. Its theology is not Trinitarian, and modern Trinitarian editors often warn their readers that its Christology "differs" from later orthodoxy:

> "The holy, pre-existent Spirit, that created every creature, **God made to dwell in flesh, which He chose**. This flesh, accordingly, in which the Holy Spirit dwelt, was nobly subject to that Spirit, walking religiously and chastely, in no respect defiling the Spirit." (_Similitude_ 5.6.5)

> "That Holy Spirit which spake with thee in the form of the Church… **that Spirit is the Son of God**." (_Similitude_ 9.1.1)

And Hermas speaks of those who "wish to be baptized **into the name of the Lord**" (_Vision_ 3.7.3).

For Hermas, God is the Spirit who dwelt in the flesh of His Son, and the Holy Spirit is not a third person beside the Son. This was written in Rome, by the bishop's brother, while his brother was bishop.

Meanwhile, **Justin Martyr** was teaching his Logos philosophy in Rome, in a private school "above the baths of Myrtinus" (_Martyrdom of Justin_ 3). He was a lay teacher, not a bishop of Rome.

### 11. Anicetus (c. 155–166)

No writing of Anicetus survives. His best-known act is receiving **Polycarp**, bishop of Smyrna and, according to Irenaeus, a disciple of the apostle John. They disagreed about Easter and remained in communion. Polycarp's one surviving letter, _To the Philippians_, speaks as Paul and Clement do:

> "…mercy and peace **from God Almighty, and from the Lord Jesus Christ**, our Saviour, be multiplied." (greeting)

> "…by grace ye are saved, not of works, but **by the will of God through Jesus Christ**." (1)

> "But may **the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, and Jesus Christ Himself, who is the Son of God**, and our everlasting High Priest, build you up in faith and truth." (12)

Polycarp calls Jesus "the Son of God," never "God the Son."

**Polycarp did not write the account of his death.** _The Martyrdom of Polycarp_ is a letter from "the church of God which sojourneth at Smyrna" to the church at Philomelium, written after he was burned (c. 156) and attributed to a certain Marcion (or Marcianus, ch. 20). Its last chapter says it was copied by Gaius from the papers of Irenaeus, then by Socrates in Corinth, and finally by Pionius (ch. 22), a fourth-century copyist; some scholars, such as Candida Moss (2010), date the account as we have it to the third century. In it, Polycarp's last prayer ends:

> "For this cause, yea and for all things, I praise Thee, I bless Thee, I glorify Thee, through the eternal and heavenly High-priest, Jesus Christ, Thy beloved Son, through whom with Him and the Holy Spirit be glory both now and ever and for the ages to come. Amen."
> — _Martyrdom of Polycarp_ 14.3 (Lightfoot's translation)

Three things should be noticed:

1. **It is not Polycarp's own writing.** It is the church of Smyrna's report of what he said, passed through several copyists.
2. **The wording is not secure.** The oldest witness to this prayer, Eusebius (_Church History_ 4.15.35, early 300s), reads "through whom to Thee, with Him, **in** the Holy Spirit, be glory" (_en pneumati hagiō_): glory given to God, through Christ, in the Spirit, not to three co-equal persons. The manuscripts' "**and** the Holy Spirit" (_kai pneumati hagiō_) looks like a later adjustment, and scholars such as Hans von Campenhausen (1957) argued that this and other passages of the Martyrdom were revised and expanded after the original letter.
3. **Polycarp's own letter has no such formula.** In _To the Philippians_, which he did write, he speaks as Paul does, of "God Almighty" and "the Lord Jesus Christ," "the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, and Jesus Christ Himself, who is the Son of God."

**The doxology at the very end.** The most Trinitarian-sounding line of the whole document is the last one:

> "That the Lord Jesus Christ may also gather me along with His elect into His heavenly kingdom, to whom, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, be glory for ever and ever. Amen."
> — _Martyrdom of Polycarp_ 22 (_Ante-Nicene Fathers_, vol. 1)

But these are not Polycarp's words, nor even the church of Smyrna's. They are the closing prayer of **Pionius**, the last copyist, who adds that Polycarp revealed the old copy to him in a vision. The _Ante-Nicene Fathers_ edition itself prints a note before this postscript: "What follows is, of course, no part of the original Epistle." Chapters 21-22 (a date note, a farewell, and the copyists' notes) are generally treated as appendices added after the letter, and the farewell's "with whom be glory to God the Father and the Holy Spirit" (22.1) belongs to the same additions.

Even if such a line were from the second century, it would not be the Trinity of the creeds:

- **Naming Father, Son and Spirit together is not the doctrine of the Trinity.** Scripture does it too: "in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost" (Matthew 28:19); "The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, and the love of God, and the communion of the Holy Ghost" (2 Corinthians 13:14).
- **The idea of three co-equal persons did not exist yet.** Polycarp died about 156, in the same years Justin Martyr was writing, and Justin placed the Son "in the second place" and the prophetic Spirit "in the third" (_1 Apology_ 13), calling the Son "another God," numerically distinct from and under the Father (_Dialogue with Trypho_ 56). The Greek word for "trinity" (_trias_) first appears about 180, in Theophilus of Antioch, for "God, His Word and His Wisdom" (_To Autolycus_ 2.15); the Latin _trinitas_ and "three persons, one substance" come from Tertullian about 213 (_Against Praxeas_), after he left the mainstream Roman church. The full equality of the Holy Spirit was not defined until the Council of Constantinople in 381.
- So a fourth-century copyist's prayer cannot tell us what Polycarp believed. Polycarp's own letter can, and it speaks of God the Father and His Son Jesus Christ.

And John, the apostle Polycarp learned from, wrote:

> **John 14:8-9:** "Philip saith unto him, Lord, shew us the Father, and it sufficeth us. Jesus saith unto him, Have I been so long time with you, and yet hast thou not known me, Philip? he that hath seen me hath seen the Father; and how sayest thou then, Shew us the Father?"

### 12. Soter (c. 166–174)

No doctrinal writing survives. Dionysius of Corinth thanked the Roman church for its generosity in Soter's day and mentions a letter from Rome that was read in Corinth (Eusebius, _Church History_ 4.23.9-11) — a letter about charity, not doctrine.

### 13. Eleutherus (c. 174–189)

No doctrinal writing survives. The martyrs of Lyon wrote to him about the Montanist prophets (Eusebius, _Church History_ 5.3.4). Tertullian says that a bishop of Rome, whom he does not name (it may have been Eleutherus or Victor), was ready to recognize those prophets until **Praxeas** arrived (see Victor below).

### 14. Victor I (c. 189–199)

Victor is the first bishop of Rome who clearly acts as a single ruling bishop, and the first remembered as writing in Latin. Three things are known about him:

- **He excommunicated Theodotus the tanner** (Eusebius, _Church History_ 5.28.6), who taught that Jesus was a mere man on whom the Spirit came at His baptism. That is the opposite error from Oneness: Theodotus denied that God Himself was in Christ from His conception. So Victor defended the deity of Christ.
- **Praxeas came to Rome** in his time or just before. Tertullian, writing against him around A.D. 213, says Praxeas persuaded the bishop of Rome to withdraw his recognition of the Montanist prophets:

> "By this Praxeas did a twofold service for the devil at Rome: he drove away prophecy, and he brought in heresy; he put to flight the Paraclete, and **he crucified the Father**." (_Against Praxeas_ 1)

"Crucified the Father" is Tertullian's mocking summary of what Praxeas taught: that the one God, the Father, was in Christ who suffered. The bishop of Rome listened to Praxeas, not to Tertullian's side.

### 15. Zephyrinus (c. 199–217)

No writing of Zephyrinus survives. We know his faith only from his enemy Hippolytus, who calls him "an ignorant and illiterate individual, and one unskilled in ecclesiastical definitions," and says he took bribes (_Refutation of All Heresies_ 9.6). But Hippolytus records what Zephyrinus confessed in public:

> "**I know that there is one God, Jesus Christ; nor except Him do I know any other** that is begotten and amenable to suffering." (_Refutation_ 9.6)

And on another occasion:

> "**The Father did not die, but the Son.**" (_Refutation_ 9.6)

Both statements fit the Oneness faith exactly: there is one God, and He is Jesus Christ; and it was the Son — the humanity — that died, not the eternal Spirit. That was the public confession of the bishop of Rome around the year 200.

### 16. Callistus I (c. 217–222)

Callistus was Zephyrinus's deacon and his successor. Hippolytus despised him, and tells his whole story: he had been a slave of a Christian named Carpophorus, managed a bank for him and lost the money, and was sent to the mines of Sardinia before he was released and became a leader of the church (_Refutation_ 9.7). Hippolytus also reports that Callistus **excommunicated Sabellius** "as not entertaining orthodox opinions" (Sabellius was probably teaching Modalism instead of Oneness, or perhaps something else that disagreed with Callistus' theology even if they both agreed on the Monarchy of God), while keeping his own teaching (9.7). And here is that teaching, as Hippolytus himself describes it:

> "Callistus alleges that the Logos Himself is Son, and that Himself is Father; and that though denominated by a different title, yet that in reality He is one indivisible spirit. And he maintains that the Father is not one person and the Son another, but that they are one and the same; and that all things are full of the Divine Spirit, both those above and those below. And he affirms that **the Spirit, which became incarnate in the virgin, is not different from the Father, but one and the same**. And he adds, that this is what has been declared by the Saviour: 'Do you not believe that I am in the Father, and the Father in me?' **For that which is seen, which is man, he considers to be the Son; whereas the Spirit, which was contained in the Son, to be the Father.** For, says (Callistus), **I will not profess belief in two Gods, Father and Son, but in one.** For the Father, who subsisted in the Son Himself, after He had taken unto Himself our flesh, raised it to the nature of Deity, by bringing it into union with Himself, and made it one; so that Father and Son must be styled one God, and that this Person being one, cannot be two. And in this way Callistus contends that the Father suffered along with the Son; for he does not wish to assert that the Father suffered, and is one Person, being careful to avoid blasphemy against the Father. (How careful he is!) senseless and knavish fellow, who improvises blasphemies in every direction, only that he may not seem to speak in violation of the truth, and is not abashed at being at one time betrayed into the tenet of Sabellius, whereas at another into the doctrine of Theodotus." (_Refutation of All Heresies_ 9.7; 9.12 in the Greek numbering)

Notice what Callistus taught, even through the words of an enemy:

- God is **one indivisible Spirit**; "Father" and "Son" are different titles, not different persons.
- **The Son is the man who was seen; the Father is the Spirit who dwelt in the Son.** That is exactly how Oneness believers explain John 14:10 and Colossians 2:9 today.
- **"I will not profess belief in two Gods, Father and Son, but in one."**
- The Father did not suffer as the Father; He **"suffered along with"** the Son, in the flesh He had taken. Hippolytus mocks this careful distinction ("How careful he is!") because it does not fit his caricature.
- Callistus himself rejected Sabellius. Callistus' teaching was not what his enemies called "Sabellianism"; Oneness believers today are often misrepresented the same way and called "Modalists," as if they denied the Son.

And Callistus answered his critics with a charge of his own. Hippolytus complains that Callistus said to him and his followers publicly, "**You are Ditheists**" — worshippers of two gods (9.7). In Rome around A.D. 220, it was the bishop who accused the believer in two divine persons of preaching two gods.

Hippolytus adds that the followers of Callistus formed a school "which continues, preserving its customs and tradition," and that they should be called "Callistians" (9.7) — a large community, still alive when he wrote.

# 3. Hippolytus, the first antipope

Hippolytus was a presbyter in Rome. He did not accept Callistus as bishop and led a rival congregation: the Catholic Church counts him as **the first antipope**. He was the one who defended a distinction of persons — the "Logos" theology of Justin and Tertullian. Yet even his own view was not the later doctrine of the Trinity: he taught that the Word was called "Son" because He would become Son at the incarnation (_Against Noetus_ 15), and that the Word was begotten when the Father willed.

So in Rome around A.D. 220:

|                          | Callistus (the bishop of Rome)               | Hippolytus (the rival bishop)                       |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Catholic status today    | Pope and saint                               | The first antipope (later reconciled; also a saint) |
| God                      | "One indivisible Spirit"                     | God and His Word, distinct persons                  |
| The Son                  | "That which is seen, which is man"           | The Word, called Son because He would become Son    |
| The Father               | "The Spirit, which was contained in the Son" | Distinct from the Son                               |
| Charge against the other | "You are Ditheists"                          | "Sabellian," "Theodotian," "knavish fellow"         |

The man on the official list of popes taught one God dwelling in the man Christ Jesus. The man who taught distinct divine persons is on the list of antipopes. (Hippolytus was exiled to Sardinia in 235 together with Pope Pontian, was reconciled there, and both died in the mines.)

# 4. Tertullian: the majority held to one God

Tertullian of Carthage wrote _Against Praxeas_ around A.D. 213, after he had joined the Montanist movement and stood outside the mainstream church. He invented the vocabulary of the Trinity — _trinitas_, _persona_ — and he admits that most believers rejected it:

> "The simple, indeed (I will not call them unwise and unlearned), **who always constitute the majority of believers**, are startled at the dispensation [of the Three in One], on the ground that their very rule of faith withdraws them from the world's plurality of gods to the one only true God… They are constantly throwing out against us that we are preachers of **two gods and three gods**… '**We,**' say they, '**maintain the Monarchy**.'" (_Against Praxeas_ 3)

Put Tertullian and Hippolytus side by side: in Carthage and in Rome, around A.D. 200–220, the **majority of believers** and the **bishops of Rome** confessed the Monarchy — one God — and accused the new Logos theologians of preaching two or three gods.

# 5. What happened next

After Callistus, the Logos theology gradually won in Rome. Around 250 the Roman presbyter **Novatian** wrote the first Latin book called _On the Trinity_ — but he too taught that the Son is subject to the Father and was born "when the Father willed" (see **Development of the Trinity**).

### Nicaea (325)

Between 250 and 318 bishops met at Nicaea under Emperor Constantine. They were not divided between modern Trinitarians and Arians:

- Only a handful openly supported Arius, and in the end only two bishops refused to sign.
- The small group that insisted on the word **_homoousios_** ("of one substance") — Alexander of Alexandria and his deacon Athanasius, Eustathius of Antioch, Marcellus of Ancyra, Hosius of Córdoba — was **a minority**.
- **The majority** were followers of the Origenist "middle" view, like Eusebius of Caesarea, who believed in a Son subordinate to the Father. Many disliked _homoousios_ precisely because it sounded like **Sabellianism**, and because the Council of Antioch (268) had rejected it. Eusebius explained to his church in a letter that he signed only after the term was explained to him in an acceptable sense (Socrates, _Church History_ 1.8).
- The word was adopted because Constantine wanted it. The historian R. P. C. Hanson, in _The Search for the Christian Doctrine of God_ (1988), describes the fourth century not as the defense of an established orthodoxy but as a long search for one.

So those who signed for _homoousios_ did it above all to protect **the deity of Christ** against Arius — and several of them held a view of God much closer to the Monarchy than to the later three-person doctrine.

### The defenders of Nicaea condemned as "Sabellians"

- **Eustathius of Antioch**, one of the leaders at Nicaea, was deposed around 327–330; among the charges was Sabellianism (Socrates, _Church History_ 1.24).
- **Marcellus of Ancyra**, another champion of Nicaea, taught that God is one person (_prosopon_), that the Word is God's own Word, and that the Son's distinct reign will end when He delivers the kingdom to the Father (1 Corinthians 15:28). He was deposed in 336 as a Sabellian. But **Julius, bishop of Rome**, received him into communion and declared him orthodox at a council in Rome (340–341), and the Western bishops at Serdica (343) acquitted him and confessed **"one hypostasis"** of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit (Theodoret, _Church History_ 2.6). Decades later Basil of Caesarea begged Athanasius to condemn Marcellus (Basil, _Letter_ 69), and the Council of Constantinople (381) anathematized "the Sabellians, the Marcellians and the Photinians" (canon 1).
- **Photinus of Sirmium**, Marcellus' disciple, was condemned in 344–351.

In other words, the bishop of Rome in 340 approved a man whom the "ecumenical" council of 381 would anathematize as a heretic.

### The State finishes the job

- Constantine ordered the books of Arius to be burned and decreed death for anyone caught hiding them (Socrates, _Church History_ 1.9). The same method would later be used against every losing party.
- In **380** the Edict of Thessalonica required all subjects to believe in "the one deity of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit… in a holy Trinity," branding everyone else "foolish madmen" to be punished (_Theodosian Code_ 16.1.2).
- In **381** Constantinople condemned the "Sabellians."
- In **385** Priscillian, accused among other things of confusing the Father, Son and Spirit, was executed at Trier.
- In **388** Theodosius forbade anyone "to go out to the public and to argue about religion or to discuss it" (_Theodosian Code_ 16.4.2).

None of the writings of Praxeas, Zephyrinus or Callistus survive. We know their faith only from the men who condemned them — and even from those hostile pages, their faith is clear.

# Conclusion

The first names on the list of popes were not popes: Rome was led by elders, Peter called himself "also an elder," and when a bishop of Rome tried to rule other churches he was rebuked. Nor is there any sign that they were Trinitarians: the writings that survive from their church speak of one God and His Son, baptize in the name of the Lord, and — in the words of the bishop's own brother — say that God made the holy Spirit "to dwell in flesh, which He chose." When we finally hear the bishops of Rome speak, their confession is: "I know that there is one God, Jesus Christ"; "the Son is that which is seen, which is man, and the Spirit contained in the Son is the Father"; "I will not profess belief in two Gods, Father and Son, but in one." Their accuser became the first antipope. Tertullian admitted that the majority of believers held the same faith. And when the doctrine of three persons was finally imposed, it took an emperor, a series of councils, the burning of books and the condemnation of Nicaea's own defenders to do it.

"Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God is one LORD." (Deuteronomy 6:4)
