Every Bible in this app is either in the public domain or licensed for free, non-commercial use. Below is a short history of each one: where it came from, what text it was translated from, and what to keep in mind when you read it side by side with the others.

A quick word on the two families of Greek New Testament texts you will see mentioned:

- **Textus Receptus (TR)** — the "Received Text," the printed Greek New Testament of Erasmus (1516) and its later editions by Stephanus and Beza. It stands behind the King James Version and the Reina-Valera.
- **Majority Text (MT)** — the reading found in the majority of surviving Greek manuscripts. It is very close to the TR.
- **Critical Text (CT)** — the modern scholarly text (Nestle-Aland / UBS) that gives more weight to a few older manuscripts. Most modern Bibles, Protestant and Catholic, follow it.

The letters in parentheses after each Bible's name in the pane menu — (TR), (MT), (CT) — tell you which family it follows.

# English

## King James Version (KJV) — with Strong's numbers

No English Bible has survived the test of time like the King James Version. More than four centuries after it first appeared, it is still one of the most printed, most memorized and most trusted translations in the world, and its phrases ("the powers that be," "a labour of love," "the salt of the earth") have shaped the English language itself.

**How it was made.** In January 1604 King James I called the Hampton Court Conference to settle disputes between the bishops of the Church of England and the Puritans. The Puritans asked for a new translation, and James agreed — partly because he despised the popular Geneva Bible, whose marginal notes he considered seditious (some notes approved of disobeying kings). About 47 scholars, organized in six companies at Westminster, Oxford and Cambridge, worked from the Hebrew Masoretic Text and the Greek Textus Receptus, revising the earlier English Bibles going back to William Tyndale rather than starting from nothing. The result was printed in 1611.

**"Church," not "congregation."** The translators did not work freely on every word. Archbishop Richard Bancroft gave them fifteen rules on the king's behalf, and rule 3 reads: *"The old ecclesiastical words to be kept, viz. the word church not to be translated congregation."* Tyndale had rendered the Greek *ekklēsia* — literally "assembly," "those called out" — as "congregation," which pictured the church as a gathered body of believers. "Church" pointed instead to the established institution with its bishops. That mattered to James, who had told the Puritans at Hampton Court, "No bishop, no king": an episcopal church under the crown supported royal authority. You can see the choice inside the KJV itself — in Acts 19:32, 39 and 41 the very same word *ekklēsia* describes the mob in the theatre at Ephesus, and there the translators wrote "assembly."

**The "He" and "She" Bibles.** The first printings of 1611 differed in Ruth 3:15: one reads "*he* went into the city," the other "*she* went into the city." Collectors still call them the "He Bible" and the "She Bible." (The Hebrew text itself has "he"; modern KJV printings read "she.") It is a good example of how small the differences between KJV printings are.

**Later editions.** The KJV was revised in 1629 and 1638 (Cambridge), and the text most people read today is Benjamin Blayney's Oxford edition of 1769. These editions corrected printers' errors, standardized spelling and punctuation, and made the use of italics (for words the translators supplied) more consistent. They did not retranslate the Bible: the wording and meaning of the 1611 text remained the same. The KJV in this app is the 1769 text, tagged with Strong's numbers and Greek morphology by the CrossWire KJV2003 Project.

## Berean Standard Bible (BSB) and Majority Standard Bible (MSB) — with Strong's numbers

The Berean Bible was produced by the team behind **Bible Hub**, the free Bible-study website, together with the Berean Bible Translation Committee and partners such as Discovery Bible and OpenBible.com. It began as the Berean Study Bible and was later renamed the Berean Standard Bible. Its makers wanted a modern, accurate translation that anyone could use without paying for a license — and on **April 30, 2023** they dedicated both the BSB and the MSB to the **public domain**, so that apps, churches and publishers can print, quote and distribute them freely. This app is one of the beneficiaries.

The BSB reads smoothly, in the same easy contemporary English as the NIV, but it was built from the start to be traceable: every English word is connected back to the Hebrew or Greek word it translates, which is why it carries Strong's numbers so well. Because it is free, it has spread quickly across Bible apps and websites.

The **BSB** follows the Critical Text in the New Testament (CT). The **MSB** is the same translation with the New Testament conformed to the **Majority Text** (MT), so it restores the readings found in most Greek manuscripts. For example, in Revelation 22:14 the BSB reads "Blessed are those who **wash their robes**," while the MSB reads "Blessed are those who **do His commandments**," like the KJV. In Mark 1:2 the BSB has "in Isaiah the prophet," while the MSB has "in the prophets." In this app the MSB's Strong's numbers are carried over from the BSB wherever the two share the same wording (about 93% of verses); words unique to the MSB are left untagged.

## Literal Standard Version (LSV)

The LSV was published by **Covenant Press** on February 2, 2020, as a thorough revision of **Robert Young's Literal Translation** (YLT, 1862). Young's translation was famous for following the Hebrew and Greek word for word, but its English is often very hard to read. The LSV's mission is to keep that literalness while making the text readable: to translate as close to word-for-word as possible, to preserve the tenses of the original verbs, and to translate key words consistently. Its Old Testament follows the Masoretic Text (with attention to the Septuagint), and its New Testament follows the Textus Receptus and Majority Text (TR).

How literal is it? A few examples you can check in the app:

- God's name is written as it stands in Hebrew: "**YHWH** [is] my shepherd" (Psalm 23:1).
- Words added for English grammar are put in brackets: "In **[the]** beginning God created the heavens and the earth" (Genesis 1:1).
- The Greek present tense is kept: "everyone who **is believing** in Him" (John 3:16).
- *Aiōnios* is rendered "continuous": "**continuous** life" (John 3:16; Matthew 25:46).
- *Metanoeō* is rendered "convert": "**Convert**, and each of you be **immersed** on the Name of Jesus Christ" (Acts 2:38).
- *Baptizō* is rendered "**immerse**" — its literal meaning — instead of the transliteration "baptize": "**immersing** them into the Name…" (Matthew 28:19).
- *Ekklēsia* is rendered "**assembly**" instead of "church": "on this rock I will build My **Assembly**" (Matthew 16:18).

The LSV is released under a **Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0** license (CC BY-SA), which allows anyone to copy, share and even sell it, as long as the LSV is credited and any adapted version is shared under the same license.

## Douay-Rheims Bible, Challoner revision (DRC)

The Douay-Rheims was the Catholic answer to the English Protestant Bibles. It was translated by English Catholic scholars in exile in France — chiefly **Gregory Martin**, under **William Allen** at the English College. The New Testament was printed at **Rheims in 1582** and the Old Testament at **Douai in 1609–1610**, just before the KJV. It was translated not from Hebrew and Greek but from the **Latin Vulgate**, the Bible the Council of Trent had declared "authentic," and it came with heavy notes refuting Protestant teaching.

The two Bibles competed directly. The KJV translators knew the Rheims New Testament and even borrowed some of its vocabulary. The Catholic Church, for its part, never allowed Catholics to read Protestant translations: the rules of the *Index of Forbidden Books* (1564) banned Bibles translated by non-Catholics, so the KJV and every other Protestant version fell under that prohibition.

Access to the Douay-Rheims itself was limited. Under the same rules, a Catholic needed written permission from a bishop or inquisitor to read a vernacular Bible, and copies were expensive. The original was also hard to read, full of Latinisms. In **1749–1752** Bishop **Richard Challoner** revised it thoroughly, and his revision — the text in this app — became the standard English Catholic Bible for two centuries. Even so, the Bible was read mostly by clergy and educated or well-off Catholics; ordinary Catholics heard it in short readings at Mass.

The Vulgate shows through in many places:

- "**Do penance**: for the kingdom of heaven is at hand" (Matthew 3:2) — the Greek says "repent."
- "she shall crush thy head" (Genesis 3:15) — the Hebrew says "**he**" (the Seed).
- "Let him bring in the **priests** of the church" (James 5:14) and "ordain **priests** in every city" (Titus 1:5) — the Greek *presbyteros* means "elder."
- "**Charity** is patient, is kind" (1 Corinthians 13:4), from the Latin *caritas*.

The turning point came in the 20th century. In 1943 Pope Pius XII's encyclical *Divino Afflante Spiritu* encouraged translating from the original languages, and the Second Vatican Council's dogmatic constitution *Dei Verbum* (1965) declared that "easy access to Sacred Scripture should be provided for all the Christian faithful," with translations made "especially from the original texts." In 1966 the Index of Forbidden Books was abolished. The English Catholic Bibles translated from the original languages that finally reached ordinary Catholics were the **Jerusalem Bible** (1966) and, in the United States, the **New American Bible** (1970), which is also read at Mass.

# Spanish

## Reina-Valera 1909 (RV1909) — with Strong's numbers

The Reina-Valera is to the Spanish-speaking world what the King James Version is to the English-speaking world: the classic Protestant Bible, translated from the Hebrew Masoretic Text and the Greek **Textus Receptus** of Erasmus and his successors, and read by generations of believers.

**Casiodoro de Reina.** Reina was a monk of the Hieronymite monastery of **San Isidoro del Campo** near Seville, where a group of monks had embraced the Reformation and read Protestant books in secret. When the **Spanish Inquisition** closed in on them, Reina and several other monks fled in **1557**. Many who stayed behind were arrested. Julián Hernández, who had smuggled Protestant books and New Testaments into Seville, was burned at the stake after the **auto de fe of December 22, 1560**. In **April 1562** the Inquisition burned Reina **in effigy** in Seville, declared him a heresiarch and placed his works on its index of forbidden books. Spain's own Index of 1559 (issued by Inquisitor General Fernando de Valdés) had already banned Bibles in the vernacular altogether.

**The Bear Bible.** Reina spent twelve years translating while moving from city to city across Europe. His complete Bible was finally printed in **Basel** on **September 28, 1569** — the first complete Bible translated into Spanish from the original languages. It is known as the **Biblia del Oso** (the Bear Bible) because its title page shows a bear reaching for honey in a tree — a printer's emblem rather than any religious symbol that would have marked it for destruction. About 2,600 copies were printed. Reina worked from the Hebrew (the Bomberg edition) and the Greek Textus Receptus, and he also consulted the Ferrara Bible (a 1553 Spanish Old Testament made for Sephardic Jews) and earlier Spanish New Testaments.

**Cipriano de Valera.** Another monk who had escaped from San Isidoro, Cipriano de Valera, spent some twenty years revising Reina's work and published it in **Amsterdam in 1602**. From then on the Bible carried both names. Smuggled copies had to be read in secret: for more than two centuries, owning a Protestant Bible in Spain or its colonies could bring you before the Inquisition.

**Later revisions.** The Reina-Valera was revised several times (including 1862 and 1909). The **1960** revision became the standard Bible of Spanish-speaking Protestants, but it is still under copyright (© Sociedades Bíblicas en América Latina, 1960; renewed © Sociedades Bíblicas Unidas, 1988). The **1909** revision is in the public domain, which is why this app uses it — together with the Strong's numbers prepared by Rubén Gómez — instead of the 1960 text. Note that the 1909 is not purely Textus Receptus in every verse; for example, in Mark 1:2 it reads "en Isaías el profeta," the Critical Text reading.

## Reina Valera Gómez (RVG)

The RVG is a revision of the Reina-Valera prepared by **Dr. Humberto Gómez Caballero**, a Mexican Baptist pastor and missionary, together with a team of collaborators. It was first published in 2004 and revised again in 2010 and 2023. Its goal was to bring the Spanish Bible into line with the Textus Receptus and the King James Version wherever earlier Reina-Valera revisions had departed from them. For that reason it is promoted by King James–only ministries and booksellers as a Spanish Bible more faithful to the KJV than the common Reina-Valera.

**Copyright.** The RVG is copyrighted (© 2004, 2010, 2023 Dr. Humberto Gómez Caballero), but it may be reproduced freely as long as it is **not sold for profit** — which is what allows it to be included in a free app like this one.

**Differences from the RV1960.** A few examples you can compare:

- **1 Corinthians 13:4, 13:** RVG "La **caridad** es sufrida… la mayor de ellas es la **caridad**" — RV1960 "El **amor** es sufrido… el mayor de ellos es el **amor**."
- **Revelation 22:14:** RVG "Bienaventurados los que **guardan sus mandamientos**" — RV1960 "Bienaventurados los que **lavan sus ropas**" (the Critical Text reading).
- **Mark 1:2:** RVG "Como está escrito **en los profetas**" — RV1960 "Como está escrito **en Isaías el profeta**."
- **Luke 2:22:** RVG "los días de la purificación **de ella**" — RV1960 "de la purificación **de ellos**."
- **1 Peter 2:2:** RVG "para que por ella crezcáis" — RV1960 adds "para que por ella crezcáis **para salvación**," a phrase absent from the Textus Receptus.

**Why "caridad"?** *Caridad* is not a mistake for "love." It comes from the Latin *caritas*, the word the Vulgate used for the Greek *agapē* — the self-giving love of God and of neighbor, as distinct from *erōs* (desire) or mere affection. Wycliffe, the Douay-Rheims and the KJV all wrote "charity" in 1 Corinthians 13 (the KJV uses "charity" 28 times); Reina's own Bible of 1569 and the RV1909 wrote "caridad." In older English and Spanish the word meant exactly that: the highest Christian love, a love that acts. Over time "charity" and "caridad" came to mean "almsgiving," so most modern versions (including the RV1960) switched to "amor." The RVG and the KJV keep the older word.

## Torres Amat (1823–1825)

The Torres Amat Bible was translated by **Félix Torres Amat**, later bishop of Astorga, on commission from the Spanish crown (Charles IV and Ferdinand VII), and published in Madrid in **1823–1825**. Much of it was based on an earlier manuscript translation by the Jesuit **José Miguel Petisco**, which is why some editions are called the Petisco–Torres Amat Bible. It was translated from the **Latin Vulgate**, with occasional reference to the Hebrew and Greek, and it became the most widely distributed Catholic Bible in Spanish of the 19th century. Only in 1782 had the Spanish Inquisition begun to allow Bible translations in the vernacular, and only with approved notes; the first such Catholic Bible, by Felipe Scío de San Miguel (1790–1793), was also translated from the Vulgate.

Because it follows the Vulgate, the Torres Amat text is no longer what Catholic scholarship considers reliable. Modern Catholic Bibles are translated from the Hebrew and Greek, following the **Critical Text**, and they depart from the Latin in many places. Compare:

- "**Haced penitencia**: porque está cerca el reino de los cielos" (Matthew 3:2) — the Straubinger reads "**Arrepentíos**."
- "**ella** quebrantará tu cabeza" (Genesis 3:15) — the Hebrew reads "he" (the Straubinger: "**este** te aplastará la cabeza"); even the official Latin *Nova Vulgata* (1979) corrected it.
- "¿No son sus **parientes** Santiago, José, Simón y Judas?" (Matthew 13:55; also 12:46) — the Greek *adelphoi* means "brothers" (the Straubinger: "sus **hermanos**"). The change protects the doctrine of Mary's perpetual virginity.
- "habiendo ordenado **sacerdotes** en cada una de las iglesias" (Acts 14:23) — the Greek *presbyteros* means "elder"; elsewhere Torres Amat writes "presbíteros."
- "dio a luz su hijo **primogénito**" (Matthew 1:25) — here the Vulgate keeps the Textus Receptus reading, while the Critical Text and the Straubinger omit "primogénito."

**Who could read it?** The first edition filled several volumes, with the Latin text, introductions and extensive notes, and it was expensive. Church law still required approved, annotated editions, and most Spanish speakers of the time could not read. In practice it was a Bible for the clergy and for educated, well-to-do Catholics, not for the ordinary faithful.

## Straubinger — Biblia Platense (1944–1951)

**Mons. Juan Straubinger** (1883–1956) was a German priest and biblical scholar who left Nazi Germany and settled in Argentina in 1938, where he taught Sacred Scripture at the seminary of La Plata. In **1943** Pope Pius XII's encyclical **Divino Afflante Spiritu** urged Catholic scholars to translate from the original languages rather than from the Latin Vulgate. Straubinger was among the very first to do so in Spanish: his Gospels appeared in 1944, the complete New Testament (from the Greek) in 1948, and the whole Bible in **1951**, the Old Testament translated from the Hebrew and Aramaic (the deuterocanonical books from the Vulgate). It has been called the first Catholic version made in the Americas from the original texts. In Spain, the Nácar-Colunga (1944) and the Bover-Cantera (1947) Bibles were produced in the same years.

Even so, these Bibles were read mostly by priests, seminarians and educated or well-to-do Catholics. Ordinary Catholics in Latin America knew Scripture mainly through the readings at Mass. For most lay Catholics, the first Bible that was affordable and truly widespread was the **Biblia Latinoamericana** (1972), printed cheaply and in huge numbers after the Second Vatican Council.

**The Index of Forbidden Books.** For four centuries the Catholic Church controlled what its members could read through the *Index Librorum Prohibitorum*. The first Roman Index appeared in 1559 under Paul IV; the Index of the Council of Trent (1564) set the rules that lasted for centuries. Rule III restricted Bibles translated by heretics (their New Testaments were banned outright), and Rule IV forbade the faithful to read even Catholic vernacular Bibles without written permission from a bishop or inquisitor, because "more harm than good" was feared from unrestricted reading. Protestant translations — the Reina-Valera, the King James Version and all the others — therefore fell under the ban. Spain kept its own, even stricter Index through the Inquisition. The last edition of the Roman Index (1948) listed some 4,000 titles. After the Second Vatican Council, the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith announced on **June 14, 1966**, that the Index no longer had the force of church law; it was simply no longer published.
