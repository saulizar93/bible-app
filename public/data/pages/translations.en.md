Every Bible in this app is either in the public domain or licensed for free, non-commercial use. Below is a short history of each one: where it came from, what text it was translated from, and what to keep in mind when you read it side by side with the others.

## The three Greek texts: TR, MT and CT

No original letter of an apostle survives; we have copies. About **5,800 Greek manuscripts** of the New Testament are known, from small papyrus fragments of the 2nd century to complete books of the 15th. They agree in the vast majority of their wording, but where they differ, editors must choose — and their choices fall into three families:

- **Textus Receptus (TR)** — the "Received Text," the printed Greek New Testament of Erasmus (1516) and its later editions by Stephanus (1550) and Beza (1598). It stands behind the King James Version and the Reina-Valera.
- **Majority Text (MT)** — the reading found in the majority of surviving Greek manuscripts (printed, for example, by Hodges and Farstad, 1982, and Robinson and Pierpont, 2005). The MSB follows it.
- **Critical Text (CT)** — the modern scholarly text (Nestle-Aland 28 / UBS 5) that gives more weight to a few older manuscripts. Most modern Bibles, Protestant and Catholic, follow it (BSB, NIV, ESV, LSB, NASB, and modern Catholic Bibles).

### The Byzantine (Antiochian) tradition: TR and MT

The great majority of Greek manuscripts — usually estimated at **80 to over 90 percent**, and the overwhelming majority of complete manuscripts — belong to one family, called the **Byzantine** text because it was copied throughout the Greek-speaking church of the Byzantine Empire. Its defenders call it the **Antiochian** text, tracing it to Antioch of Syria, where "the disciples were called Christians first" (Acts 11:26) and from which Paul's missionary journeys began. This is the text the Greek church read and preserved in continuous use for over a thousand years. The **Majority Text** prints it as it stands in most manuscripts; the **Textus Receptus**, made from a handful of late Byzantine manuscripts available to Erasmus, belongs to the same family.

### The Alexandrian tradition: CT

The Critical Text leans most heavily on two 4th-century codices, **Codex Vaticanus** (B, c. 325–350, kept in the Vatican Library) and **Codex Sinaiticus** (ℵ, c. 350, found at St. Catherine's Monastery in Sinai in 1844), together with early papyri preserved in the dry sands of **Egypt**. Their text is called **Alexandrian**, after Alexandria in Egypt. Those two codices are 2 of some 5,800 manuscripts (about 0.03%), and manuscripts with a mainly Alexandrian text are a small minority — a few dozen to around a hundred, depending on how they are counted. Vaticanus and Sinaiticus also disagree with each other in thousands of places in the Gospels alone.

Defenders of the CT answer that **older is better**: Vaticanus and Sinaiticus are centuries older than most Byzantine copies, and the earliest papyri tend to agree with them. Defenders of the TR and MT answer that the Byzantine copies are late **because they were used** — worn-out manuscripts were replaced by fresh copies — while the Alexandrian codices survived because they were not in use and because Egypt's dry climate preserves papyrus and parchment; and that a reading attested across the whole Greek-speaking world over many centuries is more likely to be original than one found in a few manuscripts from one region.

### How much do they really differ?

Very little, in most places. The great majority of differences between the CT and the TR are small: spelling, word order, or a word that is clear from the context anyway. For example, in Matthew 1:6 the TR says "**David the king** begat Solomon," the CT simply "**David** begat Solomon"; in Matthew 4:18 the TR says "And **Jesus**, walking by the sea of Galilee," the CT "And walking by the sea…" — and modern translations of the CT add "Jesus" back for clarity, because the context makes it obvious. Differences like these change nothing.

The same gospel can be learned from any of these texts: one God who came in the flesh, the virgin birth, the cross, the resurrection, repentance, baptism in Jesus' name and the gift of the Holy Ghost are all there in every one of them. Still, where they differ, the readings found across the whole Greek-speaking church deserve more trust than those of a small group of manuscripts from one region.

### John 1:18: the one CT reading I reject

There is one Critical Text reading that I do reject, because it could change doctrine: **John 1:18**. The TR and the Majority Text read "the only begotten **Son**" (_ho monogenēs huios_); the CT reads "the only begotten **God**" (_monogenēs theos_), following Papyri 66 and 75 and the codices Sinaiticus and Vaticanus. The BSB, following the CT, adds "who is Himself God"; the MSB, following the Majority Text, does not. You can compare them side by side in this app.

> **John 1:18 (KJV):** "No man hath seen God at any time; the only begotten Son, which is in the bosom of the Father, he hath declared him."

God is not begotten. The eternal God has no beginning and was not born; what was begotten is the Son, conceived in Mary by the Holy Ghost. And a God cannot "declare" (explain, make known) the invisible God to us — it is the man Christ Jesus, the one mediator between God and men (1 Timothy 2:5), in whom the invisible God is seen and known. The TR and MT reading agrees with the rest of Scripture:

- "For God so loved the world, that he gave his **only begotten Son**… because he hath not believed in the name of the **only begotten Son** of God" (John 3:16, 18; also 1 John 4:9).
- "That which is **conceived** in her is of the Holy Ghost" (Matthew 1:20) — the Greek word (_gennēthen_) means "begotten": the Son was begotten in time, in the womb of Mary.
- "Thou art my Son, **this day have I begotten thee**" (Hebrews 1:5) — the Son has a day on which He was begotten; God has none.
- "The **Son of God** is come, and hath given us an understanding, that we may know him that is true" (1 John 5:20) — the Son, come in the flesh, makes the true God known.

And the overwhelming majority of Greek manuscripts read "only begotten **Son**." The reading "only begotten God" comes from the same small Alexandrian group from Egypt, and I do not trust that group on this point: Alexandria was the home of very influential teachers such as **Origen** (c. 185–254), who taught that the Son is a "second God" (_Against Celsus_ 5.39), subordinate to the Father, and of **Arius**, a presbyter of Alexandria, who called the Son a begotten god created by the Father. "Only begotten God" fits their theology of a second, lesser divine being; "only begotten Son" fits the Bible's one God manifested in the flesh.

### TR and MT: almost the same text

The TR and the MT agree in the overwhelming majority of their wording; the Hodges–Farstad Majority Text differs from the TR in about 1,800 places, most of them small (spelling, word order, a single word). The best-known differences are places where Erasmus, who had only a few late Greek manuscripts, followed the **Latin Vulgate** against most Greek manuscripts, or where he and later editors (Stephanus, Beza) chose readings found in very few Greek manuscripts or in none:

- **Acts 9:6** — "And he trembling and astonished said, Lord, what wilt thou have me to do?" comes from the Latin; it is not in the Greek manuscripts at this verse (similar words appear in Acts 22:10).
- **1 John 5:7-8** — "in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one" (the _Comma Johanneum_) is found in Latin manuscripts but in only a handful of late Greek ones. Erasmus left it **out** of his first two editions (1516, 1519) because he could not find it in any Greek manuscript. Catholic theologians attacked him for it — the Englishman **Edward Lee** and the Spaniard **Diego López de Zúñiga** (Stunica), who worked on the Complutensian Polyglot — accusing him of favoring the Arians and of undermining the Trinity, since the Latin Vulgate had the verse. A Greek manuscript containing it then appeared in England (Codex Montfortianus, copied around 1520, probably from the Latin), and Erasmus added the passage in his third edition (1522), writing in his notes that he did so "**lest anyone have occasion for slander**," while suspecting that the manuscript had been made to agree with the Latin. So the verse came into the TR not from the Greek manuscript tradition, but under pressure from defenders of the Latin Bible.
- **Revelation 22:16-21** — Erasmus's only Greek manuscript of Revelation lacked the last verses, so he translated them back into Greek from the Latin. That is why the TR reads "the **book** of life" in 22:19, where the Greek manuscripts read "the **tree** of life."
- **Acts 8:37** — the Ethiopian's confession, "I believe that Jesus Christ is the Son of God," is found in some Greek and Latin manuscripts but not in most Greek ones.
- **John 17:11** — the TR reads "keep through thine own name **those whom** thou hast given me" (_hous_), like the Latin Vulgate (_quos_). Most Greek manuscripts read "keep them in thy name **which** thou hast given me" (_hō_): the Father's **name** was given to the Son. In verse 12 the Majority Text agrees with the TR ("those that thou gavest me"), while the CT has "the name" there too. Compare the KJV and the MSB in the app.
- **Luke 2:22** — the TR reads "the days of **her** purification," found in very few Greek manuscripts; most read "**their** purification."
- **Revelation 16:5** — "which art, and wast, **and shalt be**" is not in any known Greek manuscript; it is a conjecture by Theodore Beza (1598). The Greek manuscripts read "which art, and wast, **the Holy One**."

You can see these differences in the app: open the same verse in the **KJV (TR)** and the **MSB (MT)** side by side.

The letters in parentheses after each Bible's name in the pane menu — (TR), (MT), (CT) — tell you which family it follows.

# English

## King James Version (KJV)

No English Bible has survived the test of time like the King James Version. More than four centuries after it first appeared, it is still one of the most printed, most memorized and most trusted translations in the world, and its phrases ("the powers that be," "a labour of love," "the salt of the earth") have shaped the English language itself.

**How it was made.** In January 1604 King James I called the Hampton Court Conference to settle disputes between the bishops of the Church of England and the Puritans. The Puritans asked for a new translation, and James agreed — partly because he despised the popular Geneva Bible, whose marginal notes he considered seditious (some notes approved of disobeying kings). About 47 scholars, organized in six companies at Westminster, Oxford and Cambridge, worked from the Hebrew Masoretic Text and the Greek Textus Receptus, revising the earlier English Bibles going back to William Tyndale rather than starting from nothing. The result was printed in 1611.

**"Church," not "congregation."** The translators did not work freely on every word. Archbishop Richard Bancroft gave them fifteen rules on the king's behalf, and rule 3 reads: _"The old ecclesiastical words to be kept, viz. the word church not to be translated congregation."_ Tyndale had rendered the Greek _ekklēsia_ — literally "assembly," "those called out" — as "congregation," which pictured the church as a gathered body of believers. "Church" pointed instead to the established institution with its bishops. That mattered to James, who had told the Puritans at Hampton Court, "No bishop, no king": an episcopal church under the crown supported royal authority. You can see the choice inside the KJV itself — in Acts 19:32, 39 and 41 the very same word _ekklēsia_ describes the mob in the theatre at Ephesus, and there the translators wrote "assembly."

**Later editions.** The KJV was revised in 1629 and 1638 (Cambridge), and the text most people read today is Benjamin Blayney's Oxford edition of 1769. These editions corrected printers' errors, standardized spelling and punctuation, and made the use of italics (for words the translators supplied) more consistent. They did not retranslate the Bible: the wording and meaning of the 1611 text remained the same. The KJV in this app is the 1769 text, tagged with Strong's numbers and Greek morphology by the CrossWire KJV2003 Project.

## Berean Standard Bible (BSB) and Majority Standard Bible (MSB)

The Berean Bible was produced by the team behind **Bible Hub**, the free Bible-study website, together with the Berean Bible Translation Committee and partners such as Discovery Bible and OpenBible.com. It began as the Berean Study Bible and was later renamed the Berean Standard Bible. Its makers wanted a modern, accurate translation that anyone could use without paying for a license — and on **April 30, 2023** they dedicated both the BSB and the MSB to the **public domain**, so that apps, churches and publishers can print, quote and distribute them freely. This app is one of the beneficiaries.

The BSB reads smoothly, in the same easy contemporary English as the NIV, but it was built from the start to be traceable: every English word is connected back to the Hebrew or Greek word it translates, which is why it carries Strong's numbers so well. Because it is free, it has spread quickly across Bible apps and websites.

The **BSB** follows the Critical Text in the New Testament (CT). The **MSB** is the same translation with the New Testament conformed to the **Majority Text** (MT), so it restores the readings found in most Greek manuscripts. For example, in Revelation 22:14 the BSB reads "Blessed are those who **wash their robes**," while the MSB reads "Blessed are those who **do His commandments**," like the KJV. In Mark 1:2 the BSB has "in Isaiah the prophet," while the MSB has "in the prophets." In this app the MSB's Strong's numbers are carried over from the BSB wherever the two share the same wording (about 93% of verses); words unique to the MSB are left untagged for now.

## Literal Standard Version (LSV)

The LSV was published by **Covenant Press** on February 2, 2020, as a thorough revision of **Robert Young's Literal Translation** (YLT, 1862). Young's translation was famous for following the Hebrew and Greek word for word, but its English is often very hard to read. The LSV's mission is to keep that literalness while making the text readable: to translate as close to word-for-word as possible, to preserve the tenses of the original verbs, and to translate key words consistently. Its Old Testament follows the Masoretic Text (with attention to the Septuagint), and its New Testament follows the Textus Receptus and Majority Text (TR).

How literal is it? A few examples you can check in the app:

- God's name is written as it stands in Hebrew: "**YHWH** [is] my shepherd" (Psalm 23:1).
- Words added for English grammar are put in brackets: "In **[the]** beginning God created the heavens and the earth" (Genesis 1:1).
- The Greek present tense is kept: "everyone who **is believing** in Him" (John 3:16).
- _Aiōnios_ is rendered "continuous": "**continuous** life" (John 3:16; Matthew 25:46).
- _Metanoeō_ is rendered "convert": "**Convert**, and each of you be **immersed** on the Name of Jesus Christ" (Acts 2:38).
- _Baptizō_ is rendered "**immerse**" — its literal meaning — instead of the transliteration "baptize": "**immersing** them into the Name…" (Matthew 28:19).
- _Ekklēsia_ is rendered "**assembly**" instead of "church": "on this rock I will build My **Assembly**" (Matthew 16:18).

The LSV is released under a **Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0** license (CC BY-SA), which allows anyone to copy, share and even sell it, as long as the LSV is credited and any adapted version is shared under the same license.

## Young's Literal Translation (YLT)

**Robert Young** (1822–1888) was a Scottish publisher and self-taught scholar of the biblical languages, best known for his _Analytical Concordance to the Bible_ (1879), still used today. In **1862** he published his own translation, revised in **1887** and **1898**, with one goal: to follow the Hebrew and Greek word for word, in the same order and with the same tenses, even when the English becomes awkward. Its Old Testament follows the Masoretic Text and its New Testament the Textus Receptus (TR). It is in the **public domain**.

Young believed the Hebrew verb forms should be translated by their tense, not by context, and he translated key words the same way every time. The result is the most literal English Bible of its century, and the starting point of the LSV (2020), which revised it to make it readable. A few examples you can check in the app:

- "In the beginning of God's preparing the heavens and the earth—" (Genesis 1:1).
- God's name is written "**Jehovah**": "A Psalm of David. Jehovah is my shepherd, I do not lack" (Psalm 23:1).
- _Aiōnios_ is "**age-during**": "may have life age-during" (John 3:16); "punishment age-during… life age-during" (Matthew 25:46).
- _Metanoeō_ is "**reform**": "Reform, for come nigh hath the reign of the heavens" (Matthew 3:2); "Reform, and be baptized each of you on the name of Jesus Christ" (Acts 2:38).
- _Basileia_ is "**reign**" and _makarios_ "**happy**": "Happy the poor in spirit—because theirs is the reign of the heavens" (Matthew 5:3).
- _Ekklēsia_ is "**assembly**": "upon this rock I will build my assembly" (Matthew 16:18).
- The Greek present tense is kept: "every one who **is believing** in him" (John 3:16).

In this app the YLT text comes from the CrossWire SWORD module (1898 edition). Young's own italics (words he supplied) are kept, and its Strong's numbers were carried over from the KJV word by word, as for the LSV: about 87% of the words are tagged, and where Young's wording strays far from the KJV's a few words may carry a neighbour's number.

## Septuagint (LXX)

The **Septuagint** is the Greek translation of the Hebrew Scriptures made by Jews in **Alexandria**, Egypt. According to the _Letter of Aristeas_, King Ptolemy II Philadelphus (285–246 BC) asked for the Law of Moses in Greek, and 72 elders from Jerusalem translated it — hence the name "Seventy" (LXX). The legend is embellished, but the Torah was indeed translated in Alexandria in the 3rd century BC, and the Prophets and Writings followed over the next two centuries, by different translators of very different skill. It became the Bible of Greek-speaking Jews and then of the early Church: most Old Testament quotations in the New Testament follow it.

**There was never one fixed list of books.** "The Septuagint" is not a single book with a table of contents but a collection that grew and varied:

- **Philo** of Alexandria (c. 20 BC–AD 50), who read the Scriptures in Greek, quotes or alludes to a great many books of the Hebrew Bible but never to any of the apocrypha.
- **Josephus** (c. AD 95) counts 22 sacred books, the Hebrew canon, and says nothing has been added to them (_Against Apion_ 1.38–42).
- The great Christian codices of the 4th–5th centuries each have a different list. **Codex Vaticanus** has Wisdom, Sirach, Judith, Tobit and Baruch but **no Maccabees**; **Codex Sinaiticus** has **1 and 4 Maccabees**; **Codex Alexandrinus** has **all four** books of Maccabees, and its table of contents also lists the Psalms of Solomon.

In this app the LXX pane shows **only the 39 books of the Hebrew (Jewish and Protestant) canon**; the apocrypha are left out. Its New Testament is the KJV's.

**How this pane was made.** The English is **Sir Lancelot Brenton's** translation of 1851 (from the Sixtine edition of 1587, based mainly on Codex Vaticanus), with light language updates (LXX2012, eBible.org, public domain). The Greek of the interlinear is **Alfred Rahlfs'** _Septuaginta_ (1935), with grammatical parsing from the CCAT of the University of Pennsylvania and Strong's numbers added by the CrossWire Bible Society. In Daniel both follow Theodotion's version, and in Judges the "B" text. Brenton's English words were tagged with the Strong's numbers of the Greek words they translate (about three words in four; most names are untagged, because the Greek has no number for them).

**Verses moved to the KJV's numbers.** The Septuagint numbers chapters and verses its own way, and in some books it has a different order. So that the LXX pane can stand beside the KJV, every verse was matched by its content to the KJV verse it corresponds to, without ever splitting a verse: where the Septuagint has more, two of its verses share one KJV number, and where it lacks a verse, that number stays empty. The main moves:

- **Psalms**: the Septuagint joins Psalms 9–10 and 114–115, splits 116 and 147, and counts the titles as verse 1, so most psalms are one number behind. Its Psalm 22 ("The Lord tends me as a shepherd") is shown as **Psalm 23**, its 9:21 as **10:1**.
- **Jeremiah**: the oracles against the nations (KJV 46–51) come after 25:13 in the Greek, and chapters 26–45 are numbered 33–51. The new covenant, Greek 38:31, is shown at **31:31**; Moab, Greek 31, at **48**. **Jeremiah 30:10–11 is missing** in the Septuagint: those words stand at 46:27–28.
- **Malachi**: the Greek has only three chapters; its 3:19–24 is shown as **4:1–6**.
- **1 Kings 20 and 21** are in the opposite order, **Exodus 36–39** describe the tabernacle in another order, and **Genesis 32:1** (Greek) is the KJV's **31:55**, **Isaiah 8:23** is **9:1**, **Exodus 7:26–29** is **8:1–4**, along with many smaller shifts of a verse.

**The name of God.** By the time of the Septuagint, Jews had become afraid to pronounce the name **YHWH**. The Mishnah later says that whoever pronounces the Name as it is written has no share in the world to come (_Sanhedrin_ 10:1). In Alexandria this fear met Greek philosophy, whose highest god had no name: for Plato the maker and father of the universe is "hard to find, and having found him, impossible to declare to all" (_Timaeus_ 28c), and Philo, the Jew of Alexandria, writes that God has no proper name at all (_On the Change of Names_ 11–14). The oldest Greek manuscripts still wrote the Name: a papyrus of Deuteronomy from the 1st century BC (Papyrus Fouad 266) writes **YHWH** in Hebrew letters inside the Greek, the Minor Prophets scroll of Nahal Hever writes it in old Hebrew script, and a Qumran scroll of Leviticus writes **ΙΑΩ** (_Iaō_). In the copies that came down to us it was replaced everywhere by **Κύριος**, "Lord" — the personal name became a title. You can see where this led:

- **Leviticus 24:16** — the Hebrew condemns the man who **blasphemes** the name of YHWH; the Greek says: "And **he that names the name of the Lord**, let him die the death." To _say_ the Name became the crime, which is how the rabbis read the verse too (_Sanhedrin_ 7:5).
- **Exodus 3:14** — "I will be who I will be" (_ehyeh asher ehyeh_) became "**I am THE BEING**" (_egō eimi ho ōn_): a personal name turned into the philosophers' "Being."
- **Amos 9:6** — "YHWH is his name" became "**the Lord God Almighty** is his name": a name replaced by titles.
- **Genesis 2:4; 3:8–9** — "YHWH Elohim" became "**the Lord God**" (Rahlfs' Greek has only "God" in 2:4), and **Psalm 23:1**, "YHWH is my shepherd," became "**the Lord** tends me as a shepherd."
- **Deuteronomy 32:8** — the bounds of the peoples were set "according to the number of the children of Israel"; the Greek has "according to the number of **the angels of God**."

**A God who does not feel or act like a man.** The translators also softened passages where God is said to have a body, to feel, or to be seen:

- **Genesis 6:6** — "it **repented** the LORD… and it **grieved him** at his heart" became "God **laid it to heart**… and **pondered** it deeply."
- **Exodus 15:3** — "The LORD is **a man of war**" became "The Lord **bringing wars to nothing**."
- **Exodus 24:10** — "they **saw the God of Israel**" became "they saw **the place where** the God of Israel stood."
- **Exodus 4:24** — "the LORD met him, and sought to kill him" became "**the angel of the Lord** met him."
- **Exodus 19:3** — "Moses went up **unto God**" became "to **the mount** of God"; **Numbers 12:8**, "the **similitude** of the LORD shall he behold," became "he has seen **the glory** of the Lord."
- **Joshua 4:24** — "the **hand** of the LORD" became "the **power** of the Lord"; **Isaiah 6:1**, "**his train** filled the temple," became "the house was full of **his glory**."

**The New Testament and the Septuagint.** The apostles wrote in Greek and often quoted the Septuagint, so its wording passed into the New Testament:

- **"Lord" for YHWH**: "whosoever shall call upon the name of **the Lord** shall be saved" (Romans 10:13, from Joel 2:32); "**The Lord** said unto my Lord" (Matthew 22:44, from Psalm 110:1).
- **Hebrews 10:5**: "a **body** hast thou prepared me" — the Septuagint's reading of Psalm 40:6, where the Hebrew says "mine **ears** hast thou opened." (Brenton has "body," as most Greek manuscripts; Rahlfs prints "ears.")
- **Acts 7:14**: "threescore and **fifteen** souls" — the Septuagint's 75 in Genesis 46:27, against the Hebrew's 70.
- **Hebrews 1:6**: "let all the angels of God worship him" — Deuteronomy 32:43 in the Greek, a line not in the Hebrew.
- **Hebrews 11:21**: Jacob "worshipped, leaning upon **the top of his staff**" — the Septuagint of Genesis 47:31, where the Hebrew has "the bed's head."
- **Matthew 1:23**: "Behold, a **virgin** shall be with child" — the Septuagint's _parthenos_ in Isaiah 7:14.

## Douay-Rheims Bible, Challoner revision (DRC)

The Douay-Rheims was the Catholic answer to the English Protestant Bibles. It was translated by English Catholic scholars in exile in France — chiefly **Gregory Martin**, under **William Allen** at the English College. The New Testament was printed at **Rheims in 1582** and the Old Testament at **Douai in 1609–1610**, just before the KJV. It was translated not from Hebrew and Greek but from the **Latin Vulgate**, the Bible the Council of Trent had declared "authentic," and it came with heavy notes refuting Protestant teaching.

The two Bibles competed directly. The KJV translators knew the Rheims New Testament and even borrowed some of its vocabulary. The Catholic Church, for its part, never allowed Catholics to read Protestant translations: the rules of the _Index of Forbidden Books_ (1564) banned Bibles translated by non-Catholics, so the KJV and every other Protestant version fell under that prohibition.

Access to the Douay-Rheims itself was limited. Under the same rules, a Catholic needed written permission from a bishop or inquisitor to read a vernacular Bible, and copies were expensive. The original was also hard to read, full of Latinisms. In **1749–1752** Bishop **Richard Challoner** revised it thoroughly, and his revision — the text in this app — became the standard English Catholic Bible for two centuries. Even so, the Bible was read mostly by clergy and educated or well-off Catholics; ordinary Catholics heard it in short readings at Mass.

The Vulgate shows through in many places:

- "**Do penance**: for the kingdom of heaven is at hand" (Matthew 3:2) — the Greek says "repent."
- "she shall crush thy head" (Genesis 3:15) — the Hebrew says "**he**" (the Seed).
- "Let him bring in the **priests** of the church" (James 5:14) and "ordain **priests** in every city" (Titus 1:5) — the Greek _presbyteros_ means "elder."
- "**Charity** is patient, is kind" (1 Corinthians 13:4), from the Latin _caritas_.

The turning point came in the 20th century. In 1943 Pope Pius XII's encyclical _Divino Afflante Spiritu_ encouraged translating from the original languages, and the Second Vatican Council's dogmatic constitution _Dei Verbum_ (1965) declared that "easy access to Sacred Scripture should be provided for all the Christian faithful," with translations made "especially from the original texts." In 1966 the Index of Forbidden Books was abolished. The English Catholic Bibles translated from the original languages that finally reached ordinary Catholics were the **Jerusalem Bible (en)** (1966) and, in the United States, the **New American Bible** (1970), which is also read at Mass.

# Spanish

## Reina-Valera 1909 (RV1909)

The Reina-Valera is to the Spanish-speaking world what the King James Version is to the English-speaking world: the classic Protestant Bible, translated from the Hebrew Masoretic Text and the Greek **Textus Receptus** of Erasmus and his successors, and read by generations of believers.

**Casiodoro de Reina.** Reina was a monk of the Hieronymite monastery of **San Isidoro del Campo** near Seville, where a group of monks had embraced the Reformation and read Protestant books in secret. When the **Spanish Inquisition** closed in on them, Reina and several other monks fled in **1557**. Many who stayed behind were arrested. Julián Hernández, who had smuggled Protestant books and New Testaments into Seville, was burned at the stake after the **auto de fe of December 22, 1560**. In **April 1562** the Inquisition burned Reina **in effigy**(_a doll of him_) in Seville, declared him a heresiarch and placed his works on its index of forbidden books. Spain's own Index of 1559 (issued by Inquisitor General Fernando de Valdés) had already banned Bibles in the vernacular altogether.

**The Bear Bible.** Reina spent twelve years translating while moving from city to city across Europe. His complete Bible was finally printed in **Basel** on **September 28, 1569** — the first complete Bible translated into Spanish from the original languages. It is known as the **Biblia del Oso** (the Bear Bible) because its title page shows a bear reaching for honey in a tree — a printer's emblem rather than any religious symbol that would have marked it for destruction. About 2,600 copies were printed. Reina worked from the Hebrew (the Bomberg edition) and the Greek Textus Receptus, and he also consulted the Ferrara Bible (a 1553 Spanish Old Testament made for Sephardic Jews) and earlier Spanish New Testaments.

**Cipriano de Valera.** Another monk who had escaped from San Isidoro, Cipriano de Valera, spent some twenty years revising Reina's work and published it in **Amsterdam in 1602**. From then on the Bible carried both names. Smuggled copies had to be read in secret: for more than two centuries, owning a Protestant Bible in Spain or its colonies could bring you before the Inquisition.

**Later revisions.** The Reina-Valera was revised several times (including 1862 and 1909). The **1960** revision became the standard Bible of Spanish-speaking Protestants, but it is still under copyright (© Sociedades Bíblicas en América Latina, 1960; renewed © Sociedades Bíblicas Unidas, 1988). The **1909** revision is in the public domain, which is why this app uses it — together with the Strong's numbers prepared by Rubén Gómez — instead of the 1960 text. Note that the 1909 is not purely Textus Receptus in every verse; for example, in Mark 1:2 it reads "en Isaías el profeta," the Critical Text reading.

## Reina Valera Gómez (RVG)

The RVG is a revision of the Reina-Valera prepared by **Dr. Humberto Gómez Caballero**, a Mexican Baptist pastor and missionary, together with a team of collaborators. It was first published in 2004 and revised again in 2010 and 2023. Its goal was to bring the Spanish Bible into line with the Textus Receptus and the King James Version wherever earlier Reina-Valera revisions had departed from them. For that reason it is promoted by King James–only ministries and booksellers as a Spanish Bible more faithful to the KJV than the common Reina-Valera.

**Copyright.** The RVG is copyrighted (© 2004, 2010, 2023 Dr. Humberto Gómez Caballero), but it may be reproduced freely as long as it is **not sold for profit** — which is what allows it to be included in a free app like this one.

**Differences from the RV1960.** A few examples you can compare:

- **1 Corinthians 13:4, 13:** RVG "La **caridad** es sufrida… la mayor de ellas es la **caridad**" — RV1960 "El **amor** es sufrido… el mayor de ellos es el **amor**."
- **Revelation 22:14:** RVG "Bienaventurados los que **guardan sus mandamientos**" — RV1960 "Bienaventurados los que **lavan sus ropas**" (the Critical Text reading).
- **Mark 1:2:** RVG "Como está escrito **en los profetas**" — RV1960 "Como está escrito **en Isaías el profeta**."
- **Luke 2:22:** RVG "los días de la purificación **de ella**" — RV1960 "de la purificación **de ellos**."
- **1 Peter 2:2:** RVG "para que por ella crezcáis" — RV1960 adds "para que por ella crezcáis **para salvación**," a phrase absent from the Textus Receptus.

**Why "caridad"?** _Caridad_ is not a mistake for "love." It comes from the Latin _caritas_, the word the Vulgate used for the Greek _agapē_ — the self-giving love of God and of neighbor, as distinct from _erōs_ (desire) or mere affection. Wycliffe, the Douay-Rheims and the KJV all wrote "charity" in 1 Corinthians 13 (the KJV uses "charity" 28 times); Reina's own Bible of 1569 and the RV1909 wrote "caridad." In older English and Spanish the word meant exactly that: the highest Christian love, a love that acts. Over time "charity" and "caridad" came to mean "almsgiving," so most modern versions (including the RV1960) switched to "amor." The RVG and the KJV keep the older word.

## Reina Valera Ojeda (RVO)

The **Reina Valera Ojeda** is my own revision of the **Reina-Valera 1909**, which is in the public domain. It is a work in progress: so far I have mainly finished the **Gospel of Matthew**, and as I keep writing the study notes I will keep revising the text along with them, book by book.

Its goal is to be **modern, understandable and full of translation notes**, so that the reader gets a deeper understanding of the text than the RV1960 offers. The main changes so far:

- **The name of God is written YHWH** instead of "Jehová": "YHWH es mi pastor; nada me faltará" (Psalm 23:1).
- **Archaic words and constructions are updated** into everyday Spanish, and the text is retouched where the 1909 wording no longer says clearly what the Greek says: "Libro de la generación de **Jesús el Mesías**, hijo de David" (Matthew 1:1).
- **Translator's notes** (marked †) explain words and idioms of the original — what _mammon_ means, why "adding a cubit to one's stature" is a Hebrew way of speaking about the length of life, how much a denarius was worth — and where the Greek texts differ.
- **TR, MT and CT.** The base is the Textus Receptus (TR), like the 1909, but the notes point out where the Majority Text (MT) and the Critical Text (CT) read differently, for example: "The Critical Text (CT) omits 'without a cause' (_eikē_, G1500)… The Textus Receptus (TR) and the Majority Text (MT) include it" (Matthew 5:22).

Its words carry Strong's numbers, so the RVO works with the lexicon and the Hebrew and Greek interlinear like the other Bibles in the app.

## Torres Amat (1823–1825)

The Torres Amat Bible was translated by **Félix Torres Amat**, later bishop of Astorga, on commission from the Spanish crown (Charles IV and Ferdinand VII), and published in Madrid in **1823–1825**. Much of it was based on an earlier manuscript translation by the Jesuit **José Miguel Petisco**, which is why some editions are called the Petisco–Torres Amat Bible. It was translated from the **Latin Vulgate**, with occasional reference to the Hebrew and Greek, and it became the most widely distributed Catholic Bible in Spanish of the 19th century. Only in 1782 had the Spanish Inquisition begun to allow Bible translations in the vernacular, and only with approved notes; the first such Catholic Bible, by Felipe Scío de San Miguel (1790–1793), was also translated from the Vulgate.

Because it follows the Vulgate, the Torres Amat text is no longer what Catholic scholarship considers reliable. Modern Catholic Bibles are translated from the Hebrew and Greek, following the **Critical Text**, and they depart from the Latin in many places. Compare:

- "**Haced penitencia**: porque está cerca el reino de los cielos" (Matthew 3:2) — the Straubinger reads "**Arrepentíos**."
- "**ella** quebrantará tu cabeza" (Genesis 3:15) — the Hebrew reads "he" (the Straubinger: "**este** te aplastará la cabeza"); even the official Latin _Nova Vulgata_ (1979) corrected it.
- "¿No son sus **parientes** Santiago, José, Simón y Judas? Y sus **primas hermanas**…" (Matthew 13:55-56; also 12:46), and "**primo hermano** de Santiago" (Mark 6:3; Galatians 1:19) — the Greek _adelphoi_ means "brothers" and _adelphai_ "sisters" (the Straubinger: "sus **hermanos**"). The change protects the doctrine of Mary's perpetual virginity.
- "habiendo ordenado **sacerdotes** en cada una de las iglesias" (Acts 14:23) — the Greek _presbyteros_ means "elder"; elsewhere Torres Amat writes "presbíteros."
- "dio a luz su hijo **primogénito**" (Matthew 1:25) — here the Vulgate keeps the Textus Receptus reading, while the Critical Text and the Straubinger omit "primogénito."

**Who could read it?** The first edition filled several volumes, with the Latin text, introductions and extensive notes, and it was expensive. Church law still required approved, annotated editions, and most Spanish speakers of the time could not read. In practice it was a Bible for the clergy and for educated, well-to-do Catholics, not for the ordinary faithful.

## Straubinger — Biblia Platense (1944–1951)

**Mons. Juan Straubinger** (1883–1956) was a German priest and biblical scholar who left Nazi Germany and settled in Argentina in 1938, where he taught Sacred Scripture at the seminary of La Plata. In **1943** Pope Pius XII's encyclical **Divino Afflante Spiritu** urged Catholic scholars to translate from the original languages rather than from the Latin Vulgate. Straubinger was among the very first to do so in Spanish: his Gospels appeared in 1944, the complete New Testament (from the Greek) in 1948, and the whole Bible in **1951**, the Old Testament translated from the Hebrew and Aramaic (the deuterocanonical books from the Vulgate). It has been called the first Catholic version made in the Americas from the original texts. In Spain, the Nácar-Colunga (1944) and the Bover-Cantera (1947) Bibles were produced in the same years.

Even so, these Bibles were read mostly by priests, seminarians and educated or well-to-do Catholics. Ordinary Catholics in Latin America knew Scripture mainly through the readings at Mass. For most lay Catholics, the first Bible that was affordable and truly widespread was the **Biblia Latinoamericana** (1972), printed cheaply and in huge numbers after the Second Vatican Council (1965).

**The Index of Forbidden Books.** For four centuries the Catholic Church controlled what its members could read through the _Index Librorum Prohibitorum_. The first Roman Index appeared in 1559 under Paul IV; the Index of the Council of Trent (1564) set the rules that lasted for centuries. Rule III restricted Bibles translated by heretics (their New Testaments were banned outright), and Rule IV forbade the faithful to read even Catholic vernacular Bibles without written permission from a bishop or inquisitor, because "more harm than good" was feared from unrestricted reading. Protestant translations — the Reina-Valera, the King James Version and all the others — therefore fell under the ban. Spain kept its own, even stricter Index through the Inquisition. The last edition of the Roman Index (1948) listed some 4,000 titles. After the Second Vatican Council, the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith announced on **June 14, 1966**, that the Index no longer had the force of church law; it was simply no longer published.
