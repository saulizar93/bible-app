## The claim

Most Christians in the world today venerate images. Catholics and Orthodox Christians bow before icons and statues, kiss them, light candles and burn incense before them, and pray in front of them. They are taught that this is **apostolic tradition**: the Second Council of Nicaea (787) said that incense and lights are offered to images "according to ancient pious custom," and it pronounced an anathema on anyone who rejects it. Many Orthodox teachers go further and say that to reject icons is to deny the Incarnation of Christ.

This page tests that claim against Scripture and history. In short:

- **Scripture** forbids not only worshipping images but **bowing down** to them, and God's people were punished when they used an image to worship the true God.
- For the first **three centuries**, Christians had **no veneration of images**. Their pagan neighbours mocked them for it, and the Fathers answered that Christians have no images to worship. The first people known to honour an image of Christ were **heretics**, who did it "after the manner of the Gentiles."
- Images came into the churches **after Constantine**, together with crowds of converts from paganism. Even then bishops like Epiphanius, Augustine and Pope Gregory the Great **condemned the adoration** of pictures.
- Veneration was made **dogma only in 787**, at a council called by an empress for political reasons, after a council of 338 bishops had condemned it in 754. The West rejected the 787 council for decades.
- The word **"ecumenical"** is applied selectively, after the fact: councils that won are counted, councils that lost are erased.
- The **Book of Wisdom**, which Catholics and Orthodox accept as Scripture, describes exactly this process: an image made out of love, honoured, passed on as a custom, enforced by rulers, embellished by artists, until the people treat it as a god.

Much of the historical material on this page follows Gavin Ortlund, _What It Means to Be Protestant: The Case for an Always-Reforming Church_ (Zondervan, 2024), and Richard Price's translation of _The Acts of the Second Council of Nicaea (787)_ (Liverpool University Press, 2018). Bible quotations are from the King James Version; quotations from Wisdom are from the 1611 King James Apocrypha.

### What this page is, and is not, about

The question is not whether Christians may make **art**. Early Christians painted symbols like the fish, the anchor and the Good Shepherd on the walls of their burial places (the catacombs), and a house church at **Dura-Europos** in Syria (c. A.D. 240) had wall paintings of Bible scenes. God Himself commanded cherubim, palm trees and flowers for the tabernacle and the temple. Pictures that **tell a story** or **decorate** are not the issue.

The question is **veneration**: bowing before an image, kissing it, lighting candles and burning incense to it, carrying it in procession, praying in front of it, and treating it as a channel of grace. That is what Nicaea II commanded, and that is what the early church did not do.

# 1. The majority is not proof

It can feel safe to believe what most Christians believe. But the majority, even the majority of church leaders, has been wrong before, sometimes for centuries. A few examples of things the Roman Catholic Church taught or allowed for a long time and later abandoned or reversed:

- **Unbaptized infants go to hell.** Augustine taught it, and the Council of Florence (1439) defined that the souls of those who die "in original sin alone" go down "straightaway to hell," though to lesser punishments. Medieval theologians softened this into **Limbo**, which was taught for centuries; in 2007 the International Theological Commission wrote that there are "serious theological and liturgical grounds for hope" that such infants are saved.
- **No salvation outside the Roman Church.** Boniface VIII declared it "altogether necessary to salvation for every human creature to be subject to the Roman Pontiff" (_Unam Sanctam_, 1302), and Florence (1442) said that pagans, Jews, heretics and schismatics go "into the everlasting fire" unless they join her before death. Vatican II (_Lumen Gentium_ 16, 1964) teaches that those who are not Catholic through no fault of their own can be saved.
- **Slavery.** Nicholas V authorized the king of Portugal to reduce Saracens and pagans to "perpetual servitude" (_Dum Diversas_, 1452; _Romanus Pontifex_, 1455). As late as 1866 an instruction of the Holy Office under Pius IX said that slavery in itself "is not at all contrary to the natural and divine law."
- **Usury** (charging interest on loans). Lateran III (1179) excommunicated open usurers, the Council of Vienne (1311) declared it heresy to say usury is not a sin, and Benedict XIV repeated the condemnation in 1745 (_Vix Pervenit_). From the 1820s the Holy Office quietly allowed Catholics to lend at interest.
- **The death penalty.** Innocent III required converted Waldensians to confess that the state may execute without mortal sin (1208), and the Catechism of 1992 allowed it in rare cases. In 2018 the Catechism was changed to say it is "inadmissible."
- **Religious liberty.** Gregory XVI called liberty of conscience an "absurd and erroneous" idea, even "madness" (_Mirari Vos_, 1832), and Pius IX condemned it again (_Quanta Cura_, 1864). Vatican II affirmed religious liberty as a right (_Dignitatis Humanae_, 1965).
- **Worship with non-Catholics.** The Code of Canon Law of 1917 (canon 1258) forbade Catholics to take an active part in non-Catholic services. Since Vatican II, joint prayer is encouraged.

If the majority could be wrong about these things for centuries, then the fact that most Christians venerate images today does not prove that the apostles did. The question has to be settled by Scripture and by the evidence of history.

# 2. What God commanded in the Old Testament

### The second commandment forbids bowing, not only worshipping

> **Exodus 20:4-5:** "Thou shalt not make unto thee any graven image, or any likeness of any thing that is in heaven above, or that is in the earth beneath, or that is in the water under the earth: **Thou shalt not bow down thyself to them, nor serve them**: for I the LORD thy God am a jealous God…"

Catholic and Orthodox theology distinguishes _latreia_ (worship, given to God alone) from _proskynesis_ (bowing or "veneration," which it says may be given to images). But the Greek Old Testament used by the early church, the Septuagint, translates Exodus 20:5 with **both** words: _ou proskynēseis autois oude mē latreusēs autois_ — "thou shalt not **bow down** (_proskyneō_) to them, nor **serve** (_latreuō_) them." The commandment forbids exactly the act that Nicaea II permitted.

> **Leviticus 26:1:** "Ye shall make you no idols nor graven image, neither rear you up a standing image, neither shall ye set up any image of stone in your land, **to bow down unto it**: for I am the LORD your God."

> **Deuteronomy 4:15-16:** "Take ye therefore good heed unto yourselves; for **ye saw no manner of similitude** on the day that the LORD spake unto you in Horeb out of the midst of the fire: Lest ye corrupt yourselves, and make you a graven image, the similitude of any figure, **the likeness of male or female**."

### The golden calf: the right God, the wrong method

The golden calf was not presented as a new god. Aaron built an altar before it and proclaimed, "To morrow is **a feast to the LORD**" (Exodus 32:5). The people meant to worship **the God who brought them out of Egypt** by means of an image (32:4). God called it corruption and judgment followed:

> **Exodus 32:8:** "They have turned aside quickly out of the way which I commanded them: they have made them a molten calf, and have worshipped it, and have sacrificed thereunto, and said, These be thy gods, O Israel, which have brought thee up out of the land of Egypt."

Using an image to honour the true God is precisely the defence offered for icons: "the honour passes to the prototype." Israel's sin at Sinai shows that God does not accept it.

### Jeroboam's calves: images for political reasons

Five centuries later, King Jeroboam set up golden calves at Bethel and Dan. The reason was **politics**:

> **1 Kings 12:26-28:** "And Jeroboam said in his heart, Now shall the kingdom return to the house of David: If this people go up to do sacrifice in the house of the LORD at Jerusalem, then shall the heart of this people turn again unto their lord… Whereupon **the king took counsel**, and made two calves of gold, and said unto them, It is too much for you to go up to Jerusalem: behold thy gods, O Israel, which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt."

"And this thing became a sin" (1 Kings 12:30). Remember this pattern when we come to the emperors and empresses of the eighth and ninth centuries.

### Do not borrow the methods of the nations

> **Deuteronomy 12:30, 32:** "…enquire not after their gods, saying, **How did these nations serve their gods? even so will I do likewise.**… What thing soever I command you, observe to do it: **thou shalt not add thereto, nor diminish from it**."

### The prophets on images

> **Psalm 115:4-8:** "Their idols are silver and gold, the work of men's hands. They have mouths, but they speak not: eyes have they, but they see not: They have ears, but they hear not: noses have they, but they smell not: They have hands, but they handle not: feet have they, but they walk not: neither speak they through their throat. They that make them are like unto them; so is every one that trusteth in them."

> **Psalm 97:7:** "Confounded be all they that serve graven images, that boast themselves of idols."

> **Isaiah 42:8:** "I am the LORD: that is my name: and my glory will I not give to another, **neither my praise to graven images**."

> **Isaiah 44:17:** "And the residue thereof he maketh a god, even his graven image: **he falleth down unto it**, and worshippeth it, and prayeth unto it, and saith, Deliver me; for thou art my god."

> **Isaiah 46:7:** "They bear him upon the shoulder, they carry him, and set him in his place, and he standeth; from his place shall he not remove: yea, one shall cry unto him, yet can he not answer, nor save him out of his trouble."

> **Habakkuk 2:18:** "What profiteth the graven image that the maker thereof hath graven it; the molten image, and **a teacher of lies**…?"

> **Jeremiah 10:8:** "…**the stock is a doctrine of vanities**."

Notice the last two. Images are defended as "books for the illiterate." The prophets say an image teaches **lies** and **vanities**.

And when Nebuchadnezzar commanded everyone to bow before his golden image, three young Jews refused to bow even once, even under threat of death: "we will not serve thy gods, **nor worship the golden image which thou hast set up**" (Daniel 3:18).

### "But God commanded the cherubim and the bronze serpent"

This is the most common objection, and the text itself answers it.

- **God commanded them, with exact instructions** (Exodus 25:18-22; Numbers 21:8). They did not come from human devotion or custom. A Christian may not reason, "God commanded two cherubim over the ark, therefore I may make a statue of a saint and kiss it," any more than he may reason, "God commanded Israel to sacrifice lambs, therefore I may sacrifice one."
- **Nobody venerated them.** The cherubim were inside the Most Holy Place, seen by the high priest once a year. No Israelite kissed them, lit candles to them or prayed to them. God said, "there I will meet with thee… **from between the two cherubims**" (Exodus 25:22): His presence above the mercy seat was the point, not the figures.
- **The bronze serpent was destroyed the moment it was venerated.** Israel kept it for about seven hundred years, and it became an object of devotion. Godly King Hezekiah "brake in pieces the brasen serpent that Moses had made: **for unto those days the children of Israel did burn incense to it**: and he called it Nehushtan" ("a piece of bronze," 2 Kings 18:4). Burning incense before a sacred image is exactly what Nicaea II allowed "according to ancient pious custom." Scripture praises the king who smashed it.
- **Its purpose was a picture of the Cross.** Israel was told to **look** at it to be healed (Numbers 21:8-9), and Jesus explained its meaning: "as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, even so must the Son of man be lifted up: That whosoever **believeth in him** should not perish" (John 3:14-15). The fulfilment is faith in Christ, not devotion to bronze.

# 3. Wisdom 13–15: Rome's own Bible describes how idolatry is born

The Book of Wisdom is not in the Hebrew Bible, but the Catholic and Orthodox churches accept it as Scripture (see the page **Deuterocanonical Books**). So its testimony counts against them with special force. Chapters 13–15 describe the origin and growth of idolatry, and the description fits the history of image veneration step by step.

### Idols were not there from the beginning

> **Wisdom 14:12-14:** "For the devising of idols was the beginning of spiritual fornication, and the invention of them the corruption of life. For **neither were they from the beginning**, neither shall they be for ever. For **by the vain glory of men they entered into the world**, and therefore shall they come shortly to an end."

Wisdom says idolatry is a **human development**: it did not exist at the start; it was invented and entered later.

### The father and the king: how a custom becomes a law

> **Wisdom 14:15-21:** "For **a father afflicted with untimely mourning, when he hath made an image of his child** soon taken away, now **honoured** him as a god, which was then a dead man, and **delivered to those that were under him ceremonies and sacrifices**. Thus **in process of time an ungodly custom grown strong was kept as a law**, and graven images were worshipped **by the commandments of kings**. Whom men could not honour in presence, because they dwelt far off, they took the counterfeit of his visage from far, and **made an express image of a king whom they honoured**, to the end that by this their forwardness they might flatter him that was absent, as if he were present. Also **the singular diligence of the artificer** did help to set forward the ignorant to more superstition. For he, peradventure willing to please one in authority, forced all his skill to make the resemblance of the best fashion. And so the multitude, **allured by the grace of the work**, took him now for a god, which a little before was but honoured as a man. And this was an occasion to deceive the world: for men, being subject either to calamity or tyranny, did ascribe unto stones and stocks the incommunicable name."

Look at the steps:

1. **It begins with love and grief.** A father makes a picture of a dead son. Nothing sinister, only remembrance.
2. **The picture is "honoured."** Notice the word: not yet "worshipped," only **honoured**. This is the same distinction used today: "we do not worship images, we only honour them."
3. **The father hands down "ceremonies"** to those under him. A private devotion becomes a ritual for others.
4. **"In process of time"** the custom grows strong and is **kept as a law**. What was voluntary becomes required.
5. **Kings command it.** Rulers enforce the images, and images of the **king** himself are honoured as if he were present.
6. **Artists make the images more beautiful**, and beauty draws the people into deeper superstition.
7. **The multitude** ends up treating as a god what "a little before was but honoured as a man."

That is the history of image veneration in the church: a few portraits of loved martyrs and of Christ in the fourth century; honour paid to them by popular piety; ceremonies of candles and incense borrowed from the honour paid to the **emperor's image** in the Roman Empire; centuries of custom; then an **empress** and an assembly of bishops making it law with anathemas in 787; and finally a people who kneel, kiss and pray before painted wood and carved stone.

### Praying to what cannot hear

> **Wisdom 13:17-19:** "Then maketh he prayer for his goods, for his wife and children, and is not ashamed to speak to that which hath no life. For health he calleth upon that which is weak: for life prayeth to that which is dead: for aid humbly beseecheth that which hath least means to help: and for a good journey he asketh of that which cannot set a foot forward: And for gaining and getting, and for good success of his hands, asketh ability to do of him, that is most unable to do any thing."

> **Wisdom 14:8:** "But that which is made with hands is cursed, as well it, as he that made it: he, because he made it; and it, because, being corruptible, it was called god."

> **Wisdom 14:27:** "For the worshipping of idols not to be named is the beginning, the cause, and the end, of all evil."

A Catholic or Orthodox reader will answer that Wisdom speaks of pagan gods, not of Christ and the saints. But that is the point: Wisdom shows that idolatry **did not start** as worship of false gods. It started as honour paid to the image of a **real person** — a dead son, a living king — and it grew. The defence "we are honouring real holy people, not false gods" is the very first step Wisdom describes.

# 4. The New Testament: no change after the Incarnation

The Incarnation did not abolish the second commandment. After Christ rose and ascended, the apostles kept warning Gentile converts away from images.

> **Acts 15:20:** "…that they **abstain from pollutions of idols**…" (the first church council)

> **Acts 17:29:** "Forasmuch then as we are the offspring of God, we ought not to think that **the Godhead is like unto gold, or silver, or stone, graven by art and man's device**."

> **Romans 1:22-23, 25:** "Professing themselves to be wise, they became fools, And **changed the glory of the uncorruptible God into an image made like to corruptible man**… Who changed the truth of God into a lie, and worshipped and served the creature more than the Creator."

> **1 Corinthians 10:14:** "Wherefore, my dearly beloved, flee from idolatry."

> **1 John 5:21:** "Little children, keep yourselves from idols."

At Ephesus, the silversmiths who made shrines of Artemis started a riot because Paul was persuading people "that **they be no gods, which are made with hands**" (Acts 19:26). If the apostles had offered converts Christian statues to honour instead, the craftsmen would have had new customers, not a lost trade.

### Bowing to God's servants was refused

Catholic and Orthodox theology says one may give _proskynesis_ (bowing, "veneration") to saints and their images. In the New Testament the apostles and angels **refused** that very act:

> **Acts 10:25-26:** "Cornelius met him, and fell down at his feet, and **worshipped** him [Greek _prosekynēsen_]. But Peter took him up, saying, **Stand up; I myself also am a man**."

> **Revelation 22:8-9:** "I fell down to worship before the feet of the angel… Then saith he unto me, **See thou do it not**: for I am thy fellowservant… **worship God**." (also Revelation 19:10)

If an apostle and an angel in person would not accept a bow, how much less a painting of them?

### Faith, not sight

The New Testament describes Christian worship as worship in Spirit, by faith, of a Christ we do not see:

> **John 4:23-24:** "…the true worshippers shall worship the Father **in spirit and in truth**… God is a Spirit: and they that worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth."

> **2 Corinthians 5:7:** "(For we walk by faith, not by sight:)"

> **1 Peter 1:8:** "Whom **having not seen**, ye love; in whom, though now ye see him not, yet believing, ye rejoice…"

> **John 20:29:** "…**blessed are they that have not seen, and yet have believed**."

### The Incarnation argument

It is said that because God became visible in Christ, He may now be depicted and His image venerated, and that rejecting icons denies the Incarnation. But:

- The apostles **saw** Christ, touched Him and ate with Him, and still made **no portrait** of Him and gave us **no physical description** of Him. They gave us written Gospels. The image of Christ that God preserved for the church is the **word** of those who saw Him (1 John 1:1-3).
- Christ **is** "the image of the invisible God" (Colossians 1:15), and believers are being conformed to His image (Romans 8:29). The living image of God in Scripture is Christ Himself and redeemed people (Genesis 1:27), not wood and paint.
- Paul wrote Acts 17:29 and Romans 1:23 **after** the Incarnation. The Incarnation did not make "art and man's device" a fit way to worship.
- Any painting of Jesus is an **artist's imagination**, not a likeness of the Lord. To bow to it is to bow to someone's guess about a face no one recorded.
- The early church believed in the Incarnation as firmly as anyone, and, as we will see, for three hundred years it had no icons to venerate.

# 5. Before Nicaea (A.D. 100–325): no images to venerate

In the Greco-Roman world, images of the gods were everywhere. A statue was believed to make the god present; people bowed to it, kissed it, crowned it, lit lamps and burned incense before it. Christians refused all of this, and that refusal made them look like **atheists** to their neighbours. When the old bishop Polycarp was arrested (c. 155), the crowd shouted, "Away with the atheists!" (_Martyrdom of Polycarp_ 9).

### The first to honour an image of Christ were heretics

The earliest known group that honoured images of Jesus was not the church but the **Carpocratians**, a Gnostic sect. Irenaeus of Lyons describes them about A.D. 180:

> "They also possess images, some of them painted, and others formed from different kinds of material; while they maintain that a likeness of Christ was made by Pilate at that time when Jesus lived among them. They crown these images, and set them up along with the images of the philosophers of the world… and they have other modes of honouring these images, **after the same manner of the Gentiles**." (_Against Heresies_ 1.25.6)

Irenaeus presents it as a mark of heresy and of paganism, not as a practice of the church.

### The apologists: Christians have no images

**Justin Martyr** (c. 155): "And neither do we honour with many sacrifices and garlands of flowers such deities as men have formed and set in shrines and called gods; since we see that these are **soulless and dead**, and have not the form of God." (_First Apology_ 9)

**Athenagoras** (c. 177) answered the charge of atheism by explaining that Christians distinguish God from matter, and that the images are only the work of craftsmen, used by demons (_Plea for the Christians_ 15–18, 26).

**Minucius Felix** (_Octavius_, c. 200–250) records what pagans said about Christians: "Why have they **no altars, no temples, no acknowledged images**?" (ch. 10). The Christian answers: "What image of God shall I make, since, if you think rightly, **man himself is the image of God**?" (ch. 32).

**Clement of Alexandria** (c. 195–200) quoted the second commandment against image-making: "we are expressly prohibited from exercising a deceptive art: 'For thou shalt not make,' says the prophet, 'the likeness of anything which is in heaven above, or in the earth beneath'" (_Exhortation to the Heathen_ 4). And: "**works of art cannot then be sacred and divine**" (_Stromata_ 7.5).

**Tertullian** (c. 200–210), _On Idolatry_:

> "When the devil introduced into the world artificers of statues and of images, and of every kind of likenesses, that former rude business of human disaster attained from idols both a name and a development. Thenceforth **every art which in any way produces an idol instantly became a fount of idolatry**." (ch. 3)

> "**God prohibits an idol as much to be made as to be worshipped.**" (ch. 4)

**Origen** (c. 248), answering the pagan critic Celsus, who mocked Christians for having no images, quotes the second commandment and says:

> "It is in consideration of these and many other such commands, that they [Christians] **not only avoid temples, altars, and images, but are ready to suffer death** when it is necessary, rather than debase by any such impiety the conception which they have of the Most High God." (_Against Celsus_ 7.64)

Origen adds that Christians do have "statues" and "altars," but spiritual ones: the virtues formed in the soul are the statues, and the heart of a righteous man is the altar (_Against Celsus_ 8.17-18).

**Arnobius** (c. 305) asks the pagans:

> "What greater wrong, disgrace, hardship, can be inflicted than to acknowledge one god, and yet make supplication to something else — to hope for help from a deity, and **pray to an image without feeling**?" (_Against the Heathen_ 6.9)

He compares it to asking "asses and pigs their opinions as to what should be done."

**Lactantius** (c. 310), tutor of Constantine's son Crispus:

> "Wherefore it is undoubted that **there is no religion wherever there is an image**. For if religion consists of divine things, and there is nothing divine except in heavenly things; it follows that images are without religion, because there can be nothing heavenly in that which is made from the earth." (_Divine Institutes_ 2.19)

### A church council forbids pictures in churches

The **Synod of Elvira** in Spain (c. 305) decreed in its 36th canon:

> "It has been decided that **pictures ought not to be in churches**, lest what is worshipped and adored be painted on the walls."

Defenders of icons say this canon was only a precaution during persecution. Even so, it proves the point: in A.D. 305 a church council would not allow pictures in the church building precisely **because** they could become objects of worship. That is the opposite of Nicaea II.

### Summary of the first three centuries

| Date | Witness | Testimony |
|---|---|---|
| c. 155 | Justin Martyr | Images are "soulless and dead"; Christians do not honour them |
| c. 180 | Irenaeus | The only people honouring images of Christ are the heretical Carpocratians, "after the manner of the Gentiles" |
| c. 200–250 | Minucius Felix | Pagans ask why Christians have "no acknowledged images" |
| c. 200 | Clement of Alexandria | "Works of art cannot then be sacred and divine" |
| c. 200–210 | Tertullian | "God prohibits an idol as much to be made as to be worshipped" |
| c. 248 | Origen | Christians "avoid temples, altars, and images" and would rather die |
| c. 305 | Arnobius | To "pray to an image without feeling" is a disgrace |
| c. 305 | Synod of Elvira | No pictures in churches, lest they be worshipped |
| c. 310 | Lactantius | "There is no religion wherever there is an image" |

There is **not one** Christian writer before 325 who teaches that believers should bow to, kiss, or pray before an image. The burial art of the catacombs shows Christians used symbols and Bible scenes; nothing in it shows a cult of images. When the Fathers spoke about images, they spoke against them.

# 6. From Constantine to Gregory the Great (313–600): images arrive, veneration is resisted

### The flood of converts

In 311 the persecution ended (Edict of Galerius); in 313 the Edict of Milan gave Christians full freedom; and in 380 the Edict of Thessalonica made Nicene Christianity the religion of the empire. Within a few generations the church went from a persecuted minority to the religion of the majority, and many who entered brought their old religious habits with them.

One of those habits was the **cult of the emperor's image**. In the Roman Empire the emperor's portrait (the _laurata_) stood in courts and cities; people bowed before it, lit candles and burned incense to it, and treated an insult to the image as an insult to the emperor. This is exactly what Wisdom 14:17 describes: an "express image of a king" honoured "as if he were present." When Basil the Great wrote, "the honour paid to the image passes on to the prototype" (_On the Holy Spirit_ 18.45), his illustration was **the emperor's image**, and his subject was the Trinity, not church pictures. Nicaea II later quoted the line as if Basil had been speaking about icons.

Historian Robin Jensen (_Face to Face: Portraits of the Divine in Early Christianity_, 2005) notes that portraits of Christ, the apostles and the saints appear mostly toward the **end of the fourth century**. Even then, the leaders of the church spoke against their veneration.

### Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 325)

Eusebius, the "father of church history," saw old paintings of Peter, Paul and Christ, and a bronze statue at Caesarea Philippi said to represent Jesus and the woman healed of her bleeding. He explained them as a pagan habit:

> "…the ancients being accustomed, as is likely, **according to a habit of the Gentiles**, to pay this kind of honour indiscriminately to those regarded by them as deliverers." (_Church History_ 7.18)

When Constantia, the emperor Constantine's sister, asked him for an image of Christ, Eusebius refused and rebuked her: Christ's glorified form cannot be painted, and an image of His human form alone would be like the pagans who make images of their gods (_Letter to Constantia_, preserved in later iconoclast and iconophile writings).

### Epiphanius of Salamis (c. 394)

Epiphanius, bishop of Salamis in Cyprus, wrote to John of Jerusalem:

> "I found there a curtain hanging on the doors of the said church, dyed and embroidered. It bore an image either of Christ or of one of the saints; I do not rightly remember whose the image was. Seeing this, and being loath that **an image of a man should be hung up in Christ's church contrary to the teaching of the Scriptures**, I tore it asunder and advised the custodians of the place to use it as a winding sheet for some poor person." (Letter 51.9, in Jerome's Latin translation)

Other writings of Epiphanius against images were quoted by the iconoclasts in the eighth century; Nicaea II declared them forgeries, but most modern scholars since Karl Holl consider them genuine.

### Augustine of Hippo (c. 388–420)

Augustine shows that by his time some people in the church had begun to venerate pictures, and that the church **condemned** it:

> "**I know that there are many worshippers of tombs and pictures.** I know that there are many who drink to great excess over the dead… These the Church condemns, and daily endeavours to correct as bad children." (_On the Morals of the Catholic Church_ 34.75-76, c. 388)

He criticized those who sought Christ and the apostles "painted on walls" rather than in the holy books (_Harmony of the Gospels_ 1.10.16). Preaching on Psalm 115 (113 in the Latin numbering), he explained why images are so dangerous even when people know they are only objects:

> "…the figure resembling the living person, which induces men to worship it, hath more influence in the minds of these miserable persons than the evident fact that it is not living…" (_Expositions on the Psalms_, Psalm 113, sermon 2.5)

And in _The City of God_ (4.31) he approved the Roman scholar Varro, who said that the Romans worshipped their gods **without images** for over 170 years, and that "they who first set up images of the gods for the people diminished reverence and added error."

### Popular piety runs ahead of the church

In the late fourth and fifth centuries popular use of images grew. John Chrysostom mentions people engraving the likeness of the beloved bishop Meletius on rings and cups (c. 386), and Theodoret of Cyrrhus says that in Rome shopkeepers set up small images of Simeon the Stylite at their doors "to provide protection and safety" (c. 440). These are signs of the **development**: images used as protective charms by the people, a century after Constantine, long before any council approved it.

Even in the sixth century bishops still resisted. **Philoxenus of Mabbug** (d. 523) opposed images of angels, of Christ, and of the Holy Spirit as a dove. **Hypatius of Ephesus** (c. 530) allowed images only as a concession "for the simpler" believers, not for worship.

### Pope Gregory the Great (599–600)

When Bishop Serenus of Marseilles saw people **adoring** images and smashed them, Pope Gregory I wrote to him twice:

> "…we commend you indeed for your zeal against anything made with hands being an object of adoration; but we signify to you that you ought not to have broken these images. For pictorial representation is made use of in Churches for this reason; that such as are ignorant of letters may at least read by looking at the walls what they cannot read in books. Your Fraternity therefore should have both preserved the images and **prohibited the people from adoration of them**…" (_Letters_ 9.209)

> "For **to adore a picture is one thing**, but to learn through the story of a picture what is to be adored is another." (_Letters_ 11.10)

This is a decisive witness. Around A.D. 600, **the bishop of Rome** allowed pictures as teaching aids for the illiterate, but **forbade the people to adore them**. If the veneration that Nicaea II required in 787 had been apostolic tradition, Gregory would not have praised Serenus' "zeal against anything made with hands being an object of adoration."

### Churches without images

Many great churches were still without figural images. Justinian's **Hagia Sophia** in Constantinople (537), the greatest church in Christendom, was decorated with crosses and non-figural mosaics; its famous figural mosaics were added only after the end of iconoclasm (the apse Virgin and Child was dedicated in 867). Richard Price notes that some prominent churches in the eighth century still lacked figural decoration.

### The seventh century: the first defenses of veneration

The first writers who explicitly defend bowing before images appear in the **seventh century**, such as Leontius of Neapolis in Cyprus, usually answering Jewish objections that Christians had become idolaters. In the same century, images believed to be "made without hands" (like the Image of Edessa) were carried by Byzantine armies into battle as protectors. The **Council in Trullo** (692) issued a canon (82) on how Christ should be painted. Six centuries after the apostles, image veneration was finally becoming common in the East.

# 7. Iconoclasm and Nicaea II: politics, violence and anathemas

### A civil war over images (726–843)

The eighth and ninth centuries in the Byzantine Empire were a century of conflict over images:

- **First iconoclasm (c. 726/730–787).** Emperor **Leo III** began removing images. His son **Constantine V** persecuted monks who defended icons: Stephen the Younger was killed by a mob in 765, monasteries were confiscated, and monks were paraded in the Hippodrome to be mocked.
- **Council of Hieria (754).** Constantine V called a council of **338 bishops**, which called itself the Seventh Ecumenical Council. It condemned the making and veneration of images of Christ: an image either tries to depict His divine nature, which cannot be depicted, confusing the two natures, or it depicts only His humanity, separating them. It said the only true image of Christ is the Eucharist.
- **Nicaea II (787)** reversed Hieria.
- **Second iconoclasm (815–843).** Emperor **Leo V** restored iconoclasm with a council at Hagia Sophia (815). Under Emperor **Theophilos**, the monks Theodore and Theophanes had verses **tattooed on their faces** (they are remembered as the _Graptoi_, "the written-on"), and the painter Lazarus had his hands burned.
- **The "Triumph of Orthodoxy" (843).** After Theophilos died, his widow **Empress Theodora**, regent for her young son Michael III, restored the icons, deposed the iconoclast patriarch, and installed Methodios. The Orthodox Church still celebrates the event every year on the first Sunday of Lent.

Each side used the power of the state. When the emperor was an iconoclast, the bishops signed against icons; when an empress favoured icons, the bishops signed for them. Whichever council the throne supported became "orthodox."

### Empress Irene and the making of Nicaea II

When Emperor Leo IV died in 780, his widow **Irene** became regent for their nine-year-old son, **Constantine VI**. Irene favoured icons.

- **784:** She made **Tarasios**, the head of the imperial chancellery and **a layman**, patriarch of Constantinople. He was ordained through all the ranks in a matter of days. Pope Hadrian I protested that a layman had been raised directly to patriarch.
- **786:** The council met in the Church of the Holy Apostles in Constantinople, but soldiers of the imperial guard, loyal to the memory of Constantine V, burst in and broke it up.
- **787:** Irene sent those regiments away from the capital on the pretext of a campaign, disbanded them, and called the council again, this time at Nicaea. About 350 bishops attended.
- **Many of the same bishops had been iconoclasts.** In the first session, iconoclast bishops confessed their error and, after repenting, kept their sees. The bishops who had condemned images under one ruler now approved them under another.
- **The "representatives" of the Eastern patriarchs had no mandate.** To give the council the appearance of including the whole church, two monks from the East were presented as the representatives of the Eastern patriarchs (Alexandria, Antioch and Jerusalem). Those patriarchs, under Muslim rule, had not in fact appointed them. Yet Nicaea II rejected Hieria as non-ecumenical precisely because the pope and the Eastern patriarchs had not taken part.
- **Rome's support came with conditions.** Pope Hadrian I sent legates and approved the veneration of images, but his letter also complained about Tarasios' elevation and demanded the return of papal territories and jurisdictions the emperors had taken. Scholars note that the Greek version read at the council left out those complaints.

Historians generally agree that Irene's purpose was political as well as religious: to undo the policy of the iconoclast emperors, win the monks and the pope to her side, and present herself as the champion of orthodoxy. The bishops largely played the part assigned to them. Ten years later, in **797**, Irene had her own son, Constantine VI, **blinded** in the Porphyra, the very room where she had given birth to him, so that she could rule alone; he died of his wounds. In 800, Pope Leo III crowned Charlemagne emperor in Rome, partly on the reasoning that the imperial throne was vacant because it was held by a woman.

This is the council that declared image veneration a dogma for all Christians, and the empress who called it. As Wisdom 14:16 says, images were worshipped "**by the commandments of kings**."

### What Nicaea II decreed

The council's definition (_horos_) says:

> "…that to these should be given due salutation and **honourable reverence** (_aspasmon kai timētikēn proskynēsin_), not indeed that true worship of faith (_latreian_) which pertains alone to the divine nature; but to these, as to the figure of the precious and life-giving Cross and to the Book of the Gospels and to the other holy objects, **incense and lights may be offered according to ancient pious custom**. For the honour which is paid to the image passes on to that which the image represents, and he who reveres the image reveres in it the subject represented."

And it added anathemas:

> "If anyone does not confess that Christ our God can be represented in his humanity, let him be anathema. If anyone does not accept representation in art of evangelical scenes, let him be anathema. **If anyone does not salute such representations as standing for the Lord and his saints, let him be anathema. If anyone rejects any written or unwritten tradition of the Church, let him be anathema.**"

In the acclamations recorded in the council's acts, the bishops cried out:

> "To those who **apply to the sacred images the sayings of divine Scripture against idols**, anathema! To those who do not salute the holy and venerable images, anathema! To those who call the sacred images idols, anathema! To those who knowingly communicate with those who revile and dishonour the venerable images, anathema!"

An anathema is a solemn curse that cuts a person off from the church. The council cursed anyone who applies to images what Scripture says against idols — that is, anyone who reads Exodus 20:5, 2 Kings 18:4, Psalm 115 and Wisdom 14 as applying to bowing before images, as Tertullian, Origen, Lactantius, Epiphanius and Augustine did. Compare Paul's anathema: "though we, or an angel from heaven, preach any other gospel unto you than that which we have preached unto you, **let him be accursed** [_anathema_]" (Galatians 1:8). Paul cursed those who add to the apostolic gospel; Nicaea II cursed those who refused to add to it.

And notice the claim: "**ancient** pious custom." We have seen that the ancient church had no such custom.

### Forgeries, legends and misused texts

Because there was no support for veneration in the first centuries, the council relied heavily on legends and on texts torn from their context:

- **The letter of Jesus to King Abgar of Edessa** and the legend of the miraculous image of Christ sent to Edessa.
- **A miracle story of an icon of Christ at Beirut** that bled when Jews pierced it.
- **A story from John Moschus' _Spiritual Meadow_** read approvingly at the council: a demon promised a monk that he would stop tempting him to impurity if the monk stopped venerating an image of Christ and Mary; the monk's abbot told him it would be **better to enter every brothel in the city** than to refuse to venerate Christ and His Mother in an image.
- **Basil the Great**, quoted as if he were speaking about icons, when he was speaking about the Trinity, using the emperor's image as an illustration.
- **Psalms about God's "face" read as if they were about painted faces**: "Thy face, O Lord, will I seek" (Psalm 27:8; 26:8 in the Douay-Rheims), "All the rich among the people shall entreat thy countenance" (Psalm 45:12; 44:13 in the Douay-Rheims), and "The light of thy countenance, O Lord, is signed upon us" (Psalm 4:6; 4:7 in the Douay-Rheims).

The Frankish theologians of Charlemagne's court rejected these legends and mocked these interpretations.

# 8. The West rejected Nicaea II

Nicaea II was not received by the whole church. The largest part of the Western church rejected its teaching on veneration for decades.

- **Libri Carolini (c. 790–793).** Charlemagne's court theologian **Theodulf of Orléans** wrote a long refutation of Nicaea II. The Frankish position was clear: images may be kept as decoration or as reminders of history, but they must **neither be adored nor destroyed**, and no one may be forced to venerate them. It attacked the council's use of forgeries, legends and misread Psalms.
- **Council of Frankfurt (794).** Charlemagne summoned the bishops of the West, with papal legates present. Its second canon reads: "A question was brought forward concerning the recent synod of the Greeks… in which it was written that whoever should not pay to the images of the saints service or adoration as to the divine Trinity, should be judged anathema: our most holy fathers above mentioned, **refusing in every way such adoration and service, despised it and unanimously condemned it**." In fairness, the Franks worked from a poor Latin translation which made one bishop's statement sound as if Nicaea II required the same adoration for images as for the Trinity. But their basic position — no adoration of images at all — rejected what Nicaea II actually taught. And the council refused to call Nicaea II ecumenical.
- **Council of Paris (825).** Under Louis the Pious, the Frankish bishops again rejected the veneration of images and criticized Pope Hadrian's support for Nicaea II.
- **Claudius of Turin** (c. 820s), a bishop in northern Italy, removed images from his churches. **Agobard of Lyon** wrote _On Pictures and Images_ (c. 825) against their veneration. **Jonas of Orléans** wrote _On the Veneration of Images_ (c. 840) against Claudius, yet he too rejected adoration of images.

The popes supported Nicaea II, but according to standard accounts the papacy did not formally confirm the council's decrees until **880**, and it was only gradually counted as the "seventh ecumenical council" in the West. A full Latin translation of its acts, by Anastasius the Librarian, appeared only in 873. Image veneration entered Western church law through Gratian's _Decretum_ (c. 1140), and spread through Western churches between the eleventh and thirteenth centuries.

# 9. The doctrine kept developing

Even after Nicaea II, the practice did not stand still.

- **Nicaea II (787)** said images receive "honourable reverence," **not** _latreia_, the worship due to God alone.
- **Thomas Aquinas** (c. 1270) went further: since the honour passes to the prototype, "the same reverence should be shown to Christ's image as to Christ Himself," and therefore "**the image of Christ should be adored with the adoration of _latria_**" — the worship due to God (_Summa Theologiae_ III, q. 25, a. 3). What Nicaea II forbade, the most influential theologian of the Middle Ages taught.
- **The Council of Trent** (1563) decreed that images of Christ, Mary and the saints are to be kept especially in churches and given "due honour and veneration," "so that **by the images which we kiss, and before which we uncover the head, and prostrate ourselves**, we adore Christ; and we venerate the saints, whose similitude they bear" (Session 25).

Compare Trent's words with God's: "**Thou shalt not bow down thyself to them**" (Exodus 20:5). This is what a human development looks like: forbidden by Moses, rejected by the Fathers, condemned as adoration by Augustine and Gregory, required by an empress's council, rejected by the West, and finally intensified by medieval theology and Trent.

# 10. "Ecumenical" is selective

Catholics and Orthodox appeal to the "ecumenical councils" as if they were a fixed, obvious list recognized from the beginning. They were not. "Ecumenical" (from _oikoumenē_, "the inhabited world," meaning the empire) was a title councils claimed for themselves, and which later generations granted or denied depending on whether they agreed with the result.

### Different churches, different lists

- The **Assyrian Church of the East** accepts **2** ecumenical councils.
- The **Oriental Orthodox** churches (Coptic, Armenian, Ethiopian, Syriac) accept **3**.
- The **Eastern Orthodox** accept **7**.
- The **Roman Catholic Church** counts **21**.

Each communion says the others rejected true councils.

### Big councils that were erased

- **Rimini and Seleucia (359).** More than 400 Western bishops met at Rimini, more than at Nicaea in 325, and under imperial pressure they signed a creed that abandoned Nicaea's language. Jerome wrote: "**The whole world groaned, and was astonished to find itself Arian**" (_Dialogue against the Luciferians_ 19). It is not counted.
- **Ephesus II (449).** Called by Emperor Theodosius II as a general council, it was dominated by Dioscorus of Alexandria; Bishop Flavian of Constantinople was beaten and died soon after. Pope Leo called it a "robber synod" (_latrocinium_), and it was overturned at Chalcedon two years later.
- **Hieria (754).** 338 bishops, called by the emperor, calling itself the Seventh Ecumenical Council. Erased and replaced by Nicaea II.
- **Hagia Sophia (815).** Confirmed Hieria. Erased in 843.

### Councils that were accepted, but only after the fact or only in part

- **Ephesus I (431).** Cyril of Alexandria opened the council on June 22 **before** John of Antioch and the Syrian bishops arrived, over the protest of 68 bishops and of the emperor's representative, and deposed Nestorius the same day. When John arrived he held a rival council and deposed Cyril. The emperor first confirmed **both** depositions and had Cyril arrested. Cyril's side then sent lavish "blessings" to officials at the court — a surviving letter from his archdeacon lists carpets, ivory chairs, ostrich eggs and large amounts of gold — and the decision turned in his favour. Ephesus I and Ephesus II were called the same way and run the same way; one is "ecumenical" and the other a "robber synod" because of which side later won.
- **Constantinople I (381).** No Western bishops attended. It was recognized as ecumenical only decades later, and Rome rejected its third canon (placing Constantinople second after Rome) until the Fourth Lateran Council in 1215.
- **Chalcedon (451).** Pope Leo accepted its doctrine but rejected its 28th canon, which gave Constantinople privileges equal to Rome's.
- **Constantinople II (553).** Emperor Justinian held Pope Vigilius in Constantinople; Vigilius refused to attend, and the council struck his name from the diptychs. Later he gave in and approved it. Several Western churches (Milan, Aquileia) broke communion with Rome over it for about 150 years.
- **Constantinople III (680–681)** condemned **Pope Honorius I** as a heretic, and Pope Leo II confirmed the condemnation.
- **The Council in Trullo (692)** issued 102 canons that the Orthodox treat as completing the fifth and sixth councils. Rome never fully accepted them.
- **Constantinople IV.** Rome counts the council of **869–870**, which deposed Patriarch Photius, as the eighth ecumenical council. The council of **879–880**, attended by legates of Pope John VIII, restored Photius and annulled it; many Orthodox regard 879–880 as the eighth. Rome began to count 869–870 as ecumenical only in the late eleventh century, when canonists found its canons useful.
- **Constance (1414–1418)** ended the Great Schism, when three men claimed to be pope at once, and declared in _Haec Sancta_ that a general council holds its power directly from Christ and that even a pope must obey it. Rome counts the council as ecumenical but rejects that decree.
- Cardinal **Robert Bellarmine** (c. 1590) had to sort the councils into those "approved," "rejected," and "partly approved, partly rejected" (_On Councils_ 1.5–7).

### The double standard on Nicaea II

Nicaea II rejected Hieria as a false council because the pope did not cooperate and the patriarchs of the East were not represented. But Nicaea II's own Eastern "representatives" had not been sent by their patriarchs, and the largest church of the West, at Frankfurt, rejected it. The criteria were applied to the losing council and waived for the winning one.

In practice, "ecumenical" means: **the council whose decisions the side that won later accepted**. That is not a reason to bind the conscience of every Christian under pain of anathema. Only Scripture is "given by inspiration of God" (2 Timothy 3:16), and every council must be judged by it. As the Thirty-Nine Articles put it, general councils "may err, and sometimes have erred, even in things pertaining unto God."

# 11. Common objections

**"We don't worship images; we venerate them. Latria is for God, dulia for saints."** The second commandment forbids **bowing** (_proskynēsis_), not only _latreia_ (Exodus 20:5 in the Greek). Peter and the angel in Revelation refused the bow itself (Acts 10:25-26; Revelation 22:8-9). Wisdom 14:15 shows idolatry begins with images that are only "honoured." Hezekiah destroyed the bronze serpent for incense, not for full-blown worship (2 Kings 18:4). And Aquinas himself taught _latria_ to the image of Christ.

**"Images are books for the illiterate."** Gregory the Great said that while **forbidding** adoration of them. The prophets call a graven image "a teacher of lies" (Habakkuk 2:18). God gave His people a **book** and teachers to explain it (Nehemiah 8:8), and when the Bible was put back in ordinary people's hands, they learned to read (see **Who gave us the Bible?**).

**"The early Christians had art in the catacombs."** Yes: symbols and Bible scenes in burial places. Art is not veneration. There is no evidence of anyone bowing to, kissing or praying before these paintings, and the Christian writers of the same period said plainly that Christians have no images to worship.

**"Rejecting icons denies the Incarnation."** Then the apostles, who saw Christ and made no images, and the Fathers of the first three centuries, who believed in the Incarnation and had no icons to venerate, all denied the Incarnation. The argument proves too much. See section 4.

**"God commanded the cherubim and the bronze serpent."** God commanded them; no one venerated them; and when the serpent received incense, it was destroyed with God's approval. See section 2.

**"The Elvira canon was only a precaution during persecution."** Perhaps. Even so, its reason was that what is painted on the walls could become an object of worship — the opposite of a church that commands bowing before images.

# Conclusion

The veneration of images is not apostolic. The Law forbade bowing to images, and God judged Israel when it used an image to worship Him. The apostles warned Gentile believers away from "gods made with hands" and refused even to be bowed to themselves. For three hundred years the church had no images to venerate; its writers mocked the pagan images, and the only people known to honour an image of Christ were heretics acting "after the manner of the Gentiles." Images entered the churches after Constantine, carried in by the crowds of converts and by the custom of honouring the emperor's image, and for centuries bishops from Epiphanius to Augustine to Pope Gregory the Great condemned their adoration.

Veneration became a dogma only in 787, at a council called by an empress who later blinded her own son, presided over by a layman she had made patriarch, propped up by legends and forged texts, with Eastern "representatives" no one had sent, reversing a council of 338 bishops, and rejected for decades by the church of the West. Later theology went further still, until Trent required Christians to kiss and prostrate themselves before images. The Book of Wisdom, which Rome itself calls Scripture, had already described the whole process: a beloved image, honour, ceremonies passed down, a custom that hardens into law, the commandments of kings, the skill of artists, and a multitude that ends up giving to wood and stone the honour that belongs to God alone.

"Little children, keep yourselves from idols. Amen." (1 John 5:21)

"I am the LORD: that is my name: and my glory will I not give to another, neither my praise to graven images." (Isaiah 42:8)
