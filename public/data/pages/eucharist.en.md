## The claim

The Roman Catholic Church teaches that at the words of consecration the bread and wine stop being bread and wine. Their whole **substance** is changed into the body and blood of Christ, and only the **appearances** (taste, color, shape) of bread and wine remain. The Fourth Lateran Council (1215) called this change **transubstantiation**, and the Council of Trent (1551) confirmed it. The _Catechism_ teaches:

- "the body and blood, together with the soul and divinity, of our Lord Jesus Christ and, therefore, the whole Christ is truly, really, and substantially contained" in the Eucharist (_CCC_ 1374).
- "The sacrifice of Christ and the sacrifice of the Eucharist are one single sacrifice", offered "in an unbloody manner" and "truly propitiatory" (_CCC_ 1367, quoting Trent, Session 22).
- "The Catholic Church has always offered and still offers to the sacrament of the Eucharist the cult of adoration" (_CCC_ 1378). Trent pronounced anathema on anyone who says that Christ in the sacrament "is not to be adored with the worship of _latria_", the worship due to God alone (Session 13, canon 6).

This page tests that teaching against Scripture and history. In short:

- **Hebrews** says Christ offered **one** sacrifice, **once for all**, and that where sins are forgiven "there is no more offering for sin."
- A sacrifice for sins requires **the shedding of blood**, so an "unbloody" sacrifice cannot take away sin.
- God **forbade drinking blood** before the Law, under the Law, and after the Law, to Gentile Christians as well.
- At the table Jesus spoke in **figures**, as He did throughout His ministry, and He called the cup "this fruit of the vine" after He had said "this is my blood."
- In **John 6**, "eating" is defined as **coming** to Jesus and "drinking" as **believing** on Him.
- The Lord's Supper is a **memorial**, a fellowship meal of believers. The church has no altar, no sacrificing priests and no victim, only a **royal priesthood** offering spiritual sacrifices.
- The early Fathers did not teach transubstantiation. The word was coined in the twelfth century, defined in 1215 by the same council that branded Jews and ordered rulers to drive out heretics, and enforced with fire.

Jesus has already finished the work:

> **Hebrews 10:10-14:** "By the which will we are sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all. And every priest standeth daily ministering and offering oftentimes the same sacrifices, which can never take away sins: But this man, after he had offered one sacrifice for sins for ever, sat down on the right hand of God; From henceforth expecting till his enemies be made his footstool. For by one offering he hath perfected for ever them that are sanctified."

# 1. One sacrifice, once for all

The book of **Hebrews** was written to Jewish believers tempted to go back to the temple and its daily sacrifices. Its whole argument is that Christ's sacrifice is different in kind: it was offered **once**, it is **finished**, and it is never repeated.

The writer uses two Greek words again and again: **ἅπαξ** (_hapax_), "once," and the stronger **ἐφάπαξ** (_ephapax_), "once for all."

- Christ "needeth not daily, as those high priests, to offer up sacrifice… for this he did **once** (ἐφάπαξ), when he offered up himself" (7:27).
- "by his own blood he entered in **once** (ἐφάπαξ) into the holy place, having obtained eternal redemption" (9:12).
- "Nor yet that he should offer himself **often**… for then must he often have suffered since the foundation of the world: but now **once** (ἅπαξ) in the end of the world hath he appeared to put away sin by the sacrifice of himself" (9:25-26).
- "Christ was **once** (ἅπαξ) offered to bear the sins of many" (9:28).
- "we are sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ **once for all** (ἐφάπαξ)" (10:10).

> **Hebrews 7:26-27:** "For such an high priest became us, who is holy, harmless, undefiled, separate from sinners, and made higher than the heavens; Who needeth not daily, as those high priests, to offer up sacrifice, first for his own sins, and then for the people’s: for this he did once, when he offered up himself."

> **Hebrews 9:24-28:** "For Christ is not entered into the holy places made with hands, which are the figures of the true; but into heaven itself, now to appear in the presence of God for us: Nor yet that he should offer himself often, as the high priest entereth into the holy place every year with blood of others; For then must he often have suffered since the foundation of the world: but now once in the end of the world hath he appeared to put away sin by the sacrifice of himself. And as it is appointed unto men once to die, but after this the judgment: So Christ was once offered to bear the sins of many; and unto them that look for him shall he appear the second time without sin unto salvation."

Hebrews draws the contrast that the Mass reverses. The Levitical priest **stands daily**, offering the same sacrifices that "can never take away sins." Christ offered **one** sacrifice and **sat down**: the work is done. Every Catholic priest stands at an altar daily to offer the sacrifice again.

> **Hebrews 10:11-12:** "And every priest standeth daily ministering and offering oftentimes the same sacrifices, which can never take away sins: But this man, after he had offered one sacrifice for sins for ever, sat down on the right hand of God;"

And the conclusion of the argument closes the door completely: once sins are forgiven, there is **no more offering** for sin.

> **Hebrews 10:17-18:** "And their sins and iniquities will I remember no more. Now where remission of these is, there is no more offering for sin."

Rome answers that the Mass does not add a new sacrifice but "re-presents" the one sacrifice of the cross. But Trent itself calls the Mass "truly propitiatory" and says it is "offered for the sins, punishments, satisfactions and other necessities of the faithful who are living, and for those who are departed in Christ" (Session 22, ch. 2). A sacrifice offered again and again for sins is exactly what Hebrews says no longer exists.

# 2. Without shedding of blood, no remission

Trent says that in the Mass "the same Christ who once offered Himself in a bloody manner on the altar of the cross, is contained and immolated in an **unbloody** manner" (Session 22, ch. 2). But Scripture states the rule of every sacrifice for sin:

> **Hebrews 9:22:** "And almost all things are by the law purged with blood; and without shedding of blood is no remission."

> **Leviticus 17:11:** "For the life of the flesh is in the blood: and I have given it to you upon the altar to make an atonement for your souls: for it is the blood that maketh an atonement for the soul."

If the Mass is unbloody, it cannot remit sins. If it remits sins, it must shed blood, and then Christ suffers again, which Hebrews says is impossible (9:25-26). Either way, the claim collapses. Forgiveness comes from the blood shed **once** on the cross, received by faith:

> **Romans 3:24-25:** "Being justified freely by his grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus: Whom God hath set forth to be a propitiation through faith in his blood, to declare his righteousness for the remission of sins that are past, through the forbearance of God;"

> **1 John 1:7:** "But if we walk in the light, as he is in the light, we have fellowship one with another, and the blood of Jesus Christ his Son cleanseth us from all sin."

> **Revelation 1:5:** "And from Jesus Christ, who is the faithful witness, and the first begotten of the dead, and the prince of the kings of the earth. Unto him that loved us, and washed us from our sins in his own blood,"

# 3. Drinking blood was forbidden before, during and after the Law

**Before the Law**, God gave Noah, the father of all nations, permission to eat meat, but not its blood:

> **Genesis 9:3-4:** "Every moving thing that liveth shall be meat for you; even as the green herb have I given you all things. But flesh with the life thereof, which is the blood thereof, shall ye not eat."

**Under the Law**, the ban was repeated many times, with the reason: the life is in the blood, and the blood is given for atonement on the altar, not for food. Whoever ate blood was to be cut off.

> **Leviticus 17:10-12:** "And whatsoever man there be of the house of Israel, or of the strangers that sojourn among you, that eateth any manner of blood; I will even set my face against that soul that eateth blood, and will cut him off from among his people. For the life of the flesh is in the blood: and I have given it to you upon the altar to make an atonement for your souls: for it is the blood that maketh an atonement for the soul. Therefore I said unto the children of Israel, No soul of you shall eat blood, neither shall any stranger that sojourneth among you eat blood."

> **Leviticus 17:14:** "For it is the life of all flesh; the blood of it is for the life thereof: therefore I said unto the children of Israel, Ye shall eat the blood of no manner of flesh: for the life of all flesh is the blood thereof: whosoever eateth it shall be cut off."

> **Deuteronomy 12:23:** "Only be sure that thou eat not the blood: for the blood is the life; and thou mayest not eat the life with the flesh."

**After the Law**, when the apostles decided what to require of Gentile believers, who were free from the Law of Moses, they still kept the prohibition of blood. They wrote it three times:

> **Acts 15:19-20:** "Wherefore my sentence is, that we trouble not them, which from among the Gentiles are turned to God: But that we write unto them, that they abstain from pollutions of idols, and from fornication, and from things strangled, and from blood."

> **Acts 15:28-29:** "For it seemed good to the Holy Ghost, and to us, to lay upon you no greater burden than these necessary things; That ye abstain from meats offered to idols, and from blood, and from things strangled, and from fornication: from which if ye keep yourselves, ye shall do well. Fare ye well."

> **Acts 21:25:** "As touching the Gentiles which believe, we have written and concluded that they observe no such thing, save only that they keep themselves from things offered to idols, and from blood, and from strangled, and from fornication."

If the apostles had been drinking literal blood at every Lord's Supper, they could not have written that decree. And for a Jew of the first century the idea was **repulsive**. Years after the Last Supper, Peter could still say that he had never eaten anything unclean:

> **Acts 10:14:** "But Peter said, Not so, Lord; for I have never eaten any thing that is common or unclean."

When Jesus spoke of eating His flesh and drinking His blood in John 6, many of His disciples were offended and left Him, because they heard it literally. Jesus then explained that He was speaking of spirit and life (see section 5).

> **John 6:60-61:** "Many therefore of his disciples, when they had heard this, said, This is an hard saying; who can hear it? When Jesus knew in himself that his disciples murmured at it, he said unto them, Doth this offend you?"

> **John 6:66:** "From that time many of his disciples went back, and walked no more with him."

# 4. What Jesus said at the table

## "This fruit of the vine"

After Jesus said "this is my blood," He called what was in the cup "**this fruit of the vine**." In Matthew and Mark, He says it **after** the words of institution. Even at the moment Catholics say the change had taken place, Jesus still called it wine.

> **Matthew 26:27-29:** "And he took the cup, and gave thanks, and gave it to them, saying, Drink ye all of it; For this is my blood of the new testament, which is shed for many for the remission of sins. But I say unto you, I will not drink henceforth of this fruit of the vine, until that day when I drink it new with you in my Father’s kingdom."

> **Mark 14:23-25:** "And he took the cup, and when he had given thanks, he gave it to them: and they all drank of it. And he said unto them, This is my blood of the new testament, which is shed for many. Verily I say unto you, I will drink no more of the fruit of the vine, until that day that I drink it new in the kingdom of God."

Paul does the same. After the consecration, he still calls it **bread**: "as often as ye eat **this bread**," "whosoever shall eat **this bread**," "let him eat of **that bread**."

> **1 Corinthians 11:26-28:** "For as often as ye eat this bread, and drink this cup, ye do shew the Lord’s death till he come. Wherefore whosoever shall eat this bread, and drink this cup of the Lord, unworthily, shall be guilty of the body and blood of the Lord. But let a man examine himself, and so let him eat of that bread, and drink of that cup."

> **1 Corinthians 10:16-17:** "The cup of blessing which we bless, is it not the communion of the blood of Christ? The bread which we break, is it not the communion of the body of Christ? For we being many are one bread, and one body: for we are all partakers of that one bread."

## Jesus was holding His own body

When Jesus said "This is my body," His body was sitting at the table, alive and whole, holding the bread in its hands. His blood was in His veins. He had not yet been crucified. The disciples could not have understood Him to mean that the bread in His hand was the same body that was holding it. They understood Him as every Jew at a Passover table understood the food in front of him: as signs of something else.

## "This is" means "this represents"

The Bible regularly uses "is" for "represents," and nobody takes it literally:

- "The seven good kine **are** seven years" (Genesis 41:26).
- "the seven stars **are** the angels of the seven churches" (Revelation 1:20).
- "that Rock **was** Christ" (1 Corinthians 10:4).
- "I **am** the door" (John 10:9); "I **am** the true vine" (John 15:1).
- At the same supper: "This **cup is** the new testament in my blood" (Luke 22:20). No one claims that the cup literally became a covenant.

> **Genesis 41:26:** "The seven good kine are seven years; and the seven good ears are seven years: the dream is one."

> **1 Corinthians 10:4:** "And did all drink the same spiritual drink: for they drank of that spiritual Rock that followed them: and that Rock was Christ."

> **Luke 22:19-20:** "And he took bread, and gave thanks, and brake it, and gave unto them, saying, This is my body which is given for you: this do in remembrance of me. Likewise also the cup after supper, saying, This cup is the new testament in my blood, which is shed for you."

## It was a Passover, and the Passover was a memorial

The Last Supper was a **Passover** meal. In the Law, the lamb itself was called "the LORD's passover," though it was an animal and the Passover was an event, and the day was given "for a **memorial**." Jesus took that memorial meal and gave it a new meaning: "this do in **remembrance** of me."

> **Exodus 12:11:** "And thus shall ye eat it; with your loins girded, your shoes on your feet, and your staff in your hand; and ye shall eat it in haste: it is the LORD’s passover."

> **Exodus 12:14:** "And this day shall be unto you for a memorial; and ye shall keep it a feast to the LORD throughout your generations; ye shall keep it a feast by an ordinance for ever."

> **Luke 22:15:** "And he said unto them, With desire I have desired to eat this passover with you before I suffer:"

> **1 Corinthians 11:24-25:** "And when he had given thanks, he brake it, and said, Take, eat: this is my body, which is broken for you: this do in remembrance of me. After the same manner also he took the cup, when he had supped, saying, This cup is the new testament in my blood: this do ye, as oft as ye drink it, in remembrance of me."

# 5. John 6

John 6 is the main text Catholics use. But read in order, the chapter explains itself.

## It was a year before the Last Supper

The chapter takes place when "the passover, a feast of the Jews, was nigh" (6:4), a full year before the Last Supper. There was no Eucharist yet. Yet Jesus speaks in the **present** tense: "he that believeth on me **hath** everlasting life." His hearers could obey Him that very day.

## Jesus defines eating as coming and drinking as believing

The crowd wanted more miraculous bread. Jesus told them not to labor for food that perishes, and when they asked what work God required, He answered: **believe**. Then He defined His terms:

> **John 6:27-29:** "Labour not for the meat which perisheth, but for that meat which endureth unto everlasting life, which the Son of man shall give unto you: for him hath God the Father sealed. Then said they unto him, What shall we do, that we might work the works of God? Jesus answered and said unto them, This is the work of God, that ye believe on him whom he hath sent."

> **John 6:35:** "And Jesus said unto them, I am the bread of life: he that cometh to me shall never hunger; and he that believeth on me shall never thirst."

"He that **cometh** to me shall never **hunger**; and he that **believeth** on me shall never **thirst**." Hunger is satisfied by coming; thirst is satisfied by believing. Compare the promises: the results of "believing" and of "eating" are identical, word for word:

> **John 6:40:** "And this is the will of him that sent me, that every one which seeth the Son, and believeth on him, may have everlasting life: and I will raise him up at the last day."

> **John 6:47:** "Verily, verily, I say unto you, He that believeth on me hath everlasting life."

> **John 6:54:** "Whoso eateth my flesh, and drinketh my blood, hath eternal life; and I will raise him up at the last day."

Believing gives everlasting life and resurrection at the last day. Eating His flesh gives everlasting life and resurrection at the last day. They are the same act described two ways.

## "The flesh profiteth nothing"

When His disciples were offended, Jesus explained how His words were to be understood:

> **John 6:61-63:** "When Jesus knew in himself that his disciples murmured at it, he said unto them, Doth this offend you? What and if ye shall see the Son of man ascend up where he was before? It is the spirit that quickeneth; the flesh profiteth nothing: the words that I speak unto you, they are spirit, and they are life."

If Jesus meant that His literal flesh must be eaten, He would not have said "the flesh profiteth nothing." He said His **words** are spirit and life. Peter understood: "thou hast the **words** of eternal life" (6:68).

## Jesus' food was doing the Father's will

Jesus had already used the same figure with His disciples in Samaria. When they urged Him to eat, He said He had "meat to eat that ye know not of," and they wondered if someone had brought Him food. He explained:

> **John 4:32-34:** "But he said unto them, I have meat to eat that ye know not of. Therefore said the disciples one to another, Hath any man brought him ought to eat? Jesus saith unto them, My meat is to do the will of him that sent me, and to finish his work."

His food was to do the Father's will. Our food is to receive Him and live by Him: "he that eateth me, even he shall live **by me**" (6:57), just as He lived by the Father, not by eating the Father.

## What a literal reading would mean

If John 6:53-56 were literal:

- **Everyone** who eats the host would have eternal life and would dwell in Christ (6:54, 56), including unbelievers, the wicked, and Judas, who ate with Jesus at the Last Supper and then betrayed Him (Luke 22:19-21; John 13:26-27).
- **No one** who never ate it could have life (6:53), including the thief on the cross, to whom Jesus promised paradise (Luke 23:43), and every believer who died before the Supper was instituted.
- The bread "came down from heaven" (6:51, 58). Jesus' **flesh** did not literally come down from heaven; it was formed in Mary's womb. The statement is about who He is, not about the substance of His body.

## John's readers are trained to see past the literal

John's Gospel is full of people who take Jesus literally and miss His meaning:

- "Destroy this temple" was taken to mean Herod's temple, "but he spake of the temple of his body" (2:19-21).
- Nicodemus asked how a man could enter his mother's womb a second time (3:4).
- The Samaritan woman thought "living water" meant water from a well (4:11-15).
- The disciples thought Jesus had been brought food (4:33).
- "Lazarus sleepeth": the disciples thought He meant natural sleep (11:11-13).

John 6 is one more example: the crowd heard "eat my flesh" literally, and many left.

## Seven "I AM"s

John builds his Gospel around seven "I AM" sayings of Jesus:

> **John 6:35:** "And Jesus said unto them, I am the bread of life: he that cometh to me shall never hunger; and he that believeth on me shall never thirst."

> **John 8:12:** "Then spake Jesus again unto them, saying, I am the light of the world: he that followeth me shall not walk in darkness, but shall have the light of life."

> **John 10:9:** "I am the door: by me if any man enter in, he shall be saved, and shall go in and out, and find pasture."

> **John 10:11:** "I am the good shepherd: the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep."

> **John 11:25:** "Jesus said unto her, I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live:"

> **John 14:6:** "Jesus saith unto him, I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me."

> **John 15:1:** "I am the true vine, and my Father is the husbandman."

Jesus is not a loaf, a lamp, a wooden door, a shepherd of sheep, a road, or a grapevine. It would be violence to the text to read six of these as figures and only one, "I am the bread of life," as literal.

# 6. Jesus taught in figures on purpose

## The leaven of the Pharisees

In Matthew 16, Jesus warned the disciples to "beware of the leaven of the Pharisees." They thought He meant literal bread, because they had forgotten to bring any. Jesus **rebuked** them for thinking literally, and then they understood that He meant the **doctrine** of the Pharisees:

> **Matthew 16:6-12:** "Then Jesus said unto them, Take heed and beware of the leaven of the Pharisees and of the Sadducees. And they reasoned among themselves, saying, It is because we have taken no bread. Which when Jesus perceived, he said unto them, O ye of little faith, why reason ye among yourselves, because ye have brought no bread? Do ye not yet understand, neither remember the five loaves of the five thousand, and how many baskets ye took up? Neither the seven loaves of the four thousand, and how many baskets ye took up? How is it that ye do not understand that I spake it not to you concerning bread, that ye should beware of the leaven of the Pharisees and of the Sadducees? Then understood they how that he bade them not beware of the leaven of bread, but of the doctrine of the Pharisees and of the Sadducees."

The disciples' mistake in Matthew 16 is the same mistake as the crowd's in John 6: taking Jesus' words about bread literally, when He was speaking of teaching and faith.

## Parables reveal to the seeker and hide from the careless

Jesus taught in parables deliberately. To those who sought Him, the parables opened the mysteries of the kingdom; to those who did not care to understand, they were a judgment. "Whosoever hath, to him shall be given": the one who receives the little light he has is given more, and the one who neglects it loses even what he seems to have.

> **Matthew 13:10-13:** "And the disciples came, and said unto him, Why speakest thou unto them in parables? He answered and said unto them, Because it is given unto you to know the mysteries of the kingdom of heaven, but to them it is not given. For whosoever hath, to him shall be given, and he shall have more abundance: but whosoever hath not, from him shall be taken away even that he hath. Therefore speak I to them in parables: because they seeing see not; and hearing they hear not, neither do they understand."

> **Mark 4:33-34:** "And with many such parables spake he the word unto them, as they were able to hear it. But without a parable spake he not unto them: and when they were alone, he expounded all things to his disciples."

> **Luke 8:18:** "Take heed therefore how ye hear: for whosoever hath, to him shall be given; and whosoever hath not, from him shall be taken even that which he seemeth to have."

God rewards those who seek Him diligently. Those who stopped at the literal surface of John 6 "went back, and walked no more with him"; those who stayed received the explanation.

> **Proverbs 25:2:** "It is the glory of God to conceal a thing: but the honour of kings is to search out a matter."

> **Hebrews 11:6:** "But without faith it is impossible to please him: for he that cometh to God must believe that he is, and that he is a rewarder of them that diligently seek him."

> **Jeremiah 29:13:** "And ye shall seek me, and find me, when ye shall search for me with all your heart."

# 7. A memorial meal in the church of Acts

Jesus said "this do in **remembrance** of me," and Paul explains its purpose: to "shew the Lord's death till he come." It looks **back** to the cross and **forward** to His return. It does not repeat the cross.

> **Luke 22:19:** "And he took bread, and gave thanks, and brake it, and gave unto them, saying, This is my body which is given for you: this do in remembrance of me."

> **1 Corinthians 11:23-26:** "For I have received of the Lord that which also I delivered unto you, That the Lord Jesus the same night in which he was betrayed took bread: And when he had given thanks, he brake it, and said, Take, eat: this is my body, which is broken for you: this do in remembrance of me. After the same manner also he took the cup, when he had supped, saying, This cup is the new testament in my blood: this do ye, as oft as ye drink it, in remembrance of me. For as often as ye eat this bread, and drink this cup, ye do shew the Lord’s death till he come."

In the book of Acts, the Lord's Supper was not a complex ritual performed by a priest at an altar. Believers broke bread together **in their homes**, as part of a shared meal, with gladness and simplicity of heart:

> **Acts 2:42:** "And they continued stedfastly in the apostles’ doctrine and fellowship, and in breaking of bread, and in prayers."

> **Acts 2:46-47:** "And they, continuing daily with one accord in the temple, and breaking bread from house to house, did eat their meat with gladness and singleness of heart, Praising God, and having favour with all the people. And the Lord added to the church daily such as should be saved."

> **Acts 20:7:** "And upon the first day of the week, when the disciples came together to break bread, Paul preached unto them, ready to depart on the morrow; and continued his speech until midnight."

Paul calls the cup and the bread a **communion** (κοινωνία, _koinōnia_), a sharing, and explains: "we being many are **one bread, and one body**." Eating together shows that the believers are one body in Christ.

> **1 Corinthians 10:16-17:** "The cup of blessing which we bless, is it not the communion of the blood of Christ? The bread which we break, is it not the communion of the body of Christ? For we being many are one bread, and one body: for we are all partakers of that one bread."

# 8. "Not discerning the Lord's body"

Catholics read 1 Corinthians 11:29, "not discerning the Lord's body," as a failure to recognize Christ's literal body in the host. But the context is about how the Corinthians were treating **one another**:

- There were **divisions** among them when they came together (11:18).
- Each one went ahead with his own supper: "one is hungry, and another is drunken" (11:21).
- The rich were **despising the church of God** and shaming "them that have not" (11:22).
- Paul's solution is not about the bread at all: "when ye come together to eat, **tarry one for another**" (11:33).

> **1 Corinthians 11:18-22:** "For first of all, when ye come together in the church, I hear that there be divisions among you; and I partly believe it. For there must be also heresies among you, that they which are approved may be made manifest among you. When ye come together therefore into one place, this is not to eat the Lord’s supper. For in eating every one taketh before other his own supper: and one is hungry, and another is drunken. What? have ye not houses to eat and to drink in? or despise ye the church of God, and shame them that have not? What shall I say to you? shall I praise you in this? I praise you not."

> **1 Corinthians 11:33-34:** "Wherefore, my brethren, when ye come together to eat, tarry one for another. And if any man hunger, let him eat at home; that ye come not together unto condemnation. And the rest will I set in order when I come."

The "body" they failed to discern is the **church**, the body of Christ, which Paul has just explained ("we being many are one bread, and one body," 10:17) and which he explains in the very next chapter: "Now ye are the body of Christ" (12:27). The oldest Greek manuscripts read simply "the body," as modern translations show:

> **1 Corinthians 11:29 (BSB):** "For anyone who eats and drinks without recognizing the body eats and drinks judgment on himself."

> **1 Corinthians 12:12-13:** "For as the body is one, and hath many members, and all the members of that one body, being many, are one body: so also is Christ. For by one Spirit are we all baptized into one body, whether we be Jews or Gentiles, whether we be bond or free; and have been all made to drink into one Spirit."

> **1 Corinthians 12:27:** "Now ye are the body of Christ, and members in particular."

**Augustine** read it the same way. Speaking to the newly baptized about the bread on the altar, he said: "If you are the body and members of Christ, it is your own mystery that is placed on the Lord's table… Be what you see, and receive what you are" (_Sermon_ 272).

# 9. A royal priesthood with spiritual sacrifices

## Every believer is a priest

The New Testament never calls a Christian minister a **priest** (ἱερεύς, _hiereus_). The leaders of the church are **elders** (πρεσβύτεροι, _presbyteroi_) and **overseers** (ἐπίσκοποι, _episkopoi_). The only priests in the church are **all believers**, and the only High Priest is Christ, whose priesthood is "unchangeable," that is, it does not pass to anyone else:

> **1 Peter 2:5:** "Ye also, as lively stones, are built up a spiritual house, an holy priesthood, to offer up spiritual sacrifices, acceptable to God by Jesus Christ."

> **1 Peter 2:9:** "But ye are a chosen generation, a royal priesthood, an holy nation, a peculiar people; that ye should shew forth the praises of him who hath called you out of darkness into his marvellous light:"

> **Revelation 1:5-6:** "And from Jesus Christ, who is the faithful witness, and the first begotten of the dead, and the prince of the kings of the earth. Unto him that loved us, and washed us from our sins in his own blood, And hath made us kings and priests unto God and his Father; to him be glory and dominion for ever and ever. Amen."

> **Hebrews 7:23-25:** "And they truly were many priests, because they were not suffered to continue by reason of death: But this man, because he continueth ever, hath an unchangeable priesthood. Wherefore he is able also to save them to the uttermost that come unto God by him, seeing he ever liveth to make intercession for them."

## The sacrifices are spiritual

The sacrifices of this priesthood are **spiritual**, not literal:

- **Our bodies**, offered as living sacrifices (Romans 12:1).
- **Praise**, "the fruit of our lips giving thanks to his name" (Hebrews 13:15; Hosea 14:2).
- **Doing good and sharing** with others (Hebrews 13:16).
- **Gifts** to those who serve the Lord, "an odour of a sweet smell, a sacrifice acceptable" (Philippians 4:18).
- **Prayer**, rising like incense (Psalm 141:2; Revelation 5:8).
- **Singing** with grace in the heart (Ephesians 5:19; Colossians 3:16).
- **A broken and contrite heart** (Psalm 51:17).
- **Service** of faith, and souls won to God (Philippians 2:17; Romans 15:16).

> **Romans 12:1:** "I beseech you therefore, brethren, by the mercies of God, that ye present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable unto God, which is your reasonable service."

> **Hebrews 13:15-16:** "By him therefore let us offer the sacrifice of praise to God continually, that is, the fruit of our lips giving thanks to his name. But to do good and to communicate forget not: for with such sacrifices God is well pleased."

> **Philippians 4:18:** "But I have all, and abound: I am full, having received of Epaphroditus the things which were sent from you, an odour of a sweet smell, a sacrifice acceptable, wellpleasing to God."

> **Psalm 141:2:** "Let my prayer be set forth before thee as incense; and the lifting up of my hands as the evening sacrifice."

> **Psalm 51:17:** "The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit: a broken and a contrite heart, O God, thou wilt not despise."

## "We have an altar"

Catholics cite Hebrews 13:10, "We have an altar." But read the context: the writer is warning against being carried away by teachings about **food**, "not with meats, which have not profited them" (13:9). Our altar is Christ and His cross, outside the camp (13:12-13), and the sacrifice we offer on it is **praise** (13:15).

> **Hebrews 13:9-15:** "Be not carried about with divers and strange doctrines. For it is a good thing that the heart be established with grace; not with meats, which have not profited them that have been occupied therein. We have an altar, whereof they have no right to eat which serve the tabernacle. For the bodies of those beasts, whose blood is brought into the sanctuary by the high priest for sin, are burned without the camp. Wherefore Jesus also, that he might sanctify the people with his own blood, suffered without the gate. Let us go forth therefore unto him without the camp, bearing his reproach. For here have we no continuing city, but we seek one to come. By him therefore let us offer the sacrifice of praise to God continually, that is, the fruit of our lips giving thanks to his name."

## Malachi's "pure offering"

Catholics also cite Malachi 1:11, where God says that among the Gentiles "incense shall be offered unto my name, and a pure offering." The early Christians did apply that verse to their worship, but they explained the "offering" as **prayer and thanksgiving**. Scripture itself calls incense the prayers of the saints (Revelation 5:8; 8:3-4). Justin Martyr wrote: "prayers and giving of thanks, when offered by worthy men, are the only perfect and well-pleasing sacrifices to God" (_Dialogue with Trypho_ 117).

# 10. What the early Fathers really said

Catholic apologists claim the Fathers believed in transubstantiation from the beginning. In fact, the Fathers used strong, realistic language about the bread and wine **and** symbolic language about them, often in the same writer. What none of them taught is the medieval doctrine: that the substance of bread ceases to exist and only its appearances remain.

## Ignatius of Antioch (c. 110): fighting the Gnostics

The quotation always used is from his letter to the Smyrnaeans: "They abstain from the Eucharist and from prayer, because they confess not the Eucharist to be the flesh of our Saviour Jesus Christ, which suffered for our sins" (_Smyrnaeans_ 7.1). But read the letter. Ignatius was fighting the **Docetists**, Gnostics who said Christ "only seemed to suffer" and never had real flesh at all (_Smyrnaeans_ 2; 5). His point is that Jesus truly came in the flesh and truly suffered; those who deny that Christ had flesh have no business at a meal that remembers His flesh. He is not discussing a change of substance in the bread.

The same Ignatius uses the same language **figuratively**:

- "renew yourselves in **faith, which is the flesh of the Lord**, and in **love, which is the blood of Jesus Christ**" (_Trallians_ 8.1).
- "I desire the bread of God… which is the flesh of Jesus Christ… and for drink I desire His blood, **which is love incorruptible**" (_Romans_ 7.3).
- He takes refuge "in the **Gospel as in the flesh of Jesus**" (_Philadelphians_ 5.1).

Nobody thinks faith is literally flesh or love is literally blood. Catholics today read their medieval theology back into his words.

## Justin Martyr (c. 155)

Justin says the Eucharist is "not common bread and common drink," but the food "from which our blood and flesh **by transmutation are nourished**, is the flesh and blood of that Jesus who was made flesh" (_First Apology_ 66). The food is **digested** and nourishes our bodies, which is something real bread does, and which transubstantiation denies happens to the substance of bread. Elsewhere Justin calls the bread a **memorial**: the bread "which our Christ gave us to eat, **in remembrance** of His being made flesh" (_Dialogue_ 70).

## Irenaeus (c. 180)

Irenaeus, fighting Gnostics who despised the body, wrote: "the bread, which is produced from the earth, when it receives the invocation of God, is no longer common bread, but the Eucharist, **consisting of two realities, earthly and heavenly**" (_Against Heresies_ 4.18.5). The earthly reality, the bread, **remains**.

## Tertullian (c. 207)

"Having taken the bread and given it to His disciples, He made it His own body, by saying, 'This is my body,' **that is, the figure of my body**" (_Against Marcion_ 4.40).

## Clement of Alexandria (c. 200)

"Thus in many ways the Word is **figuratively** described, as meat, and flesh, and food, and bread, and blood, and milk" (_The Instructor_ 1.6).

## Origen (c. 245)

The consecrated food, "in accordance with the fact that it is material, goes into the belly and is cast out into the draught… it is not the material of the bread but **the word which is said over it** which is of advantage" (_Commentary on Matthew_ 11.14). Of John 6:53 he said: if you follow the words "Except ye eat the flesh of the Son of man" according to the letter, "**that letter kills**" (_Homilies on Leviticus_ 7.5).

## Eusebius (c. 315)

Christians celebrate "a memorial of this offering… on a table by means of **symbols** of His body and saving blood" (_Proof of the Gospel_ 1.10).

## Athanasius (c. 360)

Commenting on John 6, Athanasius said Jesus spoke of His ascension so that they would not think of His body in a fleshly way: "for how many would His body have sufficed for food, that it should become the food of the whole world?" His words were "spiritual" (_Letters to Serapion_ 4.19).

## Augustine (c. 400-420)

- "Why makest thou ready teeth and belly? **Believe, and thou hast eaten**" (_Tractates on John_ 25.12). "For to believe on Him is to eat the living bread" (26.1).
- John 6:53 "seems to enjoin a crime or a vice; it is therefore **a figure**, enjoining that we should have a share in the sufferings of our Lord, and that we should retain a sweet and profitable memory of the fact that His flesh was wounded and crucified for us" (_On Christian Doctrine_ 3.16.24).
- "The Lord did not hesitate to say 'This is my body' when He gave **the sign of His body**" (_Against Adimantus_ 12.3).

## Theodoret (c. 450) and Pope Gelasius (c. 495)

Against the Eutychians, who said Christ's human nature was swallowed up by His divinity, both used the Eucharist as an illustration that a nature **remains** when joined to something higher:

- Theodoret: "even after the consecration the mystic symbols are not deprived of their own nature; they **remain in their former substance, figure and form**" (_Eranistes_, Dialogue 2).
- Pope **Gelasius I**: the sacraments become a divine thing, "and yet **the substance or nature of the bread and wine does not cease**" (_On the Two Natures in Christ_ 14). A bishop of Rome, writing 700 years before Lateran IV, denied in so many words what Lateran IV would define.

## The Fathers Catholics quote

Some fourth-century Fathers did speak of a change. **Cyril of Jerusalem** told new converts that what seems bread "is not bread, though it be sensible to taste, but the body of Christ" (_Mystagogical Catecheses_ 4.9), and **Ambrose** said that the blessing changes "nature itself" (_On the Mysteries_ 9.52). But the same Cyril calls the bread and wine "the **antitype** of the body and blood of Christ" and says His body is given "in the **figure** of bread" (4.3; 5.20). None of them speaks of the substance of bread ceasing while its "accidents" remain. That language comes from Aristotle's philosophy, rediscovered in the West in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries.

## Christ present, but not in the flesh

The Fathers did believe Christ is present when His people gather at His table. So does Scripture, in the same way He is present wherever two or three gather in His name, and in the hearts of believers by faith. He sups with those who open the door to Him. That is a real presence, by His Spirit, not His flesh enclosed in bread.

> **Matthew 18:20:** "For where two or three are gathered together in my name, there am I in the midst of them."

> **Matthew 28:20:** "Teaching them to observe all things whatsoever I have commanded you: and, lo, I am with you alway, even unto the end of the world. Amen."

> **Ephesians 3:17:** "That Christ may dwell in your hearts by faith; that ye, being rooted and grounded in love,"

> **Revelation 3:20:** "Behold, I stand at the door, and knock: if any man hear my voice, and open the door, I will come in to him, and will sup with him, and he with me."

# 11. From elders to priests, from a table to an altar

The first Christians were known **because** they had no sacrifices, altars or priests, unlike the Jews and the pagans. Around the year 200 the pagan Caecilius complained: "Why have they no altars, no temples, no acknowledged images?" The Christian Octavius answered: "Do you think that we conceal what we worship, if we have no temples and altars?… The victim fit for sacrifice is a good disposition, and a pure mind, and a sincere judgment" (Minucius Felix, _Octavius_ 10; 32).

Then the language began to change:

- **Tertullian** (c. 200) is the first known writer to call the bishop a **priest**: "the chief priest (_summus sacerdos_), who is the bishop" (_On Baptism_ 17). Yet the same Tertullian still knew the New Testament truth: "Are not even we laics priests?" (_On Exhortation to Chastity_ 7).
- **Cyprian** (c. 250) went much further. For him the bishop is a _sacerdos_ who stands at the altar in Christ's place, and "the passion of the Lord is the sacrifice which we offer" (_Letter_ 63.14).
- As the ministers became priests, the table became an **altar** and the memorial became a **sacrifice**.

The bishop of Rome would later take the title of Rome's pagan high priest. The **Pontifex Maximus** was the head of the college of priests that supervised the sacrifices of the Roman state; the emperors held the title until **Gratian** gave it up, around 376-383. From the fifteenth century on it became a regular title of the popes, and it is carved on Rome's buildings to this day. A priestly caste, offering a sacrifice at an altar, under a _pontifex maximus_, looks far more like the religion of pagan Rome than like the church of Acts.

# 12. A ninth-century debate

If the change of substance had been the teaching of the apostles, there would have been nothing to argue about. Yet the first great controversy over the Eucharist broke out in the **ninth century**:

- Around **831**, **Paschasius Radbertus**, a monk of Corbie, wrote _On the Body and Blood of the Lord_, the first treatise to argue that the Eucharist is the very flesh "born of Mary, which suffered on the cross and rose from the tomb."
- Around **843**, at the request of the emperor Charles the Bald, **Ratramnus**, a monk of the **same monastery**, answered with a book of the same title: the bread and wine are the body and blood **in figure** (_in figura_), not in literal truth, and are received spiritually by faith. **Rabanus Maurus**, the archbishop of Mainz, also opposed Paschasius.
- In the eleventh century, **Berengar of Tours** taught the spiritual view and was forced, at a Roman synod in **1059**, to swear that Christ's body is "sensibly, not only in sacrament but in truth, touched and broken by the hands of priests and crushed by the teeth of the faithful."
- The word **transubstantiation** first appears in the **twelfth** century, and it was first defined as dogma in **1215**.

Why would monks of the same monastery, and the emperor himself, still be asking in 843 what the Eucharist is, if the apostles had settled it 800 years earlier?

# 13. The Fourth Lateran Council, 1215

Transubstantiation was dogmatized for the first time by the **Fourth Lateran Council**, called by **Innocent III**, the most powerful pope in history, who claimed authority over kings and launched crusades against both Muslims and Christians. The council met from **11 to 30 November 1215**, under three weeks. Its **71 canons were proposed by the pope himself** and read and approved in the final session on 30 November. Among them:

- **Canon 1**: the body and blood of Christ "are truly contained in the sacrament of the altar under the forms of bread and wine, the bread and wine having been **changed in substance** (_transsubstantiatis_), by God's power, into his body and blood."
- **Canon 3**: heretics condemned by the church are to be **handed over to the secular authorities** for punishment. Rulers are to swear to **expel (_exterminare_) all heretics** from their lands; a ruler who refuses is excommunicated, and if he still neglects it, his vassals are released from their oath and **his land is given to Catholics** who will drive the heretics out. Those who take up arms against heretics receive the same **indulgence** as crusaders to the Holy Land.
- **Canon 21**: every Christian must confess to a priest and receive communion at least once a year, at Easter.
- **Canon 67**: Jews who charge "heavy and immoderate" interest are to be cut off from contact with Christians.
- **Canon 68**: Jews and Muslims must wear **distinctive dress**, so that Christians can tell them apart, and Jews may not appear in public during the last three days before Easter.
- **Canon 69**: Jews may not hold public office, "since it is absurd that a blasphemer of Christ exercise authority over Christians."
- **Canon 70**: converted Jews may not keep any of their old customs.
- **Canon 71**: for the coming crusade, the secular powers must **compel Jews to cancel the interest** owed to them by crusaders.

The same council that defined the host as Christ's body ordered Jews to be marked out by their clothing, and rulers to clear their lands of heretics.

# 14. The host desecration libels

As the host came to be seen as Christ's literal body, a new accusation appeared: that **Jews stole consecrated hosts to torture Jesus again**, stabbing them until they bled. The accusation was always false. Jews did not believe the host was anything but bread, and had no reason to "torture" it. But it was used again and again to justify robbery and murder:

- **Belitz**, near Berlin, **1243**: Jews accused of desecrating a host were burned at the stake.
- **Paris, 1290**: a Jew named Jonathas was accused of stabbing a host that bled; he was burned, and his house became a chapel of the "miracle."
- **Röttingen, 1298**: an accusation of host desecration started the **Rintfleisch massacres**, led by a knight named Rintfleisch. Bands of men went through Franconia and Bavaria and destroyed **146 Jewish communities**; estimates of the dead range from several thousand to tens of thousands.
- **Deggendorf, 1338**: the townspeople burned the Jewish quarter and killed its residents, and the duke gave the killers the victims' property. The accusation of host desecration was added afterwards to justify it. The supposed hosts became the object of a famous pilgrimage, the _Deggendorfer Gnad_, which was **abolished only in 1992**.
- **Brussels, 1370**: Jews accused of stabbing hosts were executed; the "Sacrament of Miracle" was venerated in the cathedral for centuries.
- **Segovia, 1410**; **Sternberg, 1492**: more accusations and executions.
- **Berlin, 1510**: after a thief confessed to stealing a host, **38 Jews were burned** in one execution and the rest expelled from Brandenburg.

In many of these cases the "bleeding" host was simply bread with a red bacterium growing on it (see section 20).

# 15. Killing in the name of the Church

The Lateran canons were not words on paper. Scripture was bent to justify force:

- **"Compel them to come in"** (Luke 14:23), from the parable of the great supper, was used by **Augustine** to justify imperial force against the Donatists (_Letter_ 93; _Letter_ 185), and became the standard proof text for coercion in religion.
- **Thomas Aquinas** taught that heretics "deserve not only to be separated from the Church by excommunication, but also to be severed from the world by death," because corrupting the faith is worse than forging money. He quotes Jerome: "Cut off the decayed flesh, expel the mangy sheep from the fold" (_Summa Theologiae_ II-II, q. 11, a. 3). He justified it with the image of cutting off a diseased limb to save the body (II-II, q. 64, a. 2), the image Jesus used to tell each person to cut off **his own** sin (Matthew 5:29-30), not to kill his neighbor.
- **Innocent III** launched the **Albigensian Crusade** (1209) against the Cathars of southern France. At Béziers the whole town was massacred; Caesarius of Heisterbach reports that the papal legate, asked how to tell Catholics from heretics, answered: "Kill them all; God will know his own."
- **Innocent IV** authorized the use of **torture** by the Inquisition in the bull _Ad extirpanda_ (1252).
- In 1520 **Leo X**, in the bull _Exsurge Domine_, condemned as an error Martin Luther's statement that "it is contrary to the will of the Spirit that heretics be burned" (proposition 33).

Jesus taught the opposite. When His disciples wanted to call down fire on a village that rejected Him, He rebuked them:

> **Luke 9:54-56:** "And when his disciples James and John saw this, they said, Lord, wilt thou that we command fire to come down from heaven, and consume them, even as Elias did? But he turned, and rebuked them, and said, Ye know not what manner of spirit ye are of. For the Son of man is not come to destroy men’s lives, but to save them. And they went to another village."

> **John 18:36:** "Jesus answered, My kingdom is not of this world: if my kingdom were of this world, then would my servants fight, that I should not be delivered to the Jews: but now is my kingdom not from hence."

> **2 Corinthians 10:3-4:** "For though we walk in the flesh, we do not war after the flesh: (For the weapons of our warfare are not carnal, but mighty through God to the pulling down of strong holds;)"

> **Matthew 26:52:** "Then said Jesus unto him, Put up again thy sword into his place: for all they that take the sword shall perish with the sword."

# 16. The cup taken from the people

Jesus said "**Drink ye all of it**" (Matthew 26:27). For a thousand years the whole church received both the bread and the cup. Pope **Gelasius I** (c. 495) even ordered that those who received the bread but refused the cup should either receive the sacrament whole or be kept from it entirely, because "the division of one and the same mystery cannot take place without great sacrilege."

But as the wine came to be seen as Christ's literal blood, the fear of **spilling** it grew. During the twelfth and thirteenth centuries the cup was gradually withdrawn from the laity, and by the fourteenth century communion in the bread alone was normal. To justify it, theologians developed the doctrine of **concomitance**: the whole Christ, body, blood, soul and divinity, is contained under each species, so the bread alone is enough (Aquinas, _Summa_ III, q. 76, a. 2; q. 80, a. 12). The **Council of Constance** (1415, session 13) forbade giving the cup to the laity, and **Trent** (1562, Session 21) pronounced anathema on anyone who says that all the faithful must receive both kinds. A new doctrine was invented to justify setting aside the plain command of Christ.

> **Matthew 26:27:** "And he took the cup, and gave thanks, and gave it to them, saying, Drink ye all of it;"

> **1 Corinthians 11:28:** "But let a man examine himself, and so let him eat of that bread, and drink of that cup."

# 17. Worshipping the host

## A late development in the West

- **c. 1200**: priests in Paris began to **elevate** the host after the words of consecration so that the people could adore it, a practice confirmed by Bishop **Odo of Sully** (d. 1208).
- **1219**: Pope **Honorius III** ordered priests to teach the people to bow reverently at the elevation and when the host was carried to the sick.
- **1264**: Pope **Urban IV** established the feast of **Corpus Christi**, with processions of the host through the streets.
- Later came exposition of the host in a **monstrance** and perpetual **adoration**.
- **1551**: Trent declared that the host must be adored "with the worship of _latria_," the worship due to God, and anathematized anyone who denies it (Session 13, canon 6).

## The East

The Eastern Orthodox churches believe in a change (_metabolē_), but they did not adopt the Latin word "transubstantiation" (_metousiōsis_) officially until the **Synod of Jerusalem in 1672**, under Patriarch Dositheus, which also declared that the body and blood in the Eucharist should be "adored with _latria_." The Coptic and other Oriental Orthodox churches also believe in a change, without the Latin philosophy.

That the Orthodox and Copts also believe in a change does not make it apostolic. They are branches of the same **imperial church** that Theodosius made the official religion of the empire in **380** (the Edict of Thessalonica), which later split along political and doctrinal lines at **Chalcedon** (451) and in **1054**. They share the developments of the centuries before they divided. Agreement among the branches of one institution shows only what that institution had come to believe, not what the apostles taught.

## The worst kind of idolatry

To adore with the worship due to God an object made by human hands is what Scripture calls **idolatry**. Paul, in the same chapter where he speaks of the communion of the cup and the bread, warns: "wherefore, my dearly beloved, **flee from idolatry**."

> **1 Corinthians 10:14-16:** "Wherefore, my dearly beloved, flee from idolatry. I speak as to wise men; judge ye what I say. The cup of blessing which we bless, is it not the communion of the blood of Christ? The bread which we break, is it not the communion of the body of Christ?"

> **Isaiah 42:8:** "I am the LORD: that is my name: and my glory will I not give to another, neither my praise to graven images."

> **Acts 17:24-25:** "God that made the world and all things therein, seeing that he is Lord of heaven and earth, dwelleth not in temples made with hands; Neither is worshipped with men’s hands, as though he needed any thing, seeing he giveth to all life, and breath, and all things;"

> **Acts 17:29:** "Forasmuch then as we are the offspring of God, we ought not to think that the Godhead is like unto gold, or silver, or stone, graven by art and man’s device."

# 18. The right God, the wrong way: the golden calf

The Israelites at Sinai did not think they were abandoning the LORD. They made a calf and said it was the God who brought them out of Egypt, and Aaron proclaimed "a feast **to the LORD**," to YHWH.

## "These be thy gods" or "This is thy God"?

The Hebrew word **אֱלֹהִים** (_ʾelohim_) is plural in form, but when it refers to the one true God it means "God," singular, as in "In the beginning God (_ʾelohim_) created" (Genesis 1:1), where the verb is singular. So in Exodus 32:4 it can be translated either way. The KJV and RVG have "These be thy gods," because the Hebrew verb in that sentence is plural. But there was only **one** calf, and the **NKJV** translates:

> **Exodus 32:4 (NKJV):** "And he received the gold from their hand, and he fashioned it with an engraving tool, and made a molded calf. Then they said, 'This is your god, O Israel, that brought you out of the land of Egypt!'"

Nehemiah, retelling the same event, puts it in the singular, with a singular verb: "This is thy **God** that brought thee up out of Egypt." The Psalms, Deuteronomy and Stephen all speak of **a calf**, one image:

> **Exodus 32:4-5:** "And he received them at their hand, and fashioned it with a graving tool, after he had made it a molten calf: and they said, These be thy gods, O Israel, which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt. And when Aaron saw it, he built an altar before it; and Aaron made proclamation, and said, To morrow is a feast to the LORD."

> **Nehemiah 9:18:** "Yea, when they had made them a molten calf, and said, This is thy God that brought thee up out of Egypt, and had wrought great provocations;"

> **Psalm 106:19-20:** "They made a calf in Horeb, and worshipped the molten image. Thus they changed their glory into the similitude of an ox that eateth grass."

> **Deuteronomy 9:16:** "And I looked, and, behold, ye had sinned against the LORD your God, and had made you a molten calf: ye had turned aside quickly out of the way which the LORD had commanded you."

> **Acts 7:41:** "And they made a calf in those days, and offered sacrifice unto the idol, and rejoiced in the works of their own hands."

Compare Jeroboam, who made **two** calves and said "behold thy gods" (1 Kings 12:28). At Sinai there was one calf, presented as the God of the Exodus.

## Worshipping the right God the wrong way was death

The people worshipped the true God through an image He had not commanded, and it was idolatry. About three thousand men died that day, and the LORD plagued the people (Exodus 32:28, 35). God's people also died for worshipping Him in ways He had not commanded: Nadab and Abihu offered "strange fire" and were consumed (Leviticus 10:1-2).

> **Exodus 32:28:** "And the children of Levi did according to the word of Moses: and there fell of the people that day about three thousand men."

> **Exodus 32:35:** "And the LORD plagued the people, because they made the calf, which Aaron made."

> **Leviticus 10:1-2:** "And Nadab and Abihu, the sons of Aaron, took either of them his censer, and put fire therein, and put incense thereon, and offered strange fire before the LORD, which he commanded them not. And there went out fire from the LORD, and devoured them, and they died before the LORD."

These things were written as **examples for us**. Paul applies the golden calf directly to the church, in the passage about the Lord's table: "Neither be ye idolaters, as were some of them; as it is written, The people sat down to eat and drink, and rose up to play" (quoting Exodus 32:6). Physical death in the Old Testament pictures spiritual death in the New. Catholics worship the right God, but by a method He never commanded: bowing before bread as God.

> **1 Corinthians 10:6-7:** "Now these things were our examples, to the intent we should not lust after evil things, as they also lusted. Neither be ye idolaters, as were some of them; as it is written, The people sat down to eat and drink, and rose up to play."

> **1 Corinthians 10:11-12:** "Now all these things happened unto them for ensamples: and they are written for our admonition, upon whom the ends of the world are come. Wherefore let him that thinketh he standeth take heed lest he fall."

> **John 4:23-24:** "But the hour cometh, and now is, when the true worshippers shall worship the Father in spirit and in truth: for the Father seeketh such to worship him. God is a Spirit: and they that worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth."

# 19. "Lo, here is Christ"

Jesus warned that false teachers would say He is "here" or "there," even "in the secret chambers" (ἐν τοῖς ταμείοις, the inner rooms), and told His disciples: **believe it not**. His return will be visible to all, "as the lightning cometh out of the east."

> **Matthew 24:23-27:** "Then if any man shall say unto you, Lo, here is Christ, or there; believe it not. For there shall arise false Christs, and false prophets, and shall shew great signs and wonders; insomuch that, if it were possible, they shall deceive the very elect. Behold, I have told you before. Wherefore if they shall say unto you, Behold, he is in the desert; go not forth: behold, he is in the secret chambers; believe it not. For as the lightning cometh out of the east, and shineth even unto the west; so shall also the coming of the Son of man be."

# 20. "Eucharistic miracles"

Catholic writers point to "Eucharistic miracles," hosts that appear to bleed or turn into flesh, as proof of transubstantiation: Bolsena (1263), Buenos Aires (1996), Tixtla (2006), Sokółka (2008), Legnica (2013) and others.

## A red bacterium

Bread and other starchy foods kept damp are an ideal home for the bacterium **_Serratia marcescens_**, which produces a bright red pigment that looks like drops of fresh blood. In 1819 red spots appeared on polenta near Padua and were investigated by Bartolomeo Bizio, who named the organism; in 1848 the German naturalist **Christian Ehrenberg** linked it to more than 100 recorded cases of "miraculous blood." It has been called "the miracle bacterium" ever since, and some historians think it lies behind many of the "bleeding hosts" for which Jews were burned.

Many modern cases follow the same pattern. A consecrated host **falls on the floor** or is found discarded; as is customary, it is placed **in water** to dissolve, and locked in a tabernacle; days later red spots appear. That is exactly the damp, dark, starchy environment in which these organisms grow.

## The new scapegoat

In the Middle Ages, the "bleeding host" story needed a villain, and the Jews were blamed. Today the villain is the **Satanist**: Catholic writers warn that Satanists steal consecrated hosts for black masses **because they know** it is really Jesus. In 2014 the Archdiocese of Oklahoma City went to court to recover a host that a satanic group planned to use. The pattern is the same: an outside enemy who "proves" the doctrine by trying to harm the host.

## A miracle that contradicts the doctrine

Transubstantiation teaches that the **substance** changes but the **appearances** of bread remain: it looks, tastes and acts like bread, because the body of Christ is present invisibly. A host that looks like flesh and blood is the opposite: the appearances have changed. **Thomas Aquinas** had to face this: he taught that when flesh or blood appears in the host, it is either an impression on the eyes of the viewers or a change in the appearances, and that **Christ's body is not seen there in its own form** (_Summa Theologiae_ III, q. 76, a. 8). By Rome's own theology, then, the flesh in a "Eucharistic miracle" is **not** Christ's body. And the risen Christ's body is glorious and immortal (Philippians 3:21; Romans 6:9), not a fragment of wounded heart muscle in a jar.

# Conclusion

The Lord's Supper is precious. Jesus gave it on the night He was betrayed, so that His people would **remember** His death, **proclaim** it until He comes, and show that they are **one body**. Scripture and the earliest Christians understood the bread and the cup as signs of His body and blood, received by faith, with Christ present by His Spirit among His people.

Transubstantiation turned the memorial into a repeated sacrifice, the elders into sacrificing priests, the table into an altar, and the bread into an object of worship. It contradicts the finished work of Christ in Hebrews, God's law against blood, Jesus' own words "fruit of the vine" and "the flesh profiteth nothing," and the meaning Jesus Himself gave to eating and drinking. It was unknown as a doctrine for a thousand years, debated in the ninth and eleventh centuries, defined in 1215 by a council that marked Jews and hunted heretics, and enforced by fire. The sacrifice that saves was offered once, and it needs nothing added to it.

> **John 19:30:** "When Jesus therefore had received the vinegar, he said, It is finished: and he bowed his head, and gave up the ghost."

> **Hebrews 10:14:** "For by one offering he hath perfected for ever them that are sanctified."

> **1 Corinthians 11:26:** "For as often as ye eat this bread, and drink this cup, ye do shew the Lord’s death till he come."
